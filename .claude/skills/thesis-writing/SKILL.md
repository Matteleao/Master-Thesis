# Thesis writing skills

Three skills combined: Scientific prose, Argument structure, Evidence and delivery.

# Part: Scientific prose

## Scientific prose

### The governing instruction

Write as a proficient non-native English speaker at B2-C1 level. Keep full technical precision with ordinary syntax. Plain, slightly flat prose is correct. Elegant, rhetorical, quotable or native-marked prose is wrong, because elegance is the marker that gives the author away.

Get the clause number, the figure and the term exactly right, then say it in the plainest sentence that holds the meaning. A writer at this level explains, qualifies and reports. He does not build cadence, set up a reveal, or construct emphasis through sentence shape.

The failure mode to watch: told to keep syntax simple, a writer starts producing short emphatic declarations, antithesis and balanced lists to keep the prose interesting. That produces an essay, not a scientific chapter.

**Two tests before delivering.** Could a strong non-native writer with this technical knowledge have produced this paragraph without help? Does any sentence exist because it sounded good? A yes to the second is a failure regardless of the first.

### Sentence length

*(This is what detection literature calls burstiness: variation in sentence length and construction across a text. Low burstiness, a text where every sentence lands near the same length, is one of the two strongest statistical tells of generated prose. The other is word-level predictability, covered below under Predictable vocabulary.)*

There is no word count, no mean to hit and no ceiling. Write the sentence the idea needs, then stop. Do not pad, and do not split a sentence that is working because it has grown long.

**Variation is the goal, not a target mean.** The author values variation across a text and across chapters. An Introduction presents known concepts and admits a more direct tone. A Discussion advances the author's own ideas, which need more explanation and grounding, so longer sentences there serve a purpose and are authorized.

A long sentence is legitimate when it carries one chain that splitting would break: a regulatory status with its qualification, a definition with its conditions, a finding with the evidence it rests on. It becomes a defect when the reader has to go back to the start to parse it, when a connector has stapled two ideas together, or when several arrive in a row.

**Measurement is a diagnostic, never a target.** The author's own chapters are the reference sample.

| | Ch.1 | Ch.3 | Text written to a numeric rule |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Mean sentence length | 18.1 | 18.0 | 17.7 |
| Standard deviation | 8.9 | 8.1 | 6.5 |
| Shortest sentence | 4 | 3 | 4 |
| Longest sentence | 52 | 41 | 34 |
| Paragraph length, SD | 2.4 | 2.1 | 1.4 |

The means match. The spread does not. Low standard deviation and a longest sentence stopping at a round number are the signature of text built to a rule. If the sentences all come out about the same size, or every paragraph holds the same number of them, the text was written to a shape and needs revisiting.

### Cohesion and the chain of consequence

Read this section before the prohibitions that follow it. A text written only to avoid banned constructions comes out clipped, because the safest sentence is a short one with no connective, and a page of those reads like stop-and-go traffic. Flow is not decoration here. It is what lets a reader follow an argument that runs across several steps.

**Consecutive sentences that form a chain must be bound.** Where one statement causes, enables, or follows from the one before it, say so with a connective. Do not leave the reader to infer the link from adjacency alone.

Three tools do this work:

- **Subordination**, tying a cause to its consequence inside one sentence: *because*, *since*, *once*, *where*, *so that*, *given that*
- **Coordination**, keeping a sequence feeling like one sequence: *and*, *but*, *so*
- **A consequence connector**, marking the end of a chain: *therefore*, *for this reason*, *as a result*, *and because of that*

**The diagnostic.** Three or more consecutive sentences, each opening a fresh subject with no connective, means the paragraph was chopped. Rejoin them. A chain of consequence should close on a sentence marked as the consequence.

**Worked example.** Chopped:

> The consequence is that conduct at the site is not a peripheral activity. The data produced in the operating room and in follow-up become clinical evidence. The clinical evidence becomes the clinical evaluation report. The report becomes part of the file the notified body decides on. Every link in that chain is only as reliable as the one before it.

Five sentences, each between ten and fourteen words, none connected to the next. The chain is real and the prose hides it.

Bound:

> Conduct at the site is therefore not a peripheral activity, because the data produced in the operating room and in follow-up become clinical evidence. The clinical evidence becomes the clinical evaluation report, and the report becomes part of the file the notified body decides on. Every link in that chain is therefore only as reliable as the one before it.

Three sentences of twenty-four, twenty-two and fourteen words. *Because* binds the claim to its ground, *and* holds the two steps of the chain in one movement, and the closing *therefore* marks the conclusion as a conclusion. The spread of lengths improves at the same time, which is what the diagnostic above is for.

### Vocabulary: always take the plainer road

When two formulations carry the same meaning, choose the plainer, more everyday one.

**Empty verb plus nominalization.** Replace *involves*, *entails*, *comprises*, *constitutes*, *is characterized by*, *consists in* followed by a gerund or abstract noun with the plain verb.

| Formal | Plain |
| :-- | :-- |
| involves the identification of | identifies |
| entails an assessment of | assesses |
| performs a review of | reviews |
| provides a description of | describes |
| carries out the verification of | verifies, checks |
| the implementation of the plan | putting the plan into action, running the plan |
| the determination of eligibility | deciding eligibility |

**Single-word replacements.** Use the plain column unless the formal word is a defined regulatory or technical term.

| Formal | Plain |
| :-- | :-- |
| utilize, employ, leverage | use |
| ascertain, determine | find out, work out, decide, settle |
| facilitate | make easier, help, allow |
| commence, initiate | start, open |
| terminate, cease | stop, end, close |
| obtain, procure | get |
| sufficient | enough |
| numerous, a number of | many, several, or the figure |
| in order to | to |
| with regard to, in terms of | about, for, in |
| prior to, subsequent to | before, after |
| in the event that | if |
| additionally, furthermore | also, and |
| there exists | there is |
| render, engender | make, produce |
| encompass | cover, include |
| operationalize | put into practice, turn into something the site can run |
| undertake | do, run, carry out |
| elucidate, delineate | explain, set out |

*Demonstrate*, *establish* and *implement* stay where the evidential or regulatory meaning is exact. Replace them everywhere else.

**Lowering the register across a whole chapter.** The lists above catch single words. The standing problem is broader: the prose sits a notch more formal than it needs to. These are real replacements the author accepted.

| Written | Replaced with |
| :-- | :-- |
| The guideline formalized Quality by Design in clinical research | The guideline brought Quality by Design into the guidance for clinical research |
| gave the idea an operational form through CtQ factors | turned the idea into something usable through CtQ factors |
| strengthened that direction | pushed further in the same direction |
| Regional regulation supplies the legal force behind these standards | Regional regulation is what gives these standards their legal force |
| Accountability of this kind has to be operational to be real | Accountability of this kind only means something if it is operational |
| the methodological infrastructure built around that model | the research machinery built around that model |
| both conditions concentrate on a moment that cannot be repeated | both conditions come down to a moment that cannot be repeated |
| the analysis characterized the organizations | the analysis gives a picture of the organizations |
| has noted the absence of standardized instruments | has pointed out that there are no standardized instruments |
| translates the principles into practices a site can operate | turns the principles into practices a site can actually run |
| is designed to complement the obligations established by | is meant to sit alongside the obligations set by |
| an artifact derived from documented evidence | an artifact built from documented evidence |

None of these loses precision. Clause numbers, instrument names and evidential verbs are untouched, and the register drops only in the connective and descriptive vocabulary around them.

**The limits of casual.** Casual means plain and unforced. Slang, idiom, figurative expressions, vague placeholders (*things*, *stuff*, *aspects*), hedging fillers (*kind of*, *a bit*, *pretty much*), the second person, rhetorical questions and humor all stay out. Phrasal verbs are welcome (*work out*, *set out*, *come down to*, *rest on*, *point to*, *sit alongside*) except where they carry a colloquial edge (*mess up*, *sort out*, *end up*, *come up with*) or have two possible readings.

### Predictable vocabulary

*(This is what detection literature calls perplexity: how statistically likely each word choice is, given the words before it. A language model defaults to the most probable next word; a small set of words are so consistently that default that their presence is a signal on its own, independent of whether the surrounding sentence is otherwise plain or formal.)*

| Flagged | Replace with the exact thing meant |
| :-- | :-- |
| delve into | examine, look at, work through |
| leverage (verb) | use, draw on |
| robust | reliable, well supported, or name what makes it so |
| comprehensive | complete, or name the scope covered |
| streamline | simplify, shorten, remove a step from |
| foster | support, encourage, allow |
| pivotal | name what it changed, and for what |
| multifaceted | name the facets |
| underscore, highlight | show, point to, confirm |
| showcase | present, report, describe |
| testament to, a tapestry of, the landscape of | delete; state the fact plainly |
| garner | receive, get |
| intricate, nuanced | name the specific complication |

A flagged word replaced by a vaguer plain word is not a fix. Where the table says to name the thing, name it; do not swap one generic word for another.

**Discourse markers.** `furthermore` and `moreover` already collapse to `also` under the plain-vocabulary rule above. Beyond that substitution, do not let the same connector open two paragraphs in one section, whichever connector it is. A section where every paragraph opens on `however` is as predictable as one that opens on `furthermore`.

This section and predictable vocabulary do the same work at two different levels: word choice here, sentence and paragraph construction in Sentence length above and in Cohesion. Neither is a target to hit by counting; both are diagnostics for prose that has settled into a template instead of following what the content actually needs.

### Fixed conventions

| Item | Decision |
| :-- | :-- |
| Person, Introduction | Third person impersonal. No *we* |
| Person, Methodology onward | First person plural. *We compared*, *We adopted*, *Our analysis shows* |
| Spelling | American English: standardization, organization, analyzed, behavior, artifact, center, defense, license |
| Serial comma | Yes, where a series survives the grouping rules at all |
| Working language | Reply to the author in Portuguese. Deliver academic text in English |

Preserve official names, regulatory terms, defined technical terms, abbreviations and cross-references. Proper nouns keep their official spelling even when it breaks the convention: *International Council for Harmonisation*.

### Constructions to avoid

Every item here is a prohibition. None of them licenses breaking the chain of consequence described above.

**Native-marked.** Cleft sentences (*What remains undeveloped is X*, *it is what made this possible*). Fronted participial phrases (*Taken together, these texts position...*). Pied-piped relatives (*the party on whom the obligation falls*; write *the party it binds*). Intransitive idiomatic verbs (*The candidates grouped into three families*; write *Three families appeared*). Heavy nominalization (*The identification of X is among the least adopted components*; write *sites rarely identify X*).

**Absolute prohibitions.** Em dash and en dash. Exclamation mark. Contractions.

**`rather than` and every disguise of it**: `and not`, `instead of`, `not X but Y`, `it is not X, it is Y`, `X, not Y`. The construction is banned, not only the phrase. This is one of the author's strongest objections and it recurs, so check explicitly before delivering. The only exception is where the argument-structure skill identifies a necessary conceptual distinction.

**The self-commenting tail.** A main clause, a comma, a coordinator, and a pronoun pointing back at the clause just written in order to announce its status or significance. Offending shapes: `X, and it is the one where Y`, `X, and this is what Y`, `X, and that claim follows from Y`, `X, and the boundary follows directly`. The pronoun has no noun antecedent, it refers to the proposition, and the tail exists to tell the reader how important the sentence was.

**The test is what the second clause carries, never which word introduces it.** A second clause carrying new content is a chain link and stays, whatever it starts with.

| Defect | Legitimate |
| :-- | :-- |
| The register is maintained, and that is what the monitor reads. | The register is maintained, and the monitor reads it at the start of the visit. |
| The site holds no account, and this is the gap. | The site holds no account, and no clause requires it to hold one. |
| The claim stops there, and that is the limit. | The claim stops there, because the search was narrative. |

Searching `, and it `, `, and this `, `, and that ` returns candidates, never verdicts. Do not delete a connective that is doing cohesive work. Where the tail is real, delete the tail and let the next sentence carry the point. The form `, which is the difference between...` is the same defect wearing a relative pronoun, and the same test applies to it.

**The announcing opener.** A paragraph must begin with its substance. Do not open with a short sentence, roughly under twelve words, whose only function is to label what the paragraph will contain or name which item in a series follows. Offenders: *Two findings bound what this line has delivered.* *The second body of work measures the site.* *The defense rests on two grounds.* *Four objectives follow.* Begin with the finding, the study, the clause, the figure or the object itself. Where the reader needs the ordinal, fold it into the first substantive sentence.

**A closing sentence that merely repeats what the paragraph already said.** A consequence is not a repetition. *Every link in that chain is therefore only as reliable as the one before it* states something the paragraph had not yet stated, so it belongs. *The four pillars therefore answer the four functions described above* adds nothing and goes.

**Also out.** Announcing and deferring (*This is taken up in Chapter 6*); either explain it in one sentence or say nothing. Opening a section by restating its title. A colon mid-sentence except before a genuine list, example or definition after a complete statement. Meta-commentary about the writing itself (*we prefer to say so*, *this is regularly misread*); state the thing, do not narrate the decision to state it. Aphorism and epigram.

Prefer common connectors: however, therefore, for this reason, in addition, but, so.

### Grammar and mechanics

Non-native writers lose credibility on agreement, so check these explicitly.

- Intervening phrases do not change agreement: *The results of the study show*
- *data*, *bacteria*, *media* take plural verbs; *criterion* singular, *criteria* plural
- Collective nouns are singular: *The research team was responsible*
- *each*, *every*, *either*, *neither* take singular verbs
- A quantity acting as one unit is singular: *A total of 25 mL was added*
- Percentage agreement follows the noun: *70% of the dose was eliminated*; *70% of the patients were discharged*
- Notional agreement where a plural reading is clearer: *A total of 41 patients were enrolled*
- No dangling participles: *After analyzing the data, we found...*, never *the results showed...*
- *respectively* only for ordered correspondence, at the end of the clause, omitted when the pairing is obvious
- Possessives on institutional actors: *on the sponsor's behalf*

**Punctuation.** A restrictive clause takes no commas and changes meaning: *The samples that were contaminated were discarded* means only some were. Semicolons only inside citation parentheses or complex list items; elsewhere use a period. No hyphen after intra, inter, pre, post, non, re, sub as a general rule (*predefined*, *intraoperative*), except where an established regulatory term keeps its spelling (*post-market clinical follow-up*).

**Numbers.** Numerals for measurements, data and values with units (25 cases, 89%, 12 domains). Spell out one to nine in general prose. Numerals throughout when small and large comparable values appear together. Never begin a sentence with a numeral. Decimal point, percentages with the symbol.

**Capitalization.** Capitalize a numbered cross-reference (*Section 3.2*, *Table 4.1*, *Chapter 6*), lowercase a general one (*the section*, *the table*). Lowercase disease names, methods and techniques unless they carry a proper name.

### Voice and tense

Active by default. Passive only where the action or recipient matters more than the actor, mainly in procedural description. Never alternate voice for the same actor in adjacent sentences. Match tense to scientific function: simple present for established knowledge, interpretation, definitions and artifacts that exist; simple past for what this work did and found; simple past or present perfect for previous studies.

### Chapter tone

**Methodology.** Every sentence answers what was done, or why that method was chosen. No sentence answers what it means. Justifying a choice takes one to three sentences; five lines of defense means the text has drifted into discussion. Prefer the shortest construction that preserves the reasoning.

**Discussion.** Every section has to put the work against something outside it: prior literature, a rejected alternative, an objection, a standard of evidence, or a boundary condition. A discussion section that only explains the work again is a defect.

# Part: Argument structure

## Argument structure

The dominant stylistic problem in this author's text is not vocabulary. It is the repetition of argumentative structures, and above all the habit of explaining an idea by first saying what it is not and then saying what it is.

When reviewing, look for repeated argumentative structure, repeated negation, short emphatic sentences, sentences left unconnected where a chain of consequence runs through them, lists assembled for rhythm, uniform paragraph shape, and claims stronger than the evidence. A structure a reader can predict two paragraphs in advance has the same defect at this level that a predictable word choice has at the sentence level (Predictable vocabulary in scientific-prose) and that a flat sentence-length profile has at the prose level (Sentence length in scientific-prose): all three are the same failure — content settling into a template instead of following what it actually needs — measured at a different grain.

### The opposition construction

Examples taken from the author's own text:

- *The problem is therefore not that instruments are missing. The problem is direction.*
- *The site is the object of these measures. It is not the party that operates them.*
- *What is missing is not a rule. It is a layer.*

The construction is legitimate and can be effective. The problem is using it repeatedly as the mechanism through which arguments are developed.

**The rule.** Keep oppositions that clarify a genuine conceptual distinction, such as the difference between sponsor-level and site-level oversight, where the contrast carries information the reader needs. Delete oppositions that only make an assertion more emphatic. An opposition that could be replaced by a plain positive statement with no loss of meaning was doing emphasis, not analysis.

**Density limit.** In a chapter-length passage the construction should appear a small number of times and never twice in the same section. If two consecutive paragraphs both develop their point by negation, rewrite one.

This rule is separate from the phrase-level ban on `rather than` and its disguises in the scientific-prose skill. That ban removes the compressed form inside one sentence. This rule removes the two-sentence form, and more importantly removes the habit of thinking in that shape.

### Vary the development path

The text tends to follow one path repeatedly:

> conventional interpretation → rejection → reformulation → consequence for the framework

A reader who has seen that sequence three times anticipates the fourth, and anticipation is the opposite of what an argument should produce.

Other ways to develop a point, all usable and all under-used:

- **Evidence first, interpretation second.** Report the study, the clause or the figure, then say what follows from it
- **Consequence first, cause second.** State what happens at the site, then explain the condition that produces it
- **Condition and limit.** State where the claim holds and where it stops, with no rejected alternative anywhere in the paragraph
- **Enumeration without contrast.** Three properties of an instrument, stated in sequence
- **Question and answer.** Pose the question the section exists to answer, then answer it
- **Concession first.** Grant the opposing case at full strength, then state what survives it
- **Mechanism.** Describe how the thing works, step by step, and let the reader draw the conclusion

A discussion chapter should use several of these across its sections. Two adjacent sections built on the same pattern is a defect even when both are individually well written. When reviewing, map each section's development path in one phrase; if two neighbors share it, rewrite one.

### Short assertive sentences

Short declarative sentences used to produce emphasis are individually effective. Repeated use moves the text toward an argumentative essay and away from a scientific chapter.

Offending shapes: *The test is weak.* *That is the asymmetry this thesis reports.* *None of them is a finding.* *The claim stops there.*

**The first remedy is binding, not deleting.** A run of short sentences is usually a cohesion problem before it is an emphasis problem. The author objects strongly to chopped prose: a page of unconnected declaratives reads like stop-and-go traffic and hides the chain of consequence that the argument depends on. Join the run with a connective first, following the cohesion section of the scientific-prose skill. Delete only what survives that and is still doing nothing but emphasis.

Keep short sentences where they carry a definition, a figure, a limit or a plain fact: *Adoption lags the texts.* *Traceability is the second condition.* Even the permitted kind should not appear twice in a row.

**The deletion test, applied after binding.** Delete the sentence. If only force is lost, it was emphasis and stays deleted. If information is lost, it can stay, provided its neighbors are not also short and unconnected.

### Proportionality of the claim

Many statements in the drafts are more absolute than the evidence behind them. Preserve the clarity of the sentence and replace the categorical conclusion with a claim proportional to what was shown.

| Categorical | Proportional |
| :-- | :-- |
| No instrument requires X | None of the three instruments examined here requires X |
| This is the only component with no precedent | We found no regulatory precedent for this component |
| A site without this renegotiates its practice at every handover | A site without this has no record to carry across a handover |
| The framework converts quality into a capability owned by the site | The framework is designed so that the site holds the record of its own quality |

Scope absolute quantifiers to the work examined. Check every *every*, *never*, *always*, *none*, *only*, *all* and *no one*: each needs either a scope that makes it true or a replacement. Scope them to what was actually examined, such as the three instruments compared, the works the search returned, or the organizations in the dataset.

An absolute quantifier counting a closed set the work itself produced is correct and needs no softening. *All twelve design requirements fell inside one of the four pillars* is a statement about a set of twelve, and it stands.

### Grouping: the list of three is a default shape

The number of items in a series must follow the content. It must never follow cadence. This author's text groups ideas in threes so often that the shape has become a tic, and the effect is a page on which every idea appears to have the same weight and the same number of parts. It is the single most persistent defect in his writing and it has survived several rounds of correction.

Examples from his own text, all assembled rather than counted:

- *These Critical-to-Quality factors guide decisions about study processes, data collection, and the allocation of resources.*
- *Its provisions address clinical evidence, device traceability, and the conduct of clinical investigations.*
- *The site must arrange those assessments, check whether the images are usable, and address missed or inadequate examinations.*

Each reads well alone. Together they produce a text in which everything arrives in packets of three, and the reader stops registering content and starts hearing rhythm.

**Counting.** Write the number the subject actually has. If there are two, write two, and resist the pull to find a third that rounds the sentence out. If there is one, write one. If there are five, write five; a list of five is legitimate and does not need pruning to three for elegance.

**The deletion test.** Remove the third item and read the sentence again. If the sentence loses information, the item belongs. If it loses only rhythm, cut it.

**A real set of three is not an exemption.** A detector run on a chapter flagged eleven triads, including every one that had been exempted as genuine: the three parallel verbs of a control (*prevent, detect, and respond*), the three axes of a risk matrix (*impact, probability, and detectability*), three named regulatory documents. What gets registered is the shape on the page, not whether the three items are real. Being real settles only how many items exist. It never licenses presenting them as a coordinated series.

**Conversion forms.** Where a real set of three has to appear, use one of these:

- **A pair with the third attached.** *implant identifiers and component sizes, together with the intraoperative findings behind them*
- **A pair and a named remainder.** *sorted the challenges under credentialing and standardization, with monitoring as a third group*
- **Two sentences, bound.** *It explains how a sponsor works out its CtQ factors and then puts monitoring resources where the risk is highest, and it also covers how oversight is coordinated across several sites.*
- **A sentence for each item**, where the items differ in kind or in weight
- **Subordination**, with the principal item governing and the rest attached
- **The category instead of the members**, where naming the class is more informative
- **A numbered list**, where the items are procedural and the reader will refer back
- **A table**, where each item carries several attributes
- **Recount the set honestly.** Three items are sometimes two padded to three

When a series is broken into separate sentences, bind them. Splitting a triad into three unconnected declaratives trades one defect for another.

**Density.** Across a whole chapter the coordinated series of three should be close to absent. One or two survive, and each has to be a case where no other form works: a statute whose official name contains the series, or the set of instruments the work itself compares. Two in one paragraph is always wrong. Series of four or more are subject to the same discipline and are not a way around the rule. A run of parallel clauses beginning with the same word carries the defect too, even without commas.

**Unequal weight.** Not every idea is equally robust, and the prose has to show that. A coordinated series flattens its members into equal importance, so any list mixing a major element with two minor ones misreports the argument. Put the main item in the main clause and the secondary ones in a subordinate clause or the following sentence, or give the main item its own sentence and let the minor ones follow as examples.

### Paragraph and section shape

Paragraph length follows the reasoning. One argument that genuinely runs ten sentences gets ten, and a fact the next paragraph builds on can stand alone. Do not equalize them, and do not manufacture a one-sentence paragraph to break a run.

Each paragraph has one function and does not restate another. Space should match weight in the argument, not coverage for its own sake.

Vary the grammatical shape of the first sentence across consecutive paragraphs. A run of paragraphs all opening *The first...*, *The second...*, *The third...* reads as a template, and subject-first openers should stay under about 60% of paragraphs.

### Habits to watch

- **Evaluative adjectives without a criterion**: *substantial*, *significant*, *distinctive*, *robust*, *comprehensive*, *crucial*. State relative to what, or delete
- **Empty intensifiers**. Replace with the figure: *very large increase* becomes *a 45% increase*
- **Stacked modifiers** on one noun, and long noun strings that stop being readable
- **Preposition chains**: *the analysis of the effect of the treatment on the growth* becomes *treatment effects on growth*
- **Artificial symmetry of coverage**
- **Citation as ornament**. A reference must support an analysis

### Reporting

Read three consecutive paragraphs aloud. If a cadence is audible, the structure is repeating. If the reading keeps stopping and restarting, the sentences were chopped and need binding.

Report substantive changes, unverifiable points, and the decisions required from the author. Do not present mechanical checks as an achievement; he expects them to pass.

# Part: Evidence and delivery

## Evidence and delivery

### Honesty rules

Never invent a number, a citation, a source, a year, a finding or a precision. Verify claims against the actual source or the actual thesis document, never against drafts previously produced in a working folder.

When a claim cannot be sourced, say so explicitly and offer the options: provide a source, weaken the claim, or remove it. Mark uncertain transcriptions and missing citation years visibly instead of filling them.

Distinguish three things that are easy to blur: what the work demonstrated, what it was designed to enable, and what is only an expected benefit.

Limitations must name which inference was weakened, not merely state that limitations exist. Locate uncertainty in a concrete condition, sample or measurement.

Never present a rule as coming from a source that does not contain it.

**A worked case.** An earlier draft carried "the median investigation was conducted at four sites with a median target enrollment of 80 participants". Those figures did not appear in any file under review. The correct move was to remove them, state the volume constraint without a figure, and say that the recalculation had not been done. Not to find a plausible substitute.

This rule also bounds the predictable-vocabulary and sentence-variation work in scientific-prose. Making a claim more specific is good practice only when the added specificity is verified. A number, name, or date inserted only to make a sentence sound less generic, with no source behind it, is fabrication wearing the shape of a stylistic fix, not a style improvement.

### Verb strength

Match the verb to the strength of the evidence.

| Verb | Requires |
| :-- | :-- |
| demonstrates | strong evidence and an adequate design |
| indicates, suggests | supported interpretation |
| is consistent with | a result that merely fails to contradict |
| is associated with | correlation, never implying cause |

These verbs survive the plainness rule in the scientific-prose skill, because the choice between them carries the evidential claim.

Always separate the result from its interpretation. Result: *We found that X increased Y.* Interpretation: *Our results suggest that X increases Y.*

### Information density

The strongest marker of artificial text is high fluency with low verifiable content. Every central claim must carry something observable, measurable or methodologically justifiable.

Where a work has no experimental data, density comes from elsewhere: clause numbers and named instruments instead of *the regulation requires*; published magnitudes such as kappa values, case counts, percentages and years; the decision explained together with the alternative rejected and the reason; and the exact boundary at which a claim stops holding.

### Terminology and abbreviations

One object, one name. Never vary the term for elegance. Repeating the correct technical term beats synonym drift. This rule overrides the plain-vocabulary preference: a defined term stays exact even when a plainer word exists.

Define an abbreviation at first mention, then use it consistently and never revert to the full form. Verify that the definition precedes every use, including in text inserted later. Keep the set small and do not abbreviate a term used only once. Standard SI units need no definition. Define statistical abbreviations in a thesis. Full names of documents and formal legal citations keep their full form even after the abbreviation exists.

### Voice, tense and numbers

Check that voice does not alternate for the same actor, and that tense matches scientific function in each passage: simple present for established knowledge, interpretation and definitions; simple past for what this work did and found.

Numbers must be traceable to a source or a calculation. Check that every cross-reference points at an element that exists under the current numbering, which matters most after renumbering tables or merging sections.

### Rule precedence

1. Requirements of the university, programme, journal or funding body
2. The selected style manual
3. These skills
4. Where two valid options remain, pick one and hold it consistently across the whole document

### Before delivering

Run these as checks. Do not claim the text is clean without measuring.

**Cohesion first**, because the other checks are prohibitions and a text audited only against prohibitions comes out chopped.

1. Find every run of three or more consecutive sentences that each open a fresh subject with no connective, and bind the ones where a chain of consequence runs through them. Check that each chain closes on a sentence marked as the consequence

**Structure and register next.** The argument-structure and scientific-prose skills hold the substance of items 2 to 8.

2. Count opposition constructions, one-sentence and two-sentence forms. State which genuine distinction each survivor carries
3. Map each section's development pattern in one phrase. Two adjacent sections sharing a pattern means one gets rewritten
4. Count short declarative sentences under eight words. Bind first, then apply the deletion test to whatever is left, and check that none sit next to each other
5. List every absolute quantifier and scope it to what was examined
6. Find every coordinated series of three, including ones whose three items are real. Convert all but one or two, and bind the result where the conversion produced separate sentences. Do the same for series of four and for runs of parallel clauses
7. Scan every verb and framing noun for a plainer equivalent, including words on no list
8. Read three consecutive paragraphs aloud. An audible cadence means the structure is repeating. A reading that keeps stopping and restarting means the sentences were chopped

**Sentence level, with target counts.**

9. `rather than`, `and not`, `instead of`, `not X but Y` — reduce to zero
10. Self-commenting tails. Search `, and it `, `, and this `, `, and that `, `, which is the `. The search returns candidates, never verdicts: keep every second clause that carries new content, and delete only tails that comment on the status of the clause before them
11. First sentence of every paragraph — no announcing opener
12. Em dashes, en dashes, exclamation marks, contractions — all zero
13. British spellings against the American convention, allowing proper nouns — zero
14. Spread of sentence and paragraph lengths, not a mean. If sentences all come out the same size, the text was written to a shape. Check separately that no long sentence has to be read twice and no short ones are stacked
15. Clefts, fronted participials, pied-piped relatives. `Taken together` is the one that recurs
16. Meta-commentary about the writing, and any sentence built to be quotable

**Content.**

17. Each abbreviation defined before first use, never alternating with the full form
18. Voice does not alternate for the same actor
19. Tense matches scientific function in each passage
20. Every evaluative adjective has a criterion, and empty intensifiers are replaced by the figure
21. Terminology has not drifted across synonyms
22. Numbers follow the numeral rules and are traceable
23. Every cross-reference points at an element that exists under the current numbering
24. Each paragraph has one function and does not restate another
25. Scan for the flagged predictable-vocabulary words that survived the plain-word substitution (scientific-prose), and for the same discourse marker opening two paragraphs in one section

### Document conventions for deliverables

When producing a thesis chapter as a file:

| Item | Decision |
| :-- | :-- |
| Alignment and layout | Justified, first-line indent 720 twips, no blank line between paragraphs |
| Headings | Word Heading 1 and Heading 2 styles, so the navigation pane builds |
| Format | The author prefers downloadable deliverables, .docx or .html, over inline code blocks |

When a table is recovered from an older version of the thesis, convert its spelling, terminology and cross-references to the current version before inserting it, and state what was changed and what could not be verified.

### How to report

Reply to the author in Portuguese. Deliver academic text in English.

Be direct, disagree when there is a reason, and say clearly when something cannot be verified. When he supplies his own well-written sentence that breaks a rule, say so and offer the trade-off; consistency outranks elegance in his stated preference.

Discuss and close the plan before generating files. Do not rewrite an approved plan without warning; signal structural changes first.

Report changes, unresolved verification issues, and the decisions for the author. Close with a numbered action summary and sequenced questions. Do not claim that a text is clean merely because the scan was performed.
