# 3. Identified Gaps and Framework Design Requirements

## 3.1 Scope and analytical basis of this chapter

This chapter reports the results of the first two DSRM activities. Section 3.2 presents the comparison of the normative instruments and the gaps it identified. Section 3.3 presents the site-level CtQ domains, the operational areas of a surgical center where a local failure would most directly affect participant protection or the reliability of the results. Section 3.4 describes the conditions under which sites conduct these investigations, which determine both what the framework must control and the form it can take. Section 3.5 states the design requirements that follow, and Table 3.4 traces each one to its origin.

## 3.2 The regulatory and operational gap

We compared ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and MDR Annex XV clause by clause. The unit of comparison is the individual requirement, because that is the level at which a clause creates an obligation. For each requirement we recorded the obligation each instrument creates, the party it binds, and the clause it derives from. Section 2.2 describes the procedure. The comparison sorted the requirements into three categories: strongly harmonized, partially harmonized, and unaddressed at site level.

The first result is agreement. At system level, the three instruments place the main quality-management function on the sponsor. ICH E6(R3) assigns the quality system to the sponsor, ISO 14155:2026 assigns clinical quality management to the sponsor, and the MDR assigns monitoring to the sponsor [6, 8, 10]. At requirement level, a requirement is strongly harmonized when the three instruments in the clause comparison create an equivalent obligation on the same party. Three areas fall here. Investigator qualification and protocol compliance, source documentation and data integrity, and protocol deviation (PD) documentation are treated in broadly equivalent terms, both in principle and in the obligation they create.

The second result is partial agreement. A requirement is partially harmonized when the instruments agree on the principle but bind a different party or use different terms, and safety reporting is the only case. The instruments agree on the principle of expedited reporting, but they differ in two ways that matter in practice. ISO 14155 sets no fixed reporting deadline for the investigator. It requires reporting to the sponsor without unjustified delay and leaves the operative timeline to the Clinical Investigation Plan (CIP). ICH E6(R3) and MDR Annex XV specify timelines, and those timelines bind different parties. Throughout this comparison, a duty assigned to the investigator is reported as an investigator duty.

The instruments also differ in how they classify an event. Since the 2026 update ISO14155 separates events related to the investigational device from events related to procedures required by the CIP that fall outside routine clinical practice, and the two are assessed separately [8]. It also establishes central review and classification methods for safety or effectiveness endpoints, including serious adverse device effects (SADEs) and device deficiencies (DDs), when a Clinical Events Committee (CEC) is used [8]. The note to Clause 6.2.3 expressly recognizes that national regulations do not necessarily require categorization of AEs related to these procedures. ICH E6(R3) has no equivalent category because it was written for medicinal products, where the intervention and the procedure are one thing. These divergences affect how a site-level instrument has to be built, and Section 4.5.4 constructs the safety indicator accordingly.

The third category comprises five requirements classified as gaps at site level. Table 3.1 lists each gap alongside the corresponding provisions in the instruments. Most of these functions are required at trial level, with responsibility assigned to the sponsor. The site participates by supporting monitoring, following the CIP, reporting observations, and implementing corrective actions assigned to it [6, 8, 10]. For these functions, the gap concerns the absence of an explicit requirement for arrangements that the site owns and operates.

Under the rule in Section 2.4.2, sponsor-assigned requirements were retained when site activities determined whether they were fulfilled. Events relevant to these functions arise in the operating room, during follow-up visits, and in site records. The site therefore contributes directly to detecting and controlling these events, while the instruments generally assign the overarching systems to the sponsor. The paragraphs following Table 3.1 explain the site-level relevance of each gap.

ICH E6(R3) and ISO 14155:2026 already give the investigator several quality duties, such as supervising delegated activities and making sure that staff are qualified and resources adequate [6, 8]. ICH E6(R3) also asks the investigator to maintain appropriate site processes [6]. These duties appear in separate clauses, and meeting them consistently depends on a system that organizes them, but no clause in the three instruments requires that system. Gap 1 is therefore the absence of a structured site-level QMS, which the manufacturer and the medical laboratory in the same chain hold under ISO 13485:2016 and ISO 15189:2022 (Section 3.2.1).

The instruments require the risks of an investigation to be assessed and reviewed throughout its conduct, and they assign this cycle to the sponsor [6, 8]. Many of these risks come from the way the site executes the protocol, and the site sees them first (Section 3.4.3). For the cycle to work at that level, the site needs a register of its own risks (Gap 2), fed by an internal observation of what it actually does (Gap 4). Those observations then have to lead to correction and prevention through an improvement loop (Gap 5). The duty formally belongs to the sponsor, but the work done at the site decides whether the cycle works. The instruments, however, only ask the site to support sponsor monitoring and to apply the corrective actions it receives.

On surgical competence the three instruments agree, and sites can meet them, because all three treat competence as a qualification confirmed before the investigation starts. The surgical trials literature goes further and recommends that the surgeon’s performance also be followed during conduct, since it changes along the learning curve, and it reports that trials rarely do so [35, 36, 37, 39]. We identified Gap 3 by testing the comparison against this literature (Section 2.2.3), and the gap is the distance between the practice it recommends and what the instruments require.

The instruments have also moved in a consistent direction. ICH E6 described the duties of each party as procedures in 1996, ICH E6(R2) added a quality-management section in 2016, and ICH E6(R3) now requires investigators to maintain appropriate site processes and to ensure that qualified staff and adequate resources support trial conduct [3, 6]. ISO 14155:2026 strengthened ongoing risk review and requires proportionate responses to emerging concerns [8]. Each revision moved quality from detection after the fact toward prevention and gave the site a larger share of the responsibility, while final responsibility for trial-level quality management stayed with the sponsor [1, 6, 8, 10]. None of them has yet given the site a structure to carry that share.

The sponsor’s quality system cannot fill this space on its own, because it is built for the trial and not for any one site. In a multicenter investigation, the sponsor’s controls are designed to apply one standard across every participating site, which is a deliberate choice when the aim is comparability (Section 1.4.1). The same choice leaves out what is specific to each site, such as its daily workflow, its staffing, and the technical limits that follow from its size and structure. The literature describes the consequence from the site’s side. Site-level quality management is often reactive and handled case by case [42, 43, 44], standardized approaches remain uncommon [45], and sites experience risk-based monitoring as an external requirement over which they have little control [46, 47]. Part of the data that matter most is also generated at the point of care, where external oversight cannot see it in time (Section 3.4.3).

These five gaps do not necessarily indicate defects in the regulatory framework. Addressing them may support compliance with existing duties, even where the instruments do not explicitly require the proposed site-level arrangements. They mark where the site can act on the quality of the investigation and on the safety of its own conduct. Chapter 4 develops an operational layer to support the implementation of ICH E6(R3) principles in daily site practice.

### 3.2.1 Site conduct and the clinical evidence of the device

Under the MDR, every device subject to conformity assessment needs a clinical evaluation, with a level of clinical evidence appropriate to the device and its intended purpose [10]. Article 61 and Annex XIV set out how the evaluation is planned and how the data are appraised, and the results go into a clinical evaluation report (CER) that forms part of the technical documentation. Where the available data do not show conformity with the general safety and performance requirements, the manufacturer has to generate new data through clinical investigations, which Article 62(1), Articles 63 to 80, and Annex XV govern [10]. Conduct at the site is therefore not a peripheral activity, because the data produced in the operating room and in follow-up become clinical evidence. The clinical evidence becomes the CER, and the report becomes part of the file the notified body decides on, so every link in that chain is only as reliable as the one before it.

The manufacturer, which compiles the CER, works under a documented quality system. ISO 13485:2016 sets that system out, including corrective and preventive action, and ISO 14971:2019 adds a documented cycle for managing the risks of the device [52, 53]. A medical laboratory sits outside this chain, but it also produces results on which another party bases a decision, and ISO 15189:2022 gives it an equivalent system [54]. ISO 14155:2026 gives the investigational site nothing of the kind, because its Clause 9.1 places clinical quality management on the sponsor, while Clause 10, on the responsibilities of the principal investigator (PI), sets entry qualifications for the investigator and the site without asking for an ongoing local system [8]. The fourth edition added risks that arise from clinical-investigation processes and a provision on the CEC, so the sponsor’s side of the architecture grew further [8]. Of the two organizations that produce the evidence behind the CER, the manufacturer and the site, only the site works without a quality system of its own.

## 3.3 Site-level Critical-to-Quality domains

The comparison left us with a classified set of requirements. Three further steps turn them into domains: a filter, a grouping by operational area, and a test against the four criteria described in Section 2.4.1.

ICH E8(R1) assigns the identification of CtQ factors to the sponsor and other parties designing the study [5]. That route is a translational step which neither ICH E8(R1) nor ICH E6(R3) performs. It identifies the CtQ factors that matter at site level, as distinct from the trial-level factors a sponsor defines.

The difference between the two is not only a matter of detail. A trial-level analysis asks which design features of the study are critical to the interpretability of its results. A site-level analysis asks which operational features of this particular center would compromise participant safety or data reliability in the cases that this center contributes. Device-specific guidance on CtQ factors exists and is useful, but it is written from the same trial-level perspective [34, 55].

This process produced the twelve domains set out in Table 3.2. Each domain is described by what it covers, by the principal threat that arises when control is inadequate, and by the place in the framework where it is addressed.

Several of these domains take a different operational form in device investigations, and they account for the specificity of what follows. Device management and traceability require accountability from receipt through use in a named participant and eventual return or explantation [8, 10]. For implantable devices, ISO 14155:2026 also adds an implant-card requirement [8]. Procedural delivery and learning-curve control deal with surgeon-dependent variability, and they move it from a design problem, which is how the methodological literature has treated it, to a site-level control problem. Imaging control recognizes that imaging can determine eligibility, provide an endpoint, and supply safety data. The supporting literature is general or based on knee trials, so it supports these imaging functions and does not establish their frequency in shoulder investigations [56, 57]. Variability in how images are acquired can therefore produce systematic error that cannot be distinguished from genuine clinical variance.

These twelve domains govern the framework in two ways. Every component is introduced to control the risks associated with one or more domains, and every performance indicator is anchored in at least one domain.

## 3.4 Operational conditions constraining the design

The gaps establish what is missing, and the domains establish what has to be controlled. Neither yet establishes what form a site-level architecture can take. That is settled by the operational conditions under which the architecture would have to work, and these conditions constrain the design as tightly as the regulatory analysis does.

Surgical device investigations are conducted under conditions that a site-level framework has to take into consideration. Six main conditions apply in total. Five are structural and common to surgical trials, and two of those are restrictive enough that their constraints are tackled separately in this chapter. The sixth, although not structural, heavily influences the design and is one of the findings of this thesis.

### 3.4.1 Design consequences of the five structural conditions

Section 1.3 established five conditions that separate surgical device investigations from the pharmacological model. Each one has a direct consequence for how quality can be managed.

1\. Irreversibility. A drug can be stopped, reduced, or replaced if it fails or causes harm. A surgical implantation cannot. Once a prosthesis is fixed to bone, the procedure has produced a permanent change in the patient's anatomy. This raises the operational cost of every quality failure that happens at surgery. An incorrect implant size, an undocumented technical deviation, or a missing traceability record cannot be put right afterward in the way a transcription error can. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) has described poor risk identification and mitigation as producing a reactive approach based mainly on corrective action [1]. ICH E8(R1) therefore calls for resources to identify and prevent or control errors that matter (§ 3.3.2), and the CTTI CtQ principles also place attention on prevention before conduct [55]. A site-level architecture therefore must identify in advance which steps of the surgical pathway are irreversible and implement QA actions to prevent errors.

2\. Operator dependence. Results depend on the device, and they depend just as much on the person and technique used to implant it [19, 58]. Requirements for surgeon qualification and adequate training with the specific device are already established. Other sources of variation also require control, including learning-curve effects, differences among surgeons in positioning and soft-tissue management, and intraoperative judgment. The study by Blencowe et al. [59] provides a direct precedent: standards for surgical intervention delivery can be agreed before the trial and monitored during conduct through forms that capture deviations. Quality management therefore has to include some way of characterizing this variation, because removing it would mean removing surgical judgment itself. The consequence for the design is that the framework needs the operator as a unit of analysis, alongside the participant and the case.

3\. Persistent traceability. Pharmaceutical and device trials both require records linking batch or serial numbers to participants [6, 8]. The distinction lies in duration and persistence. A medicinal product is used or returned, whereas an implanted device may remain in the participant for years and its records must also cover return or explantation. For implantable devices, post-investigation procedures must cover traceability, and ISO 14155:2026 adds an implant card [8, 10]. Traceability here also extends to reconstructing the surgical decisions that produced that configuration in that participant, which creates a documentary burden of a different order.

4\. The operational weight of follow-up. Long follow-up is not unique to surgical trials, but the measurements involved are. ROM and radiographic assessment of implant position require the participant to be physically present, while PROs require consistent collection at the specified time points. Consistent assessment procedures and independent central review, where appropriate, are methodological responses to interobserver variability, but none of the three governing instruments requires the same assessor at every visit [25, 60]. Imaging standardization also requires compatible acquisition procedures and quality control (QC) [56, 61]. This raises the cost of every visit and increases the risk of attrition over the years between surgery and the late assessments. Orthopaedic studies with at least three years of follow-up reported 14.8% loss to follow-up, compared with 9.8% in studies with shorter follow-up [23], and one shoulder arthroplasty cohort reported 34.3% loss by the seventh year [24].

5\. Limited classification reliability. Shoulder investigations rely on radiological and clinical classifications that in many cases show only fair-to-moderate inter-observer reliability [62]. Widely used classifications for proximal humeral fractures have been reported with inter-observer kappa coefficients in the fair range, even when three-dimensional models reconstructed from computed tomography (CT) data are used [63]. Two assessors looking at the same patient can therefore reach different conclusions about eligibility or about an outcome, and the database cannot distinguish that variance from genuine clinical variance. Outcome measurement makes this worse. Shoulder arthroplasty trials vary widely in outcome domains, instruments, and assessment time points. That inconsistency was substantial enough to motivate core outcome sets for shoulder disorders [64, 65, 66].

### 3.4.2 Operator-dependent variability as a design constraint

Operator dependence produces an effect known as clustering [67]. When one surgeon operates on several participants, those participants are not independent of one another for the purposes of quality. They share that surgeon's technique, intraoperative decision thresholds, and perioperative management. A problem in that surgeon's execution therefore does not affect one case. It affects every case that surgeon contributes. This also affects trial design and analysis. Failure to account for non-negligible clustering produced type I error rates above 20% in simulations [68], while only 21.5% of eligible orthopaedic randomized trials involving multiple centers or treatment providers used an analysis that accounted for clustering [69].

The surgical trials methodology literature recognizes clustering as one of the central challenges of randomized trials involving surgery, alongside the learning curve that interacts with it [67, 70]. How it is handled in practice remains uneven. A survey of 44 United Kingdom Clinical Trials Units found that clustering was managed mainly at two points. The first is stratification at design, which means randomizing in a way that balances each surgeon's cases across the arms of the trial. The second is adjustment at analysis. Eighty-nine percent of units managed learning by setting a minimum expertise level for the treatment provider, and only one unit reported analyzing learning effects as part of the main analysis [71].

Operator learning is therefore controlled mainly at entry and is rarely observed during conduct. In a review of 247 published surgical trials, 39% addressed expertise through predefined surgeon credentials, while only one trial analyzed learning during conduct; a related analysis of funder guidance identified the same general concern [72]. The documentary search reported in Section 3.2.1 reaches the same boundary: the governing instruments address qualification and training but do not assign ongoing observation of operator learning to the site. The interval between entry qualification and performance during conduct is exactly what a site-level instrument could cover, and it is the same interval that Table 3.1 identifies as covered by no regulatory obligation. A protocol can define a procedure in general terms, but it cannot standardize the many small decisions through which a particular surgeon implants a particular device in a particular anatomy. What the protocol cannot standardize, the site has to govern. Governing it requires an instrument that operates during conduct, and entry criteria alone will not do it.

### 3.4.3 Traceability as a point-of-care obligation

Unit-level traceability is distinctive in how detailed it is, and it is distinctive again in the circumstances under which it has to be met. It is executed in the operating room, under time pressure, by staff whose attention is on the surgical procedure. ISO 14155:2026 requires records of receipt, identification, quantity, dates of use, participant identification, and return or explantation, together with written procedures for the full accountability process [8]. The sponsor maintains the record of physical location through delivery to the site, and the investigator's records continue the link through use in the participant and any return or explantation. The MDR also requires access control, return procedures, and follow-up in the CIP, immediate identification and recall of devices in an emergency, and post-investigation traceability [10]. In surgery, the required dates of use and participant identification make capture at the point of care the most reliable operational response. A break in this chain can compromise regulatory compliance and the attribution of an AE to a specific device or lot. The cited clauses do not state that such a break automatically invalidates the affected case.

Traceability is the clearest example of a broader pattern that runs through the domains in Table 3.2. A class of critical data is generated at the point of care, external oversight cannot see it while it is happening, and it cannot be recovered once it is lost. Four examples share these three properties. They are the intraoperative decision to substitute a component and the technical deviation prompted by unexpected anatomy. They are also the immediate postoperative image that may or may not be adequate, and the early complication whose relationship to the device is still undetermined. Any architecture that deals with this class of data has to operate at the moment the data are generated. Verification comes too late.

### 3.4.4 Where these investigations are conducted

The registry search described in Section 2.3.1 produced 46 eligible industry-sponsored investigations of implantable shoulder devices. Repeated facility listings were cleaned and resolved at organization level, giving 235 organization-level entries. Each organization counted once in the distribution, while every associated ClinicalTrials.gov identifier remained linked to its record. Eight entries could not be resolved to a verifiable clinical organization or setting. Section 2.3.2 describes the normalization and evidence sources, together with the case-by-case review used for these decisions.

We reported the organizational environment through the four mutually exclusive categories shown in Table 3.3. This variable was selected because academic status and institutional ownership could be identified consistently from public institutional descriptions. The registry findings describe the selected sample and cannot establish the prevalence of comparable organizations in Europe or across the global population of shoulder device investigation sites.

### 3.4.5 The operating conditions of the target center

The distribution in Table 3.3 guided the selection of the target center. We defined it as a nonhospital organization with limited or uncertain permanent clinical trial infrastructure. This profile represents a deliberately demanding implementation case within a wider sample that also contains academic organizations and hospitals with established research structures.

The selection rests on the 38 non hospital organizations for which the consulted sources did not establish a permanent clinical trial structure. Under the adopted definition, this finding concerns publicly documented organizational support. It does not establish the absence of research activity or poorer trial conduct. Internal arrangements may not appear on public institutional pages, and temporary or sponsor-provided support may still be available.

The observed distribution still provides a relevant design condition. Publicly identifiable infrastructure was present in almost all academic and nonacademic hospital organizations, while it was present in 27 of the 65 non hospital organizations. The framework therefore could not assume that every participating organization had a dedicated clinical trial office or a permanent research quality function.

Where a permanent structure is not identifiable, research duties may be distributed among staff whose main responsibilities are clinical. Clinical investigations may also receive fewer dedicated organizational resources in these settings. The registry analysis did not measure staffing or budgets. It also gave no information about technology use or the priority assigned to research. We therefore treated these conditions as conservative design assumptions that require assessment during implementation.

The framework had to fit within existing site roles without depending on a new physical infrastructure or dedicated quality department. Its instruments had to work on paper or within an electronic system already available at the site. Key quality decisions also had to remain in records held by the organization when individual responsibilities changed.

These conditions define the minimum implementation level used in the scalability model presented in Section 4.7. Organizations with established research departments can place the same functions within their existing systems and use deeper controls. The registry findings support an adaptable design for a relevant subgroup. They do not establish that the framework can equalize performance across environments, which requires empirical evaluation.

## 3.5 Design requirements

Now that we have established what is critical and what the challenges are in developing the framework, we can determine the requirements it should be structured around. For example, the CtQs define what is important but do not tell us what the site must have in place for that domain to be under control.

With the creation of those requirements, we complete the second DSRM activity, the definition of the objectives of a solution, by turning each area of criticality into a statement of what an acceptable solution has to deliver, written so that a framework component can later be checked against it.

The three analyses described in this chapter culminated on the development of these twelve requirements. Table 3.4 states them, and each one is traced to its origin. Requirements DR1 to DR5 follow from the regulatory gaps. DR6 to DR9 follow from the CtQ analysis and from the structural conditions of surgical investigation. DR10 to DR12 follow from the operating conditions of the target center.

But the requirements and the domains are therefore not in one-to-one correspondence. Some domains already carry an obligation from one of the instruments. These produce no requirement, because the framework only adds where the instruments are silent. Other domains leave two obligations unmet, and each one produces its own requirement, as Table 3.4 shows.

Two properties of this set do not appear in Table 3.4, and both affect how the framework has to answer it. The first is that meeting one requirement can make another harder to meet. DR11 limits how DR1 to DR9 can be met. A site could satisfy every functional requirement by building a dedicated information system for the investigation. That solution would pass the regulatory analysis and fail the operational one. When two requirements point to different solutions, we treat the operational constraint as the binding one. A control that the site never executes protects no one and adds no value.

The second property asks whether the framework can be justified at all. The twelve requirements say what it must do for quality but no rule or guideline tells a site to run a quality system, which is the gap this work identifies. We therefore cannot justify its existence by pointing to a rule. We justify it by tracing it back. Each component answers a requirement, each requirement comes from a gap, a domain, or an operating condition, and all three came from the clause comparison in Section 2.2. The chain ends in the instruments, so nothing in the framework is invented. It takes duties that already exist and makes them workable at the site.
