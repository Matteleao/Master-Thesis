# 6. Conclusion

ICH E6(R3) and ISO 14155:2026 give the investigational site a larger share of the responsibility for quality, but most of the instruments reviewed in this thesis were built for the sponsor. The thesis set out to work out what structure a site can use to carry that responsibility in shoulder device investigations. Chapter 3 showed that none of the three instruments compared asks the site for a quality system of its own, while ISO 13485 describes one for the manufacturer and ISO 14155:2026 Clause 9 places clinical quality management on the sponsor. Chapter 4 then derived a site-level framework with four pillars, Governance, Risk Management, Operational Controls, and Performance Oversight, operated through twenty-four instruments that can be kept on paper across a seven-phase lifecycle.

Every component of the framework traces back to a design requirement, and every requirement to a regulatory gap, a site-level CtQ domain, or an operating condition of the target center. Most components organize duties the site already holds into something it can run. The SLCT is the exception, because none of the instruments examined assigns the site the observation of each operator's proficiency during conduct, and Section 5.1 treats it as both the most exposed claim of the framework and its clearest addition. The trace shows that the framework is coherent with the evidence it was built from, but it does not show that the framework is necessary or complete.

The registry analysis is the one part of this work that produced an empirical finding of its own. Among the 235 organizations taking part in industry-sponsored shoulder device investigations, about one in six were nonhospital organizations with no publicly documented permanent research structure. The finding does not measure conduct or resources, and Section 2.4.3 states its limits, but it shows that the framework cannot assume a dedicated research office at every site. The framework was designed for the Type B center, a private center that runs investigations recurrently alongside surgical care without a system dedicated to their quality, and Type C extends it to smaller centers that face the same problems with fewer people.

The design keeps the record of quality at the site. Section 5.1 examined routes that would supply the same structure from outside the site, and none of them keeps the record in the hands of the site while also capturing information at the point of care. A site that holds its own records can act on the risks of its own execution while they arise, and it can show, when a question comes, how it controlled its conduct.

This thesis carried out four of the six DSRM activities, and Section 5.7 names the two it did not report, demonstration and evaluation, as the limitation that weighs most on every claim in Chapter 5. No site has operated any instrument in this set. The expectations built into the design, such as reduced effort at close-out and earlier detection of procedural drift, remain expectations until an implementation study measures them in a center that runs the framework through a complete investigation.

The framework was built for one surgical specialty, and Section 5.6 states what would have to hold for it to transfer. The field structures, review triggers, and escalation logic carry no assumption specific to the shoulder, while the classification lists and imaging requirements do. Whether the first part survives a move to another implant-based specialty while the second is replaced is a claim this thesis makes and does not test.

Section 1.1 described a shift from a model in which the sponsor supplies the account of what happened at the site to one in which the site is expected to hold that account itself. The framework is one proposal for what that account should contain, derived from the same texts that gave the site its larger share of responsibility. Whether it serves the centers it was built for, at the right depth, is the question an implementation study now has to answer.
