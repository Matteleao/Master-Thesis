# 6. Conclusion

This thesis set out to answer a question that ICH E6(R3) and ICH E8(R1) raise without resolving: once the investigational site shares responsibility for quality, what structure does it use to discharge that responsibility. Chapter 3 located the reason no existing instrument answers the question already. The site is the only party in the device conformity chain without a documented quality system, while the manufacturer holds ISO 13485, the risk function holds ISO 14971, and the sponsor holds ISO 14155:2026 Clause 9. Chapter 4 built the missing layer: four pillars, Governance, Risk Management, Operational Controls, and Performance Oversight, operated through twenty-four paper-compatible instruments across a seven-phase lifecycle.

Every component in that architecture traces back to a design requirement, and every design requirement traces back to a regulatory gap, a site-level Critical-to-Quality domain, or an operating condition of the target center. Section 3.5 states the twelve requirements, and Table 4.1 maps each one to the component that answers it. Nothing in the framework asserts an obligation the regulatory analysis does not already imply, with one exception. Surgical Learning Curve Tracking answers no clause directly, because no instrument examined here requires operator proficiency to be observed once conduct has started. Section 5.1 treats that exception as the framework's most exposed claim and its clearest addition to what the field already requires.

The registry analysis in Section 2.3 is the one part of this work that produces an empirical finding of its own, and the rest reports a design. It shows that a meaningful share of the organizations running shoulder device investigations, roughly one in four in the sample examined, present no publicly documented permanent research structure. That finding does not measure conduct or resources directly, and Section 5.7 states its limits, but it supports treating the resource-limited, non-hospital center as an empirically grounded part of the target population.

This thesis establishes something narrower than the framework itself, and still worth stating plainly. It derives a site-level quality architecture for surgical device investigations without inventing an obligation, built from instruments a resource-limited center can operate on paper, and organized so that a reader can check any single component against the regulatory clause, the CtQ domain, or the operating condition that produced it. That traceability is the property Section 5.1 argues for over the alternative of asking the sponsor or a commercial vendor to supply the same architecture from outside. A framework built on that alternative would reach the site without making the site the party that holds it.

Chapter 4 therefore delivers a design produced through four of the six Design Science Research activities defined in Chapter 2. Section 5.7 names the two activities it does not report, demonstration and evaluation, as the limitation that weighs most on every claim in Chapter 5. No site has operated any instrument in this set. The expectations built into the design, reduced effort at close-out, earlier detection of procedural drift, protected margin at a resource-limited center, remain expectations until an implementation study measures them against a center that runs the framework through a complete investigation. Section 5.7 states what that study would need to establish.

The architecture was built for one surgical specialty by design, and Section 5.6 states what would have to hold for it to transfer. The field structure, review triggers, and escalation logic carry no assumption specific to the shoulder. The classification lists and the imaging views do, together with the five-stratum learning-curve model built around them. Whether the first survives a move to another implant-based specialty while the second is replaced is a claim this thesis makes and does not test.

Section 1.2 opened this thesis by describing a shift from a model in which the sponsor supplies the account of what happened at the site to one in which the site is expected to hold that account itself. A center that conducts a shoulder device investigation carefully can still fail to produce that account, since holding an account of its own processes was never assigned to it in the first place. The four pillars, the twenty-four instruments, and the seven-phase lifecycle are one answer to what that account should look like. Whether it is the right answer, at the right depth, for the centers it was built for, is the question Section 5.7 leaves to the field to test.

The framework does not close the gap between what the site is asked to demonstrate and what it currently has to demonstrate it with. It gives one traceable structure for closing it, derived clause by clause from the texts that created the gap in the first place.
