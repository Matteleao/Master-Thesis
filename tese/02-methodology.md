# 2. Methodology

## 2.1 Design research

### 2.1.1 Design science research methodology

As already stated, the objective of this thesis is to build a framework of quality that supports the guidelines required principles. To achieve this, our work followed a Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM), set out by Peffers et al. [49]. Design science research (DSR) creates and evaluates artifacts that solve identified organizational problems. DSRM organizes that work into six activities: problem identification and motivation, definition of the objectives for a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication.

Four of those activities are reported here Chapter 1 and Sections 3.2 and 3.3 carry the problem identification and motivation. Section 3.5 states the objectives for a solution, as design requirements DR1 to DR12. Chapter 4 reports the design and development, and this thesis is the communication. The remaining two were not undertaken.

Peffers et al. also state that what triggers the research matters, because it determines where in the DSRM sequence the work should start. Since this thesis originates from an observed problem of practice, it enters at the first activity. The paper also recognizes four kinds of artifact, which are constructs, models, methods, and instantiations. Since the problem here is the absence of a structured, site-level quality management architecture for surgical device investigations, the artifact is a model together with the methods that put it to work.

DSRM also requires that the relationship between problem and solution stay visible. We keep it visible by tracing every major component of the artifact back to the regulatory gap or the operational constraint that motivated it. Section 3.5 states those motivations as explicit design requirements, and Table 4.1 maps each component onto the requirement it answers.

### 2.1.2 Overview of the research process

To support the problem identification and motivation step we used three sources of evidence to feed the design. The first is an analysis of the normative instruments that govern device investigations. The second is a review of the literature. The third is a characterization of the setting where these investigations are conducted.

The regulatory analysis came first and produced the grid of domains that founded the design. The instruments stated what is required, the literature stated what the field has observed and proposed, and the site registry helps define where the work is done and direct construction. A requirement supported by more than one source therefore rests on more than one kind of evidence and helps improve its strength and relevance.

## 2.2 Analysis of the normative instruments

### 2.2.1 Instruments and their status

We used ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) as conceptual and design references since they are the main source for the definition of QbD, CtQ, and risk-proportionate quality principles. Although very important their main focus is still mainly drug investigational products, so we needed to go after the device-specific obligations from two other instruments. ISO 14155:2026 is the GCP standard for these investigations and our main source of investigator-level requirements. Regulation (EU) 2017/745, also known as medical device regulations (MDR), supplies the European legal framework, including the traceability and safety reporting requirements specific to devices.

We consulted three further standards: ISO 13485, ISO 14971 with its guidance document ISO/TR 24971, and ISO 15189. None of them creates an obligation for the investigational site. We use them as models of a documented quality system elsewhere in the same regulatory and supply chain. ISO 15189 is a structural analogy since a laboratory holds the same position as an investigational site, because both are hospital services that produce data for an external decision.

### 2.2.2 Derivation of the site-level Critical-to-Quality

We read the instruments from the most general to the most specific. ICH E8(R1) came first, then ICH E6(R3), and then the device-specific texts. The CtQ concept is expressly developed in the ICH guidelines [5, 6]. A documentary search performed on 20 September 2026 found no occurrence of the exact phrases “CtQ” or “QbD” in ISO 14155:2026 or Regulation (EU) 2017/745. This is a result limited to the texts and search terms examined, not proof that related concepts are absent. Reading in this order gave us an explicit definition and criteria before we reached the device texts, allowing the same frame to be applied across them.

ICH E8(R1) describes CtQ factors as “attributes of a study whose integrity is fundamental to the protection of study participants” and to reliable, interpretable results and decisions [5]. The guideline also identifies areas for consideration when determining CtQ factors. The following 18 items were recorded from that discussion:

- 1. Stakeholder engagement
- 2. Appropriate eligibility criteria
- 3. Adequacy of prior studies
- 4. Necessary data collection
- 5. Scientific study objectives
- 6. Well-defined endpoints
- 7. Meaningful control-group
- 8. Bias minimization
- 9. Participant protection
- 10. Prespecified statistical analysis
- 11. Clear participant information
- 12. Critical data integrity
- 13. Staff competencies and training
- 14. Risk-proportionate monitoring
- 15. Study feasibility
- 16. Data monitoring committee
- 17. Adequate sample size, duration, and visit frequency
- 18. Transparent and timely reporting

We can see that those considerations are more related to guide CtQs based on the perspective of the sponsor. Since our goal is different, we incorporated those considerations when seen fit and relevant but also created four more practical criteria based on an interpretation of the guideline. As a disclaimer, these labels are our own and the guideline does not identify them collectively.

Proportionality holds that procedures should match the risks involved and the importance of the information collected. This criterion is derived from ICH E8(R1), not from a requirement for procedural perfection [5, 6].

Parsimony holds that the set of critical factors should remain focused and that data and activities serving neither participant protection nor the primary objectives should be avoided [5].

Assigned responsibility holds that the identification and protection of critical factors should be allocated to named parties, with qualified staff and adequate resources supporting that allocation [5, 6].

Reliance on existing knowledge holds that what is already known about the product, condition, and population should shape both the factors selected and the controls applied [5]. At trial level, the sponsor’s quality management should incorporate relevant prior knowledge, while the site must ensure that personnel are appropriately qualified and trained for their assigned activities [6].

When tracing these criteria into ICH E6(R3), we found that proportionality reappears in Annex 1 through risk evaluation and control. The sponsor is expected to define pre-specified acceptable ranges for risks that may affect participant safety or the reliability of trial results; quality tolerance limits are one trial-level example of such ranges [6]. This is a sponsor duty at trial level. The guideline does not convert quality tolerance limits into a required site-owned instrument.

While reading ICH E6(R3) we recorded every attribute that introduced a concept CtQ, and for each one we recorded the supporting clause and the excerpt, so that each candidate carries its source in the text. We also recorded separately the mechanisms that ICH E6(R3) prescribes for operationalizing CtQ factors and we use them later in the instrument design described in Chapter 4.

We applied the same procedure, unchanged, to ISO 14155 and to MDR Annex XV, so that the three readings would be comparable. We then merged the three sets into a single matrix and by comparing and clustering each attribute, we analyzed how the instruments present it and how consistently they treat it as critical.

### 2.2.3 The clause-level comparison

We ran the comparison clause by clause across the attributes. For each we recorded three things: the obligation that each instrument creates, the party it binds, and the clause it comes from. We then sorted them into three categories.

An attribute is strongly harmonized when all three instruments create an equivalent obligation on the same party. It is partly harmonized when the instruments agree on the principle but bind a different party or use different terms. It is unaddressed at site level when a substantive obligation exists but falls on the sponsor, with no matching requirement on the site.

Convergence shows that an attribute matters but it does not mean that the attribute belongs in a site-level framework, so we filtered the matrix against three criteria. The first is the locus of responsibility. It excludes attributes that the sponsor controls and the site does not, and it keeps an attribute when the responsibility is formally the sponsor's but site execution decides whether it is met. The second is the scope of this thesis. It excludes attributes that would push the work past its declared boundary, or that would add complexity out of proportion to what they contribute. The third is material relevance to the surgical device context.

We did not use convergence alone as the selection rule since several attributes appear in ISO 14155 and the MDR but not in ICH E6(R3), which is what we expect from a guideline written for medicinal products.

We grouped the remaining attributes by the operational area of a surgical investigation they relate to. This produced the domains; those are operational areas that group corresponding attributes from the different guidelines.

For example, ICH E6(R3) yields an attribute covering investigational product management, which includes storage conditions, correct dispensing to an eligible participant, accountability, and expiry. ISO 14155 and the MDR yield further attributes on unit-level traceability, and these have no counterpart in ICH E6(R3). All of them converge to the same operational area, so they were grouped into a single domain, device management and traceability.

We then tested each group against the four criteria we drew from ICH E8(R1): proportionality, parsimony, assigned responsibility, and reliance on existing knowledge. The test asks whether the group qualifies as a CtQ area, and the result was twelve domains. All are reported in Section 3.3 and Section 3.5 builds the design requirements on them.

## 2.3 Literature review

### 2.3.1 Scope and approach

The review was narrative. It did not follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) protocol, and we do not present it as an exhaustive account of the field. Its purpose was to inform a design process. A narrative review is the appropriate form for mapping a problem space and locating the gaps in it. A narrative review may lose exhaustiveness, but it improves the focus on the right scope and prevent divergence, this is important since our relevant material already sits in five different fields.

Those fields are quality management in clinical research, SQA in trials, the methodology of surgical trials, the clinical literature on the shoulder, and the performance and readiness of investigational sites.

We used literature search engines such as Scopus, PubMed, OpenAlex and Google Scholar to find papers related to those fields and tracked citations from the articles we retrieved. We gave priority to recent work, because the regulatory environment has changed substantially in the last few years. Two categories of source are exempt from that priority. We kept methodological work whose content does not date, which applies to the surgical trials literature on learning and clustering effects. We used normative instruments and guidance documents in the version in force at the time of writing.

### 2.3.2 How the material was analyzed

We organized the reading around one translational question, derived from the QbD and risk-proportionate quality principles of ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3). The question was what a site would need in order to implement those principles into their operational processes.

With that in mind we took notes throughout, recording what is established, where sources disagree, and where evidence is absent. We then identified recurring themes and grouped them. Three themes shaped the rest of the work, and Section 3.2 reports them alongside the regulatory gap they help to define.

This search was not systematic, so any reported absence of published work is an outcome of this review and should not be read as proof that no such work exists. We therefore extended the material beyond peer-reviewed publications to regulatory, standards, professional, and industry guidance. The interpretive sources were the CTTI’s Recommendations: QbD and QbD Documentation Tool; the MDIC’s 2017 device-specific supplement on CtQ factors; and Medical Device Coordination Group (MDCG) 2020-1, Guidance on Clinical Evaluation (MDR)/Performance Evaluation (IVDR) of Medical Device Software. MDCG 2020-1 was used only for its software-specific scope and was not treated as general guidance for all device clinical evaluation.

### 2.3.3 Analysis of existing frameworks

The review had a second function beyond mapping the problem. It had to establish what the field has already built, so that the framework could generate something new or improve what already exists.

For every framework, consensus statement, and measurement instrument we identified, we recorded four components. These are the level at which it is defined, the party that operates it, the party it is applied to, and the clinical field it was developed in. Recording these components made visible a pattern that a summary of content would have hidden. Instruments that look similar in subject matter differ in who holds them.

We then compared each instrument with the framework under construction, component by component. For each pairing we recorded one of three outcomes: a component with a direct counterpart, a component with a partial counterpart operating at a different level, and a component with no counterpart. Section 5.2 reports that comparison and states what the framework adds.

## 2.4 Registry analysis of investigational site characteristics

The framework's relevance and functionality depend on the conditions and limitations of the site, so the work needed a description of where and how well prepared the sites were for these investigations. We found no published source establishing that distribution, so we produced the characterization ourselves from registry data, and Section 3.4.4 reports the results.

### 2.4.1 Data source and search

We retrieved study records through the public ClinicalTrials.gov API v2 (documentation and records accessed 20 September 2026). We selected this source because it provides structured, publicly accessible study records, including sponsor-entered facility fields for participating locations. Those fields are useful but not necessarily complete or consistent, as prior assessments of ClinicalTrials.gov data quality have shown [50, 51]. The Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS) provides a European registry, but this study did not establish a formal field-by-field comparison between CTIS and ClinicalTrials.gov; no claim of registry equivalence or absence is made.

We searched for interventional studies involving shoulder conditions, device interventions, and an industry lead sponsor, with study start dates between 2000 and 2026. We then retained investigations involving implantable shoulder devices. The industry restriction defined the population of interest as investigations used to generate pre-market or post-market evidence for these devices. Investigator-initiated studies were excluded because they represent a different sponsorship pathway and could change the institutional composition of the sample.

After eligibility screening, the final sample comprised 46 investigations. Their location data produced 335 organization-study links. After facility names were normalized and duplicate or related entries were resolved, these links represented 235 distinct organizations across 18 countries. Each organization was counted once, irrespective of the number of investigations in which it participated. The registry data and institutional sources used for classification were publicly available, and the analysis used no participant-level personal data. The applicable university or faculty determination on ethical review or exemption must be cited here before the thesis states that approval was not required [institutional ethics determination or policy citation required].

### 2.4.2 Classification of sites

ClinicalTrials.gov records each study location under the facility name entered by the sponsor. Facility names alone provide an unreliable basis for classification because naming conventions vary across countries and organizations. We therefore normalized spelling variants, former names, and multiple entries referring to the same organization. Where a registry entry named a research foundation, administrative entity, or sponsor, we resolved it to the clinical organization where the procedure was performed when sufficient public evidence was available.

The organization was the unit of analysis. Each organization was classified once, irrespective of the number of investigations, registered locations, or name variants associated with it. The registered facility name was used to locate the organization and was not treated as classification evidence by itself.

We classified each organization individually using public sources. We prioritized the organization’s official website, followed by public licensing records, recognized institutional directories, and academic publications. Classification considered inpatient capacity, ownership, and declared university or teaching affiliation. Organizational size was analyzed separately and did not determine the environment.

We used four mutually exclusive categories. The academic environment included university or teaching hospitals, university departments, and ambulatory facilities owned by these institutions. The non-academic hospital environment included hospitals with inpatient capacity and no declared university affiliation, together with ambulatory facilities belonging to a non-academic hospital system. The non-hospital environment included independent orthopedic practices and ambulatory centers outside hospital or university ownership. Exclusively orthopedic inpatient facilities owned by the surgeons who operated in them were also assigned to the non-hospital environment under a predefined exception. The unresolved category covered incomplete or ambiguous facility names, non-clinical entities, and organizations that could not be verified from public sources.

Of the 235 organizations, 227 were assigned to an environment. These comprised 86 academic organizations, 76 non-academic hospital organizations, and 65 non-hospital organizations. The remaining eight entries could not be linked to an identifiable clinical organization and were retained as unresolved. Section 3.4.4 and Table 3.3 present the resulting distribution.

### 2.4.3 Limitations of the classification analysis

Four limitations affect the interpretation of this analysis. First, one researcher performed the classification and evidence review. A second independent assessor was not available, so inter-rater agreement could not be measured. This limitation also applies to the other qualitative classifications performed in this work.

Second, the classifications relied on publicly available institutional information. Official websites were supplemented with licensing records, institutional directories, and academic publications when necessary. These sources allow the decisions to be verified, but they may not fully represent daily operational arrangements, informal affiliations, or recent organizational changes. The analysis therefore describes the documented institutional structure available at the time of verification.

Third, the sample was geographically concentrated in the United States, which accounted for 149 of the 235 organizations, or 63.4%. This concentration was stronger in the non-hospital group, where 60 of 65 organizations were located in the United States. The distribution observed in the full sample should therefore not be interpreted as representative of European investigation sites or of all regions contributing to medical device research.

Fourth, the organization was the unit of analysis, and each normalized organization contributed one observation. This approach prevented organizations appearing in several investigations from receiving additional weight. However, it also gave equal weight to organizations with different numbers of studies, locations, and enrolled participants. The results describe the distribution and characteristics of participating organizations. They do not measure their relative contribution to participant enrollment or investigation activity.

## 2.5 Construction of the framework

Before we can build the framework, we must decide what we are looking for. What the work has done so far is the first DSRM activity, which is understanding the problem. The second activity is the definition of objectives for a solution, where we establish what we want the framework to achieve. Once the CtQ domains are known, we can define the design requirements. A design requirement states what the framework must be able to do. It answers the question of what the artifact we are going to build must guarantee.

A finding became a design requirement when it met two conditions. The first condition is that the requirement must be new. If the ISO 14155, the MDR, or ICH E6(R3) already obliges the site to do something, the framework adds nothing, because the obligation exists. The framework is needed only where those instruments stay silent about the site. It also had to be actionable at site level, meaning that a site could satisfy it using resources it controls. A finding that failed the first condition was already covered by an existing obligation. A finding that failed the second belongs to the sponsor. Section 3.5 states the resulting requirements, numbered DR1 to DR12, and each one names the gap or the operational condition it comes from.

### 2.5.1 Derivation of the architecture

We used standards governing other organizations in the device conformity chain as structural references. ISO 13485 addresses management responsibility, planning, operational realization, and measurement and improvement [52]. ISO 14971 establishes a documented risk-management cycle from planning and analysis through evaluation, control, review, and post-production activity [53]. ISO 15189 remained a structural analogy informed by the accreditation literature, and we did not map its clauses to this taxonomy. These standards do not state a common four-part taxonomy. We synthesized four functions from them and from the requirements derived in this study: Governance, RBQM, Operational Controls, and Performance Oversight.

A site that operates these four functions operates the quality-system architecture that every other actor in the chain already has, and Section 3.2.1 explains why the site is the actor that lacks one. We then mapped each design requirement onto the pillar whose function it belongs to. Table 4.1 reports that mapping, and it lists each requirement against the component or components that answer it, so a reader can check the correspondence in both directions. No requirement fell outside the four pillars, which indicates that the taxonomy fits the problem. But it does not prove that another better taxonomy can exist.

### 2.5.2 Development of the operational instruments

Each pillar needed instruments that a site can actually operate, so we derived them from three inputs in a fixed order.

We started from the mechanisms used by the ICH guidelines to operationalize CtQ factors. ICH E8(R1) frames risk in terms of probability, detectability, and impact, while ICH E6(R3) uses likelihood, detectability, and impact for risk evaluation and requires pre-specified acceptable ranges, including trial-level quality tolerance limits where appropriate [5, 6]. We operationalized that logic into site-owned records; the guidelines do not prescribe the named site instruments developed here. The adaptation was informed by the CTTI QbD Documentation Tool and MDIC’s 2017 device case material. We then considered the operational conditions reported in Section 3.4 and what each demanded of a document. Finally, we adapted familiar clinical-research formats, including risk registers, delegation logs, monitoring plans, and corrective-action logs, changing their content and ownership to fit the site-level purpose.

Each instrument carries the requirement it answers. Section 4.8 reports the instrument set, and Appendices A through X present the templates.

## 2.6 Methodological limitations and validation status

Three limitations apply to the work as a whole. Section 2.4.3 already states the limitations specific to the registry analysis.

The literature review was narrative. It supports a map of the problem space. It does not support a claim that any body of work is absent from the field.

The framework has not been implemented at a site, and we have not tested it empirically. Its components derive from documented evidence and from stated requirements, which makes them traceable. That does not make them validated. Where this thesis describes what the framework is designed to enable, it describes a design intention. Any operational benefit remains an expectation until an implementation study measures it. Section 5.7 sets out what such a study would need to establish.
