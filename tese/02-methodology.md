# 2. Methodology

## 2.1 Design research

### 2.1.1 Design science research methodology

The objective of this thesis is to build a site-level quality framework that puts the principles of the governing guidelines into practice. The work followed the Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM) set out by Peffers et al. [49]. Design science research creates and evaluates artifacts that solve identified organizational problems, and DSRM organizes that work into six activities: problem identification and motivation, definition of the objectives for a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication.

This thesis reports four of the six activities. Chapter 1 and Sections 3.2 to 3.4 carry the problem identification and motivation, and Section 3.5 states the objectives for a solution as design requirements DR1 to DR12. Chapter 4 reports the design and development, and the thesis itself is the communication. Demonstration and evaluation were not carried out, and Section 2.6 states what follows from that.

Peffers et al. also state that the trigger of the research decides where in the sequence the work starts. The problem here was identified by reading the governing guidelines and finding gaps in what they assign to the investigational site, so the work starts at the first activity. The same paper recognizes four kinds of artifact, which are constructs, models, methods, and instantiations. Because the problem is that the instruments examined give the site no structured quality framework for surgical device investigations, the artifact is a model, together with the methods that put it to work.

DSRM also asks that the link between problem and solution stays visible. We kept it visible by tracing each component of the artifact to a design requirement and each requirement to its origin in Chapter 3. Table 4.1 reports the first link, and Table 3.4 reports the second.

### 2.1.2 Overview of the research process

The problem identification drew on three sources of evidence, starting with the analysis of the normative instruments, which produced the grid of domains on which the design rests. A narrative review of the literature added what the field has observed and proposed, and a characterization of sites in a public registry described where these investigations are conducted. A requirement supported by more than one of these sources therefore rests on more than one kind of evidence.

## 2.2 Analysis of the normative instruments

### 2.2.1 Instruments and their status

We used ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) as conceptual and design references, because they define QbD and CtQ factors and set out the principle of risk-proportionate quality. Their main scope is still medicinal products, so we took the device-specific obligations from two other instruments. ISO 14155:2026 is the GCP standard for device investigations and our main source of investigator-level requirements. The MDR supplies the European legal framework, including the traceability and safety reporting requirements specific to devices.

We also consulted ISO 13485, ISO 14971 with its guidance document ISO/TR 24971, and ISO 15189. None of them creates an obligation for the investigational site, and we used them as models of a documented quality system held by other organizations in the same regulatory and supply chain. ISO 15189 served as a structural analogy, because a medical laboratory and an investigational site both produce data on which an external party bases a decision.

### 2.2.2 Derivation of the site-level CtQ domains

We read the instruments from the most general to the most specific, starting with ICH E8(R1), then ICH E6(R3), and then the device-specific texts. The CtQ concept is developed in the ICH guidelines [5, 6]. A documentary search performed on 20 September 2026 found no occurrence of the exact phrases “CtQ” or “QbD” in ISO 14155:2026 or in the MDR, a result limited to the texts and search terms examined, which does not show that the related concepts are absent. Reading in this order gave us an explicit definition and its criteria before we reached the device texts, so the same frame could be applied to all of them.

ICH E8(R1) describes CtQ factors as “attributes of a study whose integrity is fundamental to the protection of study participants” and to reliable, interpretable results and decisions [5]. The guideline also names areas to consider when identifying CtQ factors, and we recorded the following 18 from that discussion:

1. Stakeholder engagement
2. Appropriate eligibility criteria
3. Adequacy of prior studies
4. Necessary data collection
5. Scientific study objectives
6. Well-defined endpoints
7. Meaningful control group
8. Bias minimization
9. Participant protection
10. Prespecified statistical analysis
11. Clear participant information
12. Critical data integrity
13. Staff competencies and training
14. Risk-proportionate monitoring
15. Study feasibility
16. Data monitoring committee
17. Adequate sample size, duration, and visit frequency
18. Transparent and timely reporting

These considerations guide the identification of CtQ factors from the position of the sponsor, as the party that designs the study. Our object was the site, so we used them where they applied and added four criteria that we drew from our reading of the guideline. The labels are ours, and the guideline does not group them in this way.

Proportionality holds that procedures should match the risks involved and the importance of the information collected. The criterion comes from ICH E8(R1), which does not ask for procedural perfection [5, 6].

Parsimony asks that the set of critical factors stay focused, and that data and activities serving neither participant protection nor the primary objectives be avoided [5].

Assigned responsibility allocates the identification and protection of critical factors to named parties, with qualified staff and adequate resources to support that allocation [5, 6].

Reliance on existing knowledge means that what is already known about the product, the condition, and the population should shape both the factors selected and the controls applied [5]. At trial level, the sponsor's quality management should draw on relevant prior knowledge, while the site has to ensure that its personnel are qualified and trained for their assigned activities [6].

When we traced these criteria into ICH E6(R3), proportionality reappeared in Annex 1 through risk evaluation and control. The sponsor is expected to define prespecified acceptable ranges for risks that may affect participant safety or the reliability of trial results, and quality tolerance limits are one trial-level example of such ranges [6]. This is a sponsor duty at trial level, and the guideline does not turn quality tolerance limits into an instrument the site must hold.

While reading ICH E6(R3) we recorded every attribute that introduced a CtQ concept, together with the supporting clause and excerpt, so that each candidate carried its source. We recorded separately the mechanisms that ICH E6(R3) prescribes for putting CtQ factors into practice, and Chapter 4 draws on them in the design of the instruments.

We applied the same procedure, unchanged, to ISO 14155 and to MDR Annex XV, so that the three readings could be compared. We then merged the three sets into a single matrix and compared and clustered the attributes, to see how each instrument presents an attribute and how consistently the instruments treat it as critical.

### 2.2.3 The clause-level comparison

We compared the attributes clause by clause. For each attribute we recorded the obligation each instrument creates and the party it binds, together with the clause it comes from, and we then sorted the attributes into three categories. An attribute is strongly harmonized when all three instruments create an equivalent obligation on the same party. It is partly harmonized when the instruments agree on the principle but bind a different party or use different terms. It is unaddressed at site level when none of the instruments gives the site an obligation that matches the attribute, either because the obligation falls on the sponsor or because no instrument assigns it to any party in operational form. We call an attribute in this third category a gap.

Convergence shows that an attribute matters, but it does not show that the attribute belongs in a site-level framework. We therefore filtered the matrix against three criteria. The locus of responsibility excluded attributes that the sponsor controls and the site does not, and it kept an attribute when the responsibility is formally the sponsor's but site execution decides whether it is met. The scope of the thesis excluded attributes that would push the work past its declared boundary or add complexity out of proportion to what they contribute. Material relevance to the surgical device context was the last criterion.

We did not use convergence alone as the selection rule, because several attributes appear in ISO 14155 and the MDR but not in ICH E6(R3), which is expected from a guideline written for medicinal products.

We grouped the remaining attributes by the operational area of a surgical investigation they relate to, and each group became a candidate domain. For example, ICH E6(R3) yields an attribute on investigational product management, which covers storage, dispensing to an eligible participant, accountability, and expiry. ISO 14155 and the MDR yield further attributes on unit-level traceability that have no counterpart in ICH E6(R3). All of them belong to the same operational area, so they were grouped into one domain, device management and traceability.

We then tested each group against the four criteria drawn from ICH E8(R1), to decide whether it qualified as a CtQ area. The test produced twelve domains, which Section 3.3 reports and on which Section 3.5 builds the design requirements.

## 2.3 Literature review

### 2.3.1 Scope and approach

The review was narrative and did not follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement, so it is not an exhaustive account of the field. Its purpose was to inform a design process, and a narrative review suits the mapping of a problem space and the location of gaps in it. It gives up exhaustiveness, but it keeps the reading focused, which mattered here because the relevant material sits in five fields: quality management in clinical research, SQA in trials, the methodology of surgical trials, the clinical literature on the shoulder, and the performance and readiness of investigational sites.

We searched Scopus, PubMed, OpenAlex, and Google Scholar and followed citations from the articles retrieved. We gave priority to recent work, because the regulatory texts changed in the last few years, with ICH E6(R3) in 2025 and ISO 14155:2026 in 2026. Two kinds of source were exempt from that priority: methodological work whose content does not date, such as the surgical trials literature on learning and clustering effects, and normative instruments and guidance documents, which we used in the version in force at the time of writing.

### 2.3.2 How the material was analyzed

We organized the reading around one question derived from the QbD and risk-proportionate quality principles of ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3). The question was what a site would need to put those principles into its own operational processes. We took notes throughout on what is established, where sources disagree, and where evidence is absent, and we then grouped the recurring themes. Section 3.2 cites this literature where it supports or qualifies a regulatory gap.

Because the search was not systematic, any absence of published work reported here is an outcome of this review and does not prove that no such work exists. We also extended the material beyond peer-reviewed publications to regulatory, standards, and industry guidance. The main interpretive sources were the CTTI QbD recommendations with the QbD Documentation Tool, and the 2016 MDIC device-specific supplement on CtQ factors [33, 34].

### 2.3.3 Analysis of existing frameworks

The review also had to establish what the field has already built, so that the framework would add something new or improve on what exists. For every framework, consensus statement, and measurement instrument we identified, we recorded the level at which it is defined, the party that operates it, the party it is applied to, and the clinical field it was developed in. Recording these four components showed a pattern that a summary of content would have hidden, because instruments that look similar in subject matter differ in who holds them.

We then compared the instruments closest to the framework with the framework itself, component by component, and recorded whether each component has a direct counterpart, a partial counterpart operating at a different level, or no counterpart. Section 5.2 reports that comparison and states what the framework adds.

## 2.4 Registry analysis of investigational site characteristics

The relevance of the framework depends on the conditions of the sites that would use it, so the work needed a description of where these investigations are conducted and what research structure those sites have. We found no published source describing that distribution for shoulder device investigations, so we produced the characterization from registry data, and Section 3.4.4 reports the results.

### 2.4.1 Data source and search

We retrieved study records through the public ClinicalTrials.gov API v2 (documentation and records accessed 20 September 2026). We chose this source because it provides structured, publicly accessible study records, including the facility fields that sponsors enter for participating locations. Those fields are useful but not always complete or consistent, as earlier assessments of ClinicalTrials.gov data quality have shown [50, 51]. The Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS) is the European registry, but this study did not compare CTIS and ClinicalTrials.gov field by field and makes no claim about their equivalence.

We searched for interventional studies of shoulder conditions with a device intervention and an industry lead sponsor, with start dates between 2000 and 2026, and then kept the investigations of implantable shoulder devices. The industry restriction defined the population of interest as investigations that generate pre-market or post-market evidence for these devices. Investigator-initiated studies were excluded, because they follow a different sponsorship pathway and could change the institutional composition of the sample.

After eligibility screening, the final sample comprised 46 investigations. Their location data produced 335 organization-study links, and after facility names were normalized and duplicate or related entries resolved, these links represented 235 distinct organizations in 18 countries. Each organization was counted once, whatever the number of investigations it took part in. The registry data and the institutional sources used for classification were publicly available, and the analysis used no participant-level personal data.

### 2.4.2 Classification of sites

ClinicalTrials.gov records each study location under the facility name entered by the sponsor. Facility names alone are an unreliable basis for classification, because naming conventions vary across countries and organizations, so we normalized spelling variants, former names, and multiple entries referring to the same organization. Where an entry named a research foundation, an administrative entity, or the sponsor, we resolved it to the clinical organization where the procedure was performed whenever public evidence allowed it.

The organization was the unit of analysis, and each organization was classified once, whatever the number of investigations, registered locations, or name variants associated with it. The registered facility name was used to locate the organization and was not treated as classification evidence by itself.

We classified each organization from public sources, giving priority to the official website of the organization, followed by public licensing records, recognized institutional directories, and academic publications. The classification considered inpatient capacity and ownership, together with any declared university or teaching affiliation.

We assigned each organization to one of four mutually exclusive categories. The academic environment included university or teaching hospitals, university departments, and ambulatory facilities owned by these institutions. The nonacademic hospital environment included hospitals with inpatient capacity and no declared university affiliation, together with ambulatory facilities belonging to a nonacademic hospital system. The nonhospital environment included independent orthopedic practices and ambulatory centers outside hospital or university ownership. Exclusively orthopedic inpatient facilities owned by the surgeons who operate in them were also assigned to the nonhospital environment, under an exception defined before classification. The unresolved category covered incomplete or ambiguous facility names, nonclinical entities, and organizations that could not be verified from public sources.

Of the 235 organizations, 227 were assigned to an environment, of which 86 were academic, 76 nonacademic hospital, and 65 nonhospital organizations. The remaining eight entries could not be linked to an identifiable clinical organization and stayed unresolved. Section 3.4.4 and Table 3.3 present the resulting distribution.

### 2.4.3 Limitations of the classification analysis

One researcher performed the classification and the evidence review. No second independent assessor was available, so interrater agreement could not be measured, and the same limitation applies to the other qualitative classifications in this work.

The classifications relied on publicly available institutional information. Official websites were supplemented with licensing records, institutional directories, and academic publications when necessary, and these sources allow each decision to be checked. They may not show daily operational arrangements, informal affiliations, or recent organizational changes, so the analysis describes the documented institutional structure at the time of verification.

The sample was concentrated in the United States, which accounted for 149 of the 235 organizations (63.4%), and the concentration was stronger in the nonhospital group, where 60 of the 65 organizations were in the United States. The distribution in the full sample therefore does not represent European investigation sites or all the regions that contribute to medical device research.

Each normalized organization contributed one observation. This prevented organizations that appear in several investigations from receiving extra weight, but it also gave equal weight to organizations with different numbers of studies, locations, and enrolled participants. The results describe the distribution and characteristics of the participating organizations and do not measure their share of enrollment or investigation activity.

## 2.5 Construction of the framework

The analysis described so far belongs to the first DSRM activity, the identification of the problem. The second activity defines the objectives for a solution, which here take the form of design requirements. A design requirement states what the framework must be able to do once the CtQ domains are known, and each component of the framework can later be checked against it.

A finding became a design requirement when it met two conditions. It had to name something the site must be able to do that the three instruments compared do not turn into a site-level procedure, whether because the obligation falls on the sponsor, because the site holds the obligation without an operational means to meet it, or because no instrument assigns it at all. It also had to be actionable at site level, meaning that a site could meet it with resources it controls. A finding that failed the first condition was already covered in operational form, and a finding that failed the second belongs to the sponsor. Most requirements therefore organize duties the site already holds, or duties that depend on site execution, into something the site can run. One requirement, DR3 on the surgical learning curve, answers a gap for which we found no counterpart obligation in the instruments examined. Section 3.5 states the resulting requirements, DR1 to DR12, and each one names its origin.

### 2.5.1 Derivation of the pillars

We used standards that govern other organizations in the device conformity chain as structural references. ISO 13485 organizes the quality system of a manufacturer from management responsibility to measurement and improvement [52], and ISO 14971 sets a documented risk-management cycle from planning to post-production activity [53]. ISO 14155:2026 places clinical quality management on the sponsor in Clause 9.1 [8]. These standards do not state a common four-part taxonomy. We synthesized four functions from the clauses they share and from the requirements derived in this study, and these became the four pillars of the framework: Governance, Risk Management, Operational Controls, and Performance Oversight. Section 4.1 gives the clause mapping behind each pillar. ISO 15189 remained a structural analogy, and we did not map its clauses.

We then mapped each design requirement onto the pillar whose function it belongs to. Table 4.1 reports the mapping and lists each requirement against the components that answer it, so the correspondence can be checked in both directions. All twelve requirements fell inside one of the four pillars. Because the pillars were synthesized partly from the requirements themselves, this fit follows from the construction and does not show that the four pillars are the only possible structure.

### 2.5.2 Development of the operational instruments

Each pillar needed instruments that a site can operate, and we derived them from three inputs in a fixed order. We started from the mechanisms the ICH guidelines use to put CtQ factors into practice. Both guidelines evaluate a risk by its likelihood and its impact and by how easily it can be detected, and ICH E6(R3) also asks for prespecified acceptable ranges, including trial-level quality tolerance limits where appropriate [5, 6]. We turned that logic into records held by the site, which the guidelines do not prescribe, and the CTTI QbD Documentation Tool and the 2016 MDIC device material informed the adaptation. We then considered the operational conditions reported in Section 3.4 and what each one demands of a document. Finally, we adapted familiar clinical-research formats, such as risk registers and delegation logs, and changed their content and ownership to fit the site-level purpose.

Each instrument carries the requirement it answers. Section 4.8 reports the instrument set, and Appendices A to X present the templates.

## 2.6 Methodological limitations and validation status

Section 2.4.3 states the limitations specific to the registry analysis, and three others apply to the work as a whole. The literature review was narrative, so it supports a map of the problem space and cannot show that a body of work is absent from the field. The qualitative steps of the analysis, from the clause comparison to the derivation of the requirements, were carried out by one researcher without a second assessor, so we do not know how far an independent reading would agree with them.

The framework has not been implemented at a site or tested empirically. Its components are traceable to documented evidence and to stated requirements, but traceability does not make them validated. Where this thesis describes what the framework is designed to enable, it describes a design intention, and any operational benefit remains an expectation until an implementation study measures it. Section 5.7 sets out what such a study would need to establish.
