# 2. Methodology

## 2.1 Design research

### 2.1.1 Design science research methodology

As already stated, the objective of this thesis is to build a framework of quality that supports the principles required by the guidelines. To achieve this, our work used an adapted Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM), based on Peffers et al. [49]. Design science research (DSR) creates and evaluates artifacts that solve identified organizational problems. DSRM organizes that work into six activities: problem identification and motivation, definition of the objectives for a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication.

Four of those activities are reported here. Chapter 1 and Sections 3.2 and 3.4 carry the problem identification and motivation. Section 3.5 states the objectives for a solution, as design requirements DR1 to DR12. Chapter 4 reports the design and development, and this thesis is the communication. The remaining two activities, demonstration and evaluation, were not undertaken.

Peffers et al. also note that a DSRM study can enter the sequence at different activities, depending on what triggers the research, and that a study triggered by a problem, which they call problem-centered initiation, starts at the first activity. This thesis is problem-centered. The difficulty of managing quality at the site is already recognized in the literature, which describes data-quality management at sites as often reactive and standardized site practices as uncommon [42, 45], together with measures for surgical trials that are rarely carried through conduct [39]. As Sections 1.1 and 1.3 describe, the recent guidelines place more of the responsibility for quality on the investigator and the site team, but they do not describe how a site should organize itself to carry it. Since the problem is the absence of a structured, site-level quality management framework for surgical device investigations, the artifact is a model together with the methods that put it to work.

DSRM also asks that the link between problem and solution stay visible. We kept it visible by tracing each major component of the framework to the design requirement it answers, and each requirement to the regulatory gap, CtQ domain, or operating condition that motivated it. Section 3.5 states the requirements and Table 3.4 traces each one to its origin, while Table 4.1 maps the requirements onto the components that answer them and Figure 4.5 does the same for the 24 instruments.

### 2.1.2 Overview of the research process

To support the problem identification and motivation step we used three sources of evidence to feed the design. The first is an analysis of the normative instruments that govern device investigations. The second is a review of the literature. The third is a characterization of the setting where these investigations are conducted.

The regulatory analysis came first and provided the basis on which the design was going to be built. The instruments showed what is required, the literature showed what the field has observed and proposed, and the registry analysis showed where the work is done and under what conditions.

For the definition of the objectives, we derived the site-level CtQ domains from the requirements recorded in the first activity and then formulated the design requirements that the framework must attend. For design and development, we built the pillars and the operational instruments of the framework. Section 5.7 sets out the limitations of each step and the validation status of the framework, and this thesis is the communication of the work.

## 2.2 Problem identification

### 2.2.1 Normative instruments

We used ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) as conceptual and design references since they are the main source for the definition of QbD, CtQ, and risk-proportionate quality principles. Although very important their main focus is still drug investigational products, so we needed to go after the device-specific obligations from two other instruments. ISO 14155:2026 is the GCP standard for these investigations and our main source of investigator-level requirements. The MDR supplies the European legal framework, including the traceability and safety reporting requirements specific to devices.

We consulted the latest version of three further standards: ISO 13485:2016, ISO 14971:2019 with its guidance document ISO/TR 24971:2020, and ISO 15189:2022. None of them creates an obligation for the investigational site. We use them as models of a documented quality system elsewhere in the same regulatory and supply chain.

### 2.2.2 Reading and comparison of the normative instruments

We read ICH E8 (R1), ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and the MDR with the objective to identify the responsibilities that the instruments declare for the site or sponsor, and the quality factors on which the direct action of the site is already known and recognized to influence. The matrix was built from ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and the MDR. ICH E8(R1) did not enter the matrix, and we used it as a reference to check the entries and the questions raised during the comparison. In each of the three texts we recorded every requirement that concerns participant protection, reliability of the results or procedural consistency. Each entry was associated with the clause, section or chapter it comes from, with the explicit text of that clause. We also recorded the obligation that the clause creates and the party responsible for it.

Requirements that describe how quality is to be controlled, such as periodic risk review or quality tolerance limits in ICH E6(R3), went into a separate list, which Section 2.5.2 uses in the design of the instruments.

We merged the three sets of entries into a single matrix and compared the requirements at each instrument. Each one was sorted into one of three categories. A requirement is strongly harmonized when all three instruments create an equivalent obligation on the same party. Partially harmonized when the instruments agree on the principle but bind different parties or use different terms. It is unaddressed at site level when none of the instruments gives the site an obligation that matches the requirement, either because the obligation falls on the sponsor or because no instrument assigns it to any party in operational form. We call a requirement in this third category a gap.

Requirements found in only some of the instruments were classified case by case. A requirement that is absent from ICH E6(R3) only because it is specific to medical devices, and that both device instruments address, was classified as strongly harmonized. A requirement that one device instrument requires and the other does not was also classified as a gap, as with an explicit MDR requirement that ISO 14155:2026 does not contain, or a factor that ISO 14155:2026 treats as critical and the MDR does not require.

### 2.2.3 Literature review

The literature relevant to this work is spread across five fields: quality management in clinical research, SQA in trials, the methodology of surgical trials, the clinical literature on the shoulder, and the performance measurement of investigational sites. We reviewed it narratively so that the review would not drift toward other questions. The review mapped the problem already described in these fields, and it also showed gaps reported by other authors that our reading of the instruments had missed and allowed us to test our first findings against published work.

We used literature search engines such as Scopus, PubMed, OpenAlex and Google Scholar to find papers related to those fields and tracked citations from the articles we retrieved. We gave priority to recent work, because the regulatory environment has changed substantially in the last few years, although we kept methodological work whose content does not date, which for example applies to the surgical trials literature on learning and clustering effects. We extended the material beyond peer-reviewed publications to regulatory and industry guidance and to standards. The main interpretive sources were the CTTI QbD recommendations with the QbD Documentation Tool, and the 2016 MDIC device-specific supplement on CtQ factors [33, 34].

We organized the reading around a main question: what a site would need to put the QbD and risk-proportionate quality principles of ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) into its own operational processes. For each source we noted what it establishes, and where sources disagree or evidence is absent, and we then grouped the recurring themes.

### 2.2.4 Analysis of existing frameworks

The review had a second function beyond mapping the problem. It had to establish what the field has already built, so that the framework could generate something new or improve what already exists.

For every framework, consensus statement, and measurement instrument we identified, we recorded four components. These are the level at which it is defined, the party that operates it, the party it is applied to, and the clinical field it was developed in. Recording these components made visible a pattern that a summary of content would have hidden. Instruments that look similar in subject matter differ in who holds them.

We then compared each instrument with the framework under construction, component by component. For each pairing we recorded one of three outcomes: a component with a direct counterpart, a component with a partial counterpart operating at a different level, and a component with no counterpart.

## 2.3 Registry analysis of investigational site characteristics

The framework's relevance and functionality depend on the conditions and limitations of the site, so the work needed a description of where and how well prepared the sites were for these investigations. We found no published source establishing that distribution, so we produced the characterization ourselves from registry data available at public sources.

### 2.3.1 Data source and search

We first searched the European database on medical devices (Eudamed), set up under the MDR, the applications for clinical investigations are submitted through its electronic system on clinical investigations (Articles 33 and 73). In the application, the sponsor must identify each investigational site and the principal investigator at each one (Annex XV, Chapter II, Section 3.1.3) [10]. Eudamed did not return site-level data for the investigations searched, so we retrieved study records from ClinicalTrials.gov through its public application programming interface (API) v2, with records accessed on 2 May 2026. ClinicalTrials.gov provides structured, publicly accessible records, including the facility fields that sponsors enter for participating locations. Those fields are not always complete or consistent, as earlier assessments of ClinicalTrials.gov data quality have shown [50, 51].

We searched for interventional studies involving shoulder conditions, device interventions, and an industry lead sponsor, with study start dates between 2000 and 2026. We then retained investigations involving implantable shoulder devices. The site record was the unit of analysis, so an organization was counted once for each investigation in which it took part, and the distribution reflects how often each environment hosts investigational activity. The registry data and the institutional sources used for classification were publicly available, and the analysis used no participant-level personal data.

### 2.3.2 Classification of sites

ClinicalTrials.gov records each study location under the facility name entered by the sponsor. Facility names alone provide an unreliable basis for classification because naming conventions vary across countries and organizations. We therefore normalized spelling variants, former names, and multiple entries referring to the same organization.

We classified each organization from public sources, giving priority to its official website and then to public licensing records and recognized institutional directories, with academic publications as the last source. The classification considered inpatient capacity and ownership, together with any declared university or teaching affiliation, and assigned each organization to one of four categories:

1.  **Academic.** University or teaching hospitals and university departments, together with the ambulatory facilities these institutions own.

2.  **Nonacademic hospital.** Hospitals with inpatient capacity and no declared university affiliation, together with ambulatory facilities belonging to a nonacademic hospital system.

3.  **Private clinics.** Independent orthopedic practices and ambulatory centers outside hospital or university ownership. Exclusively orthopedic inpatient facilities owned by the surgeons who operate in them were also assigned here.

4.  **Unresolved.** Entries with incomplete or ambiguous facility names or naming nonclinical entities, together with organizations that could not be verified from public sources.

## 2.4 Definition of the objectives of a solution

### 2.4.1 Selection criteria for site-level CtQ factors

We used CtQ factors to decide which of the requirements recorded in Section 2.2.2 a site-level framework has to control. The test came from ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3), the only instruments examined that define CtQ factors and describe how to identify them [5, 6].

ICH E8(R1), in Section 3.2, describes CtQ factors as “attributes of a study whose integrity is fundamental to the protection of study participants, the reliability and interpretability of the study results, and the decisions made based on the study results” [5]. Its Section 7 lists 18 considerations for identifying them, written from the position of the sponsor as the party that designs the study. But, because our object was the site, we used four criteria that apply to any requirement, independent of the party responsible for it. Each of them is a principle already stated in the ICH guidelines, and ICH E6(R3) states three of them among its principles (Principles 4, 7, and 10) [5, 6]. We selected them as a joint test for site-level domains and gave them short labels for reference.

1\. Proportionality. Study procedures and controls are scaled to the risks of the study and to the importance of the data they produce (ICH E8(R1), Section 3.2; ICH E6(R3), Principle 7) [5, 6].

2\. Parsimony. The set of critical factors stays focused. Data and activities that serve neither participant protection nor the primary objectives are left out (ICH E8(R1), Sections 3.2 and 3.3.2) [5].

3\. Assigned responsibility. The identification and protection of critical factors are allocated to defined parties, with qualified staff and sufficient resources. At the site, the investigator confirms that each person given a delegated activity is qualified for it (ICH E6(R3), Principle 10 and Sections 2.3.2 and 3.2 to 3.4) [5, 6].

4\. Reliance on existing knowledge. What is known about the product and the condition, and about the population for which the product is intended, determines which factors are treated as critical and which controls apply, and both are revised as knowledge grows during the study (ICH E8(R1), Section 3.2; ICH E6(R3), Principle 4 and Section 3.10.1.5) [5, 6].

### 2.4.2 Derivation of the site-level CtQ domains

We built the domains from the matrix of Section 2.2.2 in three steps. The first step removed the requirements that a site-level framework does not need to cover, using three filters:

1.  Responsibility. We removed requirements that only the sponsor controls. We kept a requirement when the duty formally belongs to the sponsor but the work done at the site decides whether it is met.

2.  Scope. We removed requirements that fall outside the limits of this thesis, and requirements that would add more complexity than value.

3.  Relevance. We removed requirements that do not apply to a surgical device investigation.

In the second step, we grouped the remaining requirements by the operational area of a surgical investigation to which they relate, and each group became a candidate domain. Requirements on the storage and dispensing of the investigational product and accountability, for example, were grouped with requirements on unit-level traceability, because all of them concern the handling of the device.

In the third step, we tested each candidate domain against the four criteria of Section 2.4.1, applied together as a single judgment. For each domain we assessed whether a failure would affect participant protection or the reliability of the results to a meaningful degree, weighing its likelihood against its consequences. We also assessed whether control of the domain demands an assigned responsibility, and to which party it should be assigned. Finally, we checked whether the issue had already been identified, in the literature, together with the risks and difficulties associated with it and any response or solution already available.

### 2.4.3 Formulation of the design requirements

We formulated the design requirements from a structured analysis of the operational needs of investigational sites. The analysis drew on the regulatory and ethical expectations that apply to these sites and on the literature, including the evidence on how sites manage quality and the difficulties they report. The absence of prescriptive operational guidance in the regulatory documents was not treated as a gap. We used these sources to understand the responsibilities and constraints that shape trial conduct at the site, and how the processes involved depend on each other.

From this analysis we derived candidate requirements wherever we found an operational need, or a recurring difficulty in current practice, that the framework could help the site address. Each candidate was assessed on two grounds: how relevant it is to site activities and how important the underlying need is, and whether the site can act on it, within its own control or in coordination with the sponsor and other parties to the trial. We kept regulatory obligations apart from design requirements throughout. An obligation defines what is expected of trial conduct, while a design requirement specifies what the framework should provide to help the site meet that expectation. Each requirement was written as an actionable statement of what the framework should enable, independent of any particular solution, and linked to the evidence and reasoning that support it.

## 2.5 Design and development

### 2.5.1 Derivation of the pillars

We derived the pillars from the clause structure of three standards that govern other organizations in the device conformity chain: ISO 13485:2016 [52], ISO 14971:2019 [53], and ISO 15189:2022, which sets requirements for medical laboratories [54]. None of them applies to the investigational site. We read the main clauses of each standard, grouped them by the function they serve in the organization, and synthesized four pillars from these groups and from the design requirements in Section 3.5: Governance, Risk Management, Operational Controls, and Performance Oversight. ISO 13485 applies risk management across its other processes (Clauses 4.1.2 and 7.1), whereas ISO 14971 sets it out as a documented cycle of its own, so we made risk management a separate pillar. We then assigned each design requirement to the pillar whose function it serves. Section 4.1 gives the clauses behind each pillar, and Table 4.1 lists each requirement with the components that answer it.

### 2.5.2 Development of the operational instruments

Each pillar needed instruments that a site can actually operate, so we derived them from three inputs in a fixed order.

We started from the mechanisms used by the ICH guidelines to operationalize CtQ factors. ICH E8(R1) frames risk in terms of probability, detectability, and impact, while ICH E6(R3) uses likelihood, detectability, and impact for risk evaluation and requires pre-specified acceptable ranges, including trial-level quality tolerance limits where appropriate [5, 6]. We operationalized that logic into site-owned records; the guidelines do not prescribe the named site instruments developed here. The adaptation was informed by the CTTI QbD Documentation Tool and MDIC’s 2016 device case material. We then considered the operational conditions reported in Section 3.4 and what each demanded of a document. Finally, we adapted familiar clinical-research formats, including risk registers, delegation logs, monitoring plans, and corrective-action logs, changing their content and ownership to fit the site-level purpose.

Each instrument carries the requirement it answers. Section 4.8 reports the instrument set, and Appendices A through X present the templates.
