# 4. Results: The Quality Framework

## 4.1 Overview and design rationale

Chapter 3 closed the second DSRM activity with the design requirements DR1 to DR12. This chapter reports the third activity, design and development, and describes each component of the framework in enough detail for a site to operate it.

The model is called the Site-Centered Operational Quality Management Framework. It has four pillars, and each pillar holds a set of instruments. A lifecycle decides when each instrument is used and a role structure decides who operates it, while a scalability model decides how deeply it is implemented given the structure and capacity of the site.

The four pillars are Governance, Risk Management, Operational Controls, and Performance Oversight. They are a synthesis made for this framework, and no single standard states them as a taxonomy. They map recurring functions across ISO 13485:2016, ISO 14971:2019, ISO 14155:2026, and the ICH guidance. Governance appears in ISO 13485 Clause 5, ISO 14971 Clause 4, and ISO 14155 Clause 9.1. Risk management appears in ISO 13485 Clause 7.1, ISO 14971 Clauses 5 to 7, and ISO 14155 Clauses 6.2.1 to 6.2.4. Operational controls appear in ISO 13485 Clauses 7 and 7.5 and in ISO 14155 Clause 7, while performance oversight appears in ISO 13485 Clauses 8, 8.2.4, 8.4, and 8.5, ISO 14971 Clauses 9 and 10, and ISO 14155 Clause 9.1. Because these functions are already familiar from the device quality system, the framework is easier to interpret for people who work with those standards.

Table 4.1 maps the twelve design requirements to the components that answer them, and each section of this chapter returns to that mapping. The framework was designed for the target center described in Section 3.4.5, a private center that runs investigations recurrently alongside surgical care, and that setting explains the form the instruments take. The components are nonetheless described as functions, so that a center with a different staffing structure can map its own posts onto them.

## 4.2 Pillar 1: Governance

Governance defines the local authority structure through which regulatory accountability is exercised at the site. The quality of a surgical device investigation depends on technical execution by the surgeon and on decisions taken during the procedure, so the pillar controls clinical authority and delegated execution in a documented and risk-proportionate way, together with the procedural competence and device training of the people involved.

The regulatory basis sits in two places. ICH E6(R3) expects investigator oversight to be proportionate to the nature of the delegated activities, the importance of the data collected, and the risks to participant safety and data reliability. ISO 14155:2026 keeps responsibility for the investigation with the PI under Clause 10.1, even when activities are delegated, and requires the qualification of the investigator under Clause 10.2 and of the site under Clause 10.3.

The pillar has four components: the oversight model, the delegation structure with its roles, surgical qualification, and device training documentation. The oversight model defines how authority is exercised, and the other three define who may act under it. The qualification decision belongs to this pillar, while the observation of how a qualified surgeon performs afterward belongs to Risk Management (Section 4.3.2). Seven instruments carry the pillar, and the sections below name each one where it is described.

### 4.2.1 The oversight model

During the design we developed three analytic alternatives for allocating oversight at site level. They are not an empirically established taxonomy. They represent recurrent ways in which site functions may be distributed under different constraints of staffing, capacity, and existing organization. Published descriptions of trial-site operations confirm that these functions must be covered, but they do not divide them into the same three models. We therefore present the alternatives as design choices and explain why the framework adopts the third.

In a strongly investigator-centered model, the PI intervenes directly in most critical activities and personally reviews most documentary and operational workflows. It gives an impression of intensive supervision, but it becomes difficult to sustain as activity expands. Site PIs already carry scientific, regulatory, and administrative duties, and their contribution is often under-recognized [72]. Administrative review then consumes the attention needed where clinical judgment cannot be replaced, such as confirming surgical appropriateness, interpreting intraoperative deviations, or assessing whether a complication is related to the investigational device. The model also scales poorly across the operating room, radiology, functional follow-up, and implant accountability.

In a coordinator-led model, daily management is delegated to the study coordinator while the investigator keeps final authority. The model is operationally plausible, because the clinical research coordinator is a core site function [73], and ICH E6(R3) permits delegation while the investigator keeps ultimate responsibility and proportionate oversight [6]. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance on supervisory responsibilities also emphasizes appropriate delegation, staff qualification, and investigator oversight [74]. The weakness of this model lies in nominal oversight, which appears when no defined review structure, periodicity, or escalation pathway states what requires direct investigator involvement. In surgical investigations, those points include borderline eligibility decisions, unplanned intraoperative changes, and component substitutions.

The framework adopts a third arrangement, a tiered and risk-adjusted model of PI oversight recorded in the PI Oversight Template (POT). Investigator involvement is graded by task criticality and calibrated to the risk each task poses to participant safety and data reliability. Oversight is concentrated on the processes, decisions, and data flows that can affect participant protection and study validity, which applies the CtQ logic of ICH E8(R1) to the allocation of the investigator's own attention. Five mechanisms put this principle into practice.

The first mechanism is a schedule, set in advance for each type of activity, of what requires the investigator's own judgment and what can be reviewed after the fact without loss. The line moves with the device, the procedure, and the experience of the team. If it is set too low, the investigator returns to the first model, and if it is set too high, oversight becomes a formality that produces no real review.

The second mechanism grades delegated activity by its stakes. Criticality is not a fixed property of a task. Coordinating a routine visit is administrative until a scheduling choice places a follow-up outside the protocol window, and recording an intraoperative note is clerical until the note is what later reconstructs a disputed complication. The grading has to anticipate which routine activities carry this kind of latent significance, so a scheme copied from another site will miss the ones specific to this investigation and this team.

The third mechanism places formal review points, recorded in the Milestone Review Log (MRL). Reviewing too early misses problems that only appear once a procedure or a follow-up window has occurred, and reviewing too late lets a correctable pattern continue through several more cases. The framework places the points at procedural implementation, the immediate postoperative period, the approach to endpoint assessment, and close-out, which reflects the risk profile of a shoulder implant investigation and would not transfer unchanged to a different device or procedure.

The fourth mechanism is an event-triggered escalation pathway, which requires investigator notification regardless of the routine review cadence. Some events threaten participant safety or causal interpretability immediately and have to reach the investigator before the next scheduled review.

The fifth mechanism places this review in the recurring rhythm of the site through scheduled oversight meetings, so it does not depend on ad hoc contact. It transposes to the site the structured, consensus-based review that ISO 14155:2026 uses at sponsor level under Clause 6.12, together with the multidisciplinary review of risk that the EMA reflection paper describes [1]. No study has yet shown that site-level forums of this kind improve oversight. They are also harder to sustain in a Type B center, where the people who attend the meeting are the same people who do the clinical and administrative work the meeting reviews.

These five mechanisms answer DR1 at the level of authority. They also carry part of DR10, because an oversight model that is written down, scheduled, and evidenced leaves a record that survives the departure of the people who operated it.

### 4.2.2 Delegation, roles, and functions

The delegation structure makes the oversight model operable, and the relationship runs in both directions. Without designated functions and training-linked release, a well-designed oversight model has no clear holders, and a strong delegation structure in turn makes escalation thresholds meaningful and lets the investigator concentrate attention where clinical judgment is needed.

ICH E6(R3) allows the investigator to delegate trial-related activities while keeping ultimate responsibility, and it requires oversight of delegated persons in proportion to the nature of the activity, the importance of the data, and the risks involved. Delegated personnel must be qualified and adequately informed about the protocol and the investigational product. ISO 14155:2026 governs the other members of the investigation site team under Clause 7.6, which requires documented training before assignments begin, together with a record of names, initials, signatures, functions, and designated authorizations.

The conventional delegation log is mainly a record of who performs which trial-related activity. ICH E6(R3) Section 2.3.3 requires documentation proportionate to the significance of the activity, but it does not require a criticality code or evidence of competence in the same record. Delegation records are nevertheless subject to direct inspection, and the investigator remains accountable for delegated work [74, 75]. The framework therefore replaces the plain log with the Criticality-Coded Delegation Matrix (CCDM), which is task-based and competency-gated, kept under version control, and linked to the review intensity defined in the POT. The task-based property matters most, because a research nurse may be entirely appropriate for coordinating visits and maintaining screening logs and still not be the right person to resolve a clinically meaningful discrepancy in operative documentation.

The framework defines a minimum set of six functions that reflects how orthopedic implant investigations are run.

**The PI** is accountable for conduct at the site and for the appropriateness of every delegated activity. Delegation transfers execution, and responsibility stays with the investigator.

**The study coordinator,** or the research nurse where the site uses that title, is the principal operational integrator. The function coordinates consent logistics and study visits and carries the flow of documents and the escalation of issues across the site. Its limit is clinical, so a coordinator does not decide eligibility, interpret safety information, or classify a PD.

**Operating room staff** prepare the investigational device for use and apply the chain-of-custody controls. They record the intraoperative procedural data and complete the procedural source records, and they hold no authority over clinical decisions.

**The radiology liaison** supports adherence to the imaging protocol and its windows and keeps image acquisition and transfer technically consistent. Control stops at technical quality, and where central reading applies, endpoint adjudication is not a site-level function.

**The functional assessor,** in many sites a physiotherapist, conducts ROM measurement and the PROM workflows wherever those activities are critical to endpoint quality. The function administers assessments by a defined method and does not interpret what an endpoint means.

**The data QA** function manages query flow and monitors the completeness of critical data. It identifies discrepancies, and a discrepancy that is clinically meaningful goes to the PI for resolution.

A site does not need a separate post for each function, and in a Type B center one person often holds several of them. The framework therefore requires each function to be assigned to a named individual and to have a named substitute, whatever the number of people involved, so that no CtQ function depends on one person alone. The Responsibility Assignment Matrix (RACI) records, for each activity, who is responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed, together with the backup and the limit of authority of each holder.

### 4.2.3 Surgeon qualification and continued authorization

The sponsor and the site share the control of which surgeons may perform study procedures throughout the investigation. The sponsor normally defines the minimum entry criteria through the CIP, the training requirements, and the study risk assessment, and during site qualification it reviews the available evidence and confirms whether the site meets those criteria. The site applies the criteria to its surgical team and demonstrates that each delegated surgeon meets them.

ISO 14155:2026 sets out this division of responsibility. Clause 6.8 requires the sponsor to define site selection criteria and to verify the qualifications of the PI and the adequacy of the site, and Clause 9.2.1 requires the sponsor to ensure that relevant training, experience, and knowledge are verified and documented. Clauses 10.1 and 10.3 require the PI to ensure the qualification, training, and oversight of the team and to demonstrate that its members have suitable education, training, and experience for their assigned responsibilities. Clause 10.6 further requires the PI to maintain and document an adequate investigation site team throughout the investigation. These are duties to qualify and staff the team, and none of them asks the site to observe, case by case, how the proficiency of each surgeon develops during conduct, which Section 4.3.2 addresses.

The framework reflects this allocation through the Surgical Credentialing Record (SCR). Sponsor requirements set the threshold, and the site turns that threshold into a documented decision for each surgeon and links the decision to the CCDM, so that assigned surgical tasks stay consistent with current authorization. The SCR separates general standing as a surgeon from competence in the procedure, and both from readiness on this particular device, because each can be adequate while another is not. Standing can be adequate for a comparable procedure and still uncertain for the technique of this device, and the site has to decide how much of that uncertainty the entry criteria of the sponsor already resolve and how much its own judgment has to close before the first case.

The site may also apply temporary controls when the risk assessment or the expected learning curve supports additional supervision, such as proctoring or an initial assignment to less complex cases. Their use remains proportionate to the procedure, the device, and the experience of the surgeon.

Qualification remains a current status throughout the investigation. The framework requires re-evaluation annually and whenever a defined trigger occurs, for example a complication cluster or evidence of performance drift, and the site can then confirm, restrict, suspend, or restore surgical authorization according to the evidence. Deciding what counts as evidence of drift early enough to act is the difficult part, because the performance of a surgeon is the most direct signal available and also the hardest to read without a longer run of cases. This maintenance function is a site-level contribution of the framework, because it connects observed performance with continued authorization to operate.

### 4.2.4 Device training documentation

Device training documentation is the controlled body of records showing that site personnel received the training their activities require, covering the protocol, the instructions for use (IFU), and the responsibilities of each person. It shows that critical activities were performed by personnel who were both delegated and prepared for the specific investigational context, so its function is operational as well as evidentiary.

The requirement rests on both bodies of guidance. ICH E6(R3) requires individuals involved in the trial to be qualified by education, training, and experience, and those assisting to receive training that corresponds to their delegated activities. ICH E8(R1) requires study personnel to be trained in proportion to their role before becoming involved, with retraining as needed during conduct. ISO 14155:2026 requires documented training before assignments begin under Clause 7.6. The fourth edition also strengthened the upstream side of this obligation, since Annex B now asks the sponsor to include information on device use training in the Investigator's Brochure under item B.2(h). The sponsor therefore documents what training exists, and none of the clauses examined requires the site to verify that the resulting competence is maintained. The training component of the framework covers that verification.

In orthopedic device investigations, training has to cover the proper use of the investigational device and its instrumentation, the operative workflow, device accountability, and the safety-reporting interfaces. MDR Annex XV, Chapter I, Section 2.7 requires the sponsor to verify, arrange where necessary, and document training in proper device use, the CIP, and GCP, and Annex XV, Chapter II, Section 3.2 links investigator suitability to device and procedural training and experience. Clause 7.9 of ISO 14155:2026 requires device-accountability procedures. Medicinal-product GCP also requires qualified personnel and investigator familiarity with the investigational product [6], so the framework does not claim that the device burden is wider in kind. It specifies the additional device-linked functions that must be controlled.

Two instruments carry this component. The Device and Study Training Requirements Matrix (DTRM) links each role and activity to the training it requires, with the prerequisite, the assessment method, and the renewal interval. Training Record Evaluation and Release (TRE) compares the evidence held for each person with what the DTRM requires and records who was trained, on what, when, by whom, and under which version of the controlled document. The DTRM distinguishes five training domains in a shoulder investigation:

1. Protocol and procedural training covers the study objectives, key eligibility restrictions, endpoint-relevant visit windows, PDs, and study-specific decision points.
2. Device-specific training covers the investigational system, its instrumentation and implant components, preparation and handling, and any workflow constraint the study introduces.
3. Operative and perioperative documentation training covers intraoperative records, component reconciliation, the capture of device and production identifiers, the documentation of substitutions and deviations, and chain-of-custody expectations.
4. Assessment training covers radiographic acquisition standards, imaging timing, ROM measurement methods, and the administration of PROMs where these are endpoint-relevant.
5. Safety and reporting training covers the recognition and documentation of AEs and DDs, together with the escalation pathway for clinically important findings.

Completing a training session is not the same as being ready to perform a task independently. For activities with higher risk or variability, particularly those involving the device, the imaging, or the outcome assessment, the site records an explicit release decision in the TRE once training is complete. This release decision connects training documentation to the CCDM and, for surgeons, to the SCR.

Training documentation is kept current throughout the investigation. Retraining is triggered by protocol amendments, revised IFU, new device iterations, or workflow changes, and also by safety signals and repeated documentation errors. ICH E8(R1) requires retraining when issues related to CtQ factors arise during a study and when protocol modifications are implemented. The site should therefore be able to show that staff were trained at start-up and that their training stayed current as the investigation developed.

## 4.3 Pillar 2: Risk Management

Risk Management identifies where site conduct is most likely to fail and decides how those failures are controlled before they reach participants or the data. The pillar concentrates on the risks that threaten CtQ factors and leaves aside the exhaustive listing of everything that could go wrong.

ICH E8(R1) frames quality around CtQ factors and a proportionate response to the threats against them [5]. ICH E6(R3) asks for an approach to the risks that may meaningfully affect participant rights, safety, and well-being or the reliability of results, running from their identification to their periodic review [6]. The EMA reflection paper on RBQM describes a similar operational cycle and names Failure Modes and Effects Analysis among the tools that can be useful for risk assessment [1]. The EMA paper is nonbinding methodological guidance, and the normative duties the framework draws on come from ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3).

ISO 14155:2026 gives this pillar an anchor that the third edition did not provide. The fourth edition restructured Clause 6.2 into three risk streams. Clause 6.2.2 covers risks arising from use of the investigational device, which remain inside the ISO 14971 framework and its Annex H. Clause 6.2.3 covers risks from clinical procedures that the CIP requires outside routine clinical practice, which are assessed descriptively, and Clause 6.2.4 covers risks arising from the conduct of the investigation itself. The second and third streams fall outside ISO 14971, and the standard assigns none of the three streams to the investigational site, so the risks of Clauses 6.2.3 and 6.2.4 have no named local owner.

Three risk-management functions therefore sit side by side in the conformity chain. ISO 14971 governs the risk the device itself carries, and that risk stays with the manufacturer whatever site runs the investigation. ICH E6(R3) and the EMA reflection paper govern risk at trial level, through a cycle that the sponsor operates across every participating center. Between them sits the risk that the execution of a specific site creates. The cycle of the sponsor has to generalize, because the same protocol runs at centers that differ in staffing, workflow, equipment, and experience, and a control built for the weaknesses of one center would not suit the next. The site is the party present when its own data are generated and it knows where its process is strong and where it is weak, so it is well placed to name these risks and control them, although central monitoring by the sponsor can detect some of them later. Risk management at site level follows the same quality logic as the system of the sponsor, anchored in CtQ factors and in controls that prevent, detect, and respond.

Orthopedic device investigations contain sources of variability that general trial controls do not reach, such as surgical learning curves, intraoperative decisions, imaging-dependent assessment, and early device-related complications. A generic register copied from sponsor-level practice would not capture them, so the pillar is built around the risks the center can detect and mitigate through its own processes. It holds a prospective register of process risk and a log of the stabilization of each operator, together with the surveillance of clinical signals, and Sections 4.3.1 to 4.3.3 describe them.

### 4.3.1 The Site Risk Register

The Site Risk Register (SRR) is the record through which the site identifies, prioritizes, and periodically reassesses the risks that arise from its own execution of the investigation. It is separate from the trial-level risk assessment of the sponsor and from the device risk file of the manufacturer. The assessment of the sponsor supports trial-wide quality management and monitoring strategy across every site, so it is broad by design and does not capture risks shaped by local staffing and workflows. Examples are inconsistent radiographic acquisition, incomplete capture of implant identifiers, delay in documenting intraoperative deviations, and weakness in escalating safety-relevant findings. A trial-level assessment is not built to see these risks as they arise, although some may later appear in central monitoring or central imaging review, and all of them are within the control of the site.

A 2023 study of Portuguese clinical trial sites is consistent with this gap. More than half of the sites reported using some form of systematic risk management, yet only 19.6% described a standard tool or document that systematically captured site-level risk analysis. The authors concluded that sites remain underdeveloped in the autonomous control of risk in their own processes and would benefit from a risk tool built for site operational needs [76]. The study supports DR2 more directly than the other evidence used in this work, although it was conducted in one country and across therapeutic areas.

The SRR can fail in two ways. It can grow into an exhaustive catalogue that no one revisits, or it can stay symbolic because no one holds the authority to act on it. The register therefore rates each risk and assigns it an owner, a residual risk level, and a review trigger, so that the risks that matter to participant safety or data reliability stay separate from the longer list of things that could conceivably happen. The record has to stay current enough that a real change in the conditions of the site appears in it before the change causes a failure.

### 4.3.2 Operator-dependent risk and the learning curve

Surgeon-dependent variation is a risk in a device investigation for a reason that does not apply to a medicinal product, because the procedure is part of the intervention. Variability in surgical execution reaches protocol adherence, implant traceability, and endpoint consistency, and it reaches clinical outcomes as well. Section 3.4.2 showed why this matters and why none of the instruments examined in this thesis assigns the site a role in observing it once the investigation is under way.

The literature associates procedural experience with changes in operative time, complications, and reoperations, but it is heterogeneous in how it defines and measures proficiency. Avendano et al. reported an average of about 25 cases for reverse total shoulder arthroplasty across eight studies, while a multicenter analysis found plateaus between 12 and 86 cases across surgeons and procedures [20, 77]. These values are descriptive and do not define a universal proficiency threshold, so the framework does not adopt a fixed case count.

The Surgeon Learning Curve Tracking Log (SLCT) answers DR3. It asks whether surgeon-dependent variation is becoming a material source of risk to CtQ factors at this site, in this investigation, with this device, which follows the requirement of ICH E8(R1) to review periodically whether risks to critical factors remain controlled during conduct. SQA frameworks address the learning curve mainly at selection, and the validated Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure contains no counterpart for surgeon procedural consistency once a trial is under way [37, 39, 41]. The SLCT occupies that interval and is operated by the site.

The log records, for each surgeon, the sequence of cases with their complexity and the technical metrics, deviations, and complications of each one, and it reads that sequence against case complexity, with no fixed count. The main difficulty is separating genuine instability from ordinary variation between cases. A longer operative time or an unplanned substitution can mean very different things depending on the complexity of the case and on how far the surgeon has progressed with the device. A reading that ignores this context will flag normal variation as drift, or it will miss real drift inside a case dismissed as complex. Review is therefore triggered by criteria the site defines before the first case, and its output is a decision on whether supervision, retraining, restriction, or continued release is warranted.

Because the SLCT is kept at the point of care as cases occur, it is designed to show procedural drift before the drift becomes an AE, a PD, or an anomalous data point in the study database. Whether it does so in practice is an expected benefit that implementation has to test.

### 4.3.3 Device-specific complication monitoring

Device-specific complication monitoring is the process the site uses to identify, classify, review, and escalate complications, AEs, adverse device effects (ADEs), and DDs that may relate to the investigational device, to the procedure, or to their interaction in clinical use. Three instruments carry it. The Safety and Adverse Effect Reporting Template (SART) tracks each safety occurrence from the moment it opens until it closes. The Site Complication Monitoring Log (SCML) gives the site a single index of every complication, referencing the SART case and the procedure record without repeating them, and the Complication Signal Surveillance Template (CSST) reads the SCML to decide whether a pattern of complications requires a response.

The scope of complication monitoring is narrower than that of the SRR. The register is a quality-management instrument for the prospective control of execution risk, and a register entry may state that the implant identifier may not be captured before the operating room workflow closes. Complication monitoring is a surveillance mechanism for clinical and technical signals, and its entry records that a specific participant experienced a specific event on a specific date and asks what the event means. The register anticipates a process vulnerability, and complication monitoring evaluates something that has already happened.

The regulatory basis is the MDR and the safety reporting framework for device investigations. Under Article 80, the sponsor must report without delay any SAE with a causal relationship to the investigational device, the comparator, or the procedure, or where such a relationship is reasonably possible. The same obligation covers DDs that might have led to an SAE, together with new findings relating to those events. ISO 14155:2026 sharpened one point that bears on this component, since Annex F now states that an AE associated with a DD triggers both the AE pathway and the DD pathway.

This component adds timing to an obligation that the MDR and ISO 14155 already impose. Both require individual event reports within defined deadlines, but a deadline measured from sponsor notification is different from detection at the point of care. Left to the reporting cycle alone, a pattern of complications becomes visible to the site only when the next periodic summary is compiled for the sponsor, and by then several more cases may share the same cause. The difficulty is aggregation, because a single complication rarely appears as part of a pattern, and several events that are each explicable on their own may still share a cause. The CSST therefore reviews the events together on a cadence the site sets for itself, independent of the reporting calendar of the sponsor, so that a cluster linked to one implant component, one technique, or one surgeon can be recognized while the investigation can still act on it.

ISO 14155:2026 introduced the CEC under Clause 6.12 and requires the sponsor to consider establishing one for unbiased, confidential, and consensus-based event review. The committee works at trial level with aggregated information across participating centers. The framework gives the site a local counterpart that uses its own exposure denominator and predefined local triggers. The available sources do not establish that either level will detect a signal first. A local response is justified when the trigger of the site is met, while the CEC supports consistent assessment across the investigation. ISO 14155 and the MDR require the management of individual events, and neither requires pattern surveillance at site level.

## 4.4 Pillar 3: Operational Controls

Operational Controls are the practical mechanisms through which the site keeps execution consistent and traceable over time. Governance defines who is responsible, and Operational Controls define how critical activities are carried out and checked in routine operation. The pillar holds nine instruments. Internal verification uses the Internal Monitoring Plan (IMP) and the Internal Monitoring Checklist (IMC), and the Intraoperative Checklist and Device Log (ICDL) records the procedure and the device. Imaging uses the Site Imaging Readiness Record (SIRR) and the Imaging Quality Log (IQL), and screening uses the Participant Screening and Eligibility Assessment Record (PSEAR) and the Subject Identification and Failure Screening Log (SIFSL). Follow-up uses the Participant Follow-up Visit Record (PFVR) and the Follow-up Compliance Review (FUCR).

The difficulty of the pillar lies in the distance between a procedure as the protocol describes it and the same procedure as it happens in an operating room on a given day. Two places where that distance matters are hard for an outside party to observe in time, because local conduct drifts between sponsor visits, and imaging varies in ways that only become clear once images from different time points are compared. Both depend on records that must reconstruct the investigation without competing with the records that already exist, so every control the pillar adds could become one more place where the same fact is written differently.

### 4.4.1 Internal verification of site conduct

Internal verification is a site-operated function for the periodic review of trial conduct, documentation, and critical site processes, aimed at detecting local process failure and supporting timely correction within the sphere of responsibility of the site. It is narrower than formal monitoring and replaces none of it.

The terminology needs care, because formal monitoring under GCP is a sponsor responsibility. ISO 14155:2026 assigns the monitoring plan to the sponsor under Clause 6.7, site monitoring under Clause 7.3, and the monitoring function under Clause 9.2.4. ICH E6(R3) places formal monitoring inside the QC system of the sponsor. The FDA guidance on a risk-based approach to monitoring recognizes that monitoring is used in several senses but addresses sponsor oversight of trial conduct and data reporting. Its legal basis is Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations (21 CFR) 812.40 for device investigations and 21 CFR 312.50 for drug investigations [78]. None of the instruments examined requires the site to review its own conduct in this way, and DR4 answers that gap.

The value of internal review depends on where it looks. The recurring, operationally verifiable problems in implant-based orthopedic investigations sit where operating room practice meets implant logistics, documentation, and imaging, so review concentrates there, following the RBM principle that the intensity of review should reflect the risk to participants and to data integrity. The IMP sets the frequency, sample, criteria, and escalation route for each domain, and the IMC records the execution of each review, with the finding, the action, its owner and deadline, and the verification that the correction worked. The same review, turned periodically on the conduct of the site as a whole and independent of any sponsor visit, is the self-inspection of the site. Nine checks define its scope:

1. Sterile release-to-use verification confirms package integrity, expiry, storage, and release status before use, and reconciles any breach of sterile handling or intraoperative substitution.
2. Implant traceability and component reconciliation checks that the lot number, serial number, or UDI where the device carries one was captured consistently across the operative note, the accountability records, the implant labels, and the case report form (CRF), and that the implanted configuration matches what was planned.
3. Instrumentation and operating room readiness confirms that the correct implant system and instrument set were available and documents any incompatibility or malfunction, as a procedural issue or as a DD where that applies.
4. Operative documentation adequacy checks that operative notes record the final configuration and the intraoperative decisions, substitutions, deviations, and complications in enough detail for later interpretation, since this detail cannot be added afterward.
5. Imaging usability and timing compliance verifies that required imaging was obtained within the window, with the correct views and technical quality, and whether repeat acquisition followed when it was not.
6. Follow-up execution quality checks that each visit produced the outputs that surveillance and endpoint assessment require, since attendance alone does not show a complete visit.
7. Timeliness, backlog, and closure discipline tracks whether notes, data entry, queries, and corrective actions close within acceptable timelines. The EMA reflection paper lists delayed data entry and delayed SAE reporting among its examples of trigger metrics [1], but no source examined establishes that backlog predicts a broader site failure, so the framework treats the relationship as a monitoring hypothesis.
8. External context review checks literature, registry findings, safety alerts, and field notices for the same implant class or procedure.
9. Early complication pattern review examines the complication categories of the investigation, such as instability or loosening, by timing, severity, and possible association with implant configuration or procedure, and links this check to the surveillance in Section 4.3.3.

The ICDL feeds several of these checks. It preserves the record of the procedure and the device at the point of care, including the components used, any substitution, and the final configuration as the case proceeds.

### 4.4.2 Imaging standardization

Imaging standardization reduces avoidable variability in the information derived from images wherever imaging contributes to eligibility assessment, procedural documentation, safety evaluation, or endpoint interpretation, and it answers DR7. Preoperative imaging may confirm anatomical suitability or fracture configuration, intraoperative and immediate postoperative imaging document implant placement, and follow-up imaging assesses component position, loosening, or periprosthetic fracture. Poorly standardized imaging can change how a case is classified and reduce comparability across participants and follow-up windows. Section 3.4.1 described the underlying problem, which is that the classifications applied to these images already have only fair to moderate reliability, so acquisition variability adds error that the database cannot separate from genuine clinical difference.

The methodological basis exists, although formal trial-imaging guidance has developed mainly outside orthopedics. Core-imaging and central-reading literature shows that acquisition procedures, site qualification, equipment compatibility, QC, and the distinction between local and central interpretation have to be specified in advance [55, 60, 79]. Shoulder-specific evidence is more directly relevant, since radiographic positioning and projection materially affect the measurements used in shoulder assessment, and standardized views improve reproducibility [80, 81, 82, 83, 84].

ISO 14155:2026 added support for one part of this. Clause 9.2.4.5(p) requires routine monitoring visits to verify that the maintenance and calibration of equipment relevant to assessment are performed and documented, and Annex A.6.4 adds calibration to the content of the CIP. The duty to verify falls on the monitor, and the work of keeping the equipment in that condition falls on the site, which is where this component operates.

The SIRR is a site self-assessment, made before imaging arrangements are submitted for review by the sponsor or the CRO, of whether local acquisition, equipment, and radiology resources can meet the imaging charter or manual of the sponsor. It records in advance what acquisition requires, from the modalities and time points to the mandatory views and, in an orthopedic investigation, patient positioning, laterality confirmation, and the acquisition window at each visit. The requirement has to be concrete enough to remove ambiguity at the point of acquisition while staying within what the imaging manual of the sponsor already sets, so that the site does not maintain a second, competing standard.

Site qualification and personnel training decide whether radiology staff, research staff, and assessors can produce technically acceptable and protocol-compliant images. In shoulder radiography, views only 5 degrees from true anteversion can alter the critical shoulder angle by more than 2 degrees [80], and measurements deviate materially beyond about 9 degrees of malrotation [81]. In routine practice, only 22% of images in one study met the standard anteroposterior projection [82]. Projection also produced ranges of 14 to 17 degrees in arthroplasty inclination measures [84], while standardized outlet views achieved interobserver reliability up to an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.91 for acromiohumeral distance [83]. These findings justify explicit qualification, training, and calibration of acquisition and assessment procedures.

Local review is recorded in the IQL, which checks each required image against the window, technical acceptability, completeness, and traceability. Centralized image review requires trial-level coordination across sites and lies outside the control of any one site, so only the consistency of its own review is within reach of the site. That narrower target still has to state who reviews, against what criteria, when a second reviewer or clinical escalation is required, and how a nonconforming image is handled and documented. If these points stay open, they are answered case by case and possibly inconsistently.

Timing also affects comparability. Baseline images acquired too early or too late and follow-up radiographs taken outside the protocol window reduce comparability. Shoulder studies show that positioning determines the resulting measurement and that inadequate positioning is common [81, 82]. They do not establish that pain, dressings, or sling use are frequent causes of inadequate immediate postoperative films, so the link between timing and a reproducible position is an operational inference that the cited studies do not test. For some time points the framework therefore allows an acceptable window around the nominal date, so that the image is taken when a reproducible acquisition is realistic.

### 4.4.3 Source documentation and the boundary of the framework

General source documentation belongs to the documentation system of the investigational site, and ISO 14155:2026 governs it under Clauses 7.5.3 and 7.8. The framework does not attempt to rebuild it.

The study-level requirement is narrower. The existing documentary system of the site has to be adapted so that it captures and links the records relevant to CtQ factors, which in an orthopedic device investigation concern implant traceability, operative conduct, imaging, follow-up, and revision events. Routine site documentation then remains sufficient for reconstructing the investigation and for regulatory review, and the framework creates no separate documentation system for the study. This restraint is a design decision that follows from DR11, because a parallel documentation system would impose a cost the target center cannot absorb and would create the duplication problem discussed next.

### 4.4.4 Avoiding parallel records

Any site-level framework has to deal with the risk that its instruments become a second, divergent record of information that already exists elsewhere. ICH E6(R3) Section 2.12.2 requires the investigator to define the source records before the trial begins and states that unnecessary transcription between the source record and the data-acquisition tool should be avoided, and Section 4.2.2(a)(2) requires changes to be traceable. Practical site guidance on E6(R3) makes the same point, since an up-front source definition prevents uncertainty over which record is authoritative [85]. Each instrument of the framework must therefore have a defined purpose and a defined relationship to the source. Where the same event is documented inconsistently in two places, the discrepancy is itself a finding.

The objection applies to this framework with some force, because it introduces a risk register, a learning curve log, a complication log, a surveillance template, and several review records. These are additional records, and a design rule about what each one is for keeps them from diverging from the source.

The instruments fall into two categories. The first, made up of the SLCT, the CSST, the SRR, and the CAPA Log, holds the judgments of the site about data recorded elsewhere. They do not duplicate the medical record or the electronic CRF, because what they add is the classification, aggregation, and decision of the site, and they point to the source for the underlying facts. The second category records how the site works, which covers the CCDM, the training records, the POT, and the findings of internal monitoring. These process records belong in the investigator site file, and the framework gives them structure without creating a second copy.

The design rule applies throughout the framework. No instrument may become a parallel record of a datum that already has a defined source, and where an instrument needs participant-level information, it references the source and does not copy it. The SLCT records that a component substitution occurred and points to the operative note, and the SCML records the classification and the reporting action without becoming a second clinical narrative. For this reason the instruments are built around identifiers, classifications, and decisions.

## 4.5 Pillar 4: Performance Oversight

Performance Oversight turns study conduct into measurable signals and shows whether the site keeps control of the processes that weigh most on participant safety and data reliability. The pillar uses a small set of indicators, recorded in the Performance Indicator Record (PIR), which reconciles the sources, denominators, and numerators of each indicator for the period under review and states whether the indicator shows control, a trend, or a need for action.

The indicators answer DR8, which asks for signals that show loss of control while correction is still possible. We selected six on this criterion, because failure in these six areas reaches participant protection or the validity of the investigation directly. Some of them, such as follow-up compliance, also describe what has already happened, and Section 5.4 discusses which ones give an early signal.

DR8 comes from CtQ domains 2, 7, 10, and 11 (Section 3.5). Enrollment quality answers domain 2, follow-up compliance answers domain 7, safety reporting timeliness answers domain 10, and critical data completeness answers domain 11, which covers data capture, source documentation, and the integrity of data flow. The PD rate and surgeon procedural consistency answer domains 12, 5, and 6.

### 4.5.1 Enrollment quality

Enrollment quality assesses whether the inclusion of each participant was correct, documented, and supported by evidence available before enrollment or surgery, and recruitment volume is assessed separately. The distinction matters in surgical device investigations, where eligibility may depend on anatomical suitability, imaging interpretation, previous surgery, and device compatibility. Errors at inclusion can affect participant safety and outcome interpretation, and they may become irreversible after surgery.

The PSEAR records the eligibility decision for each candidate with its supporting sources, and the SIFSL keeps the reconciliation of every candidate screened, whatever the outcome, so that the pattern of screening failures stays visible. Review covers the proportion of participants with every critical criterion documented before the decision and the frequency of post-enrollment eligibility clarification or error, together with important eligibility-related deviations and repeated ambiguity in a criterion. The site applies structured confirmation, such as a checklist or second-person confirmation, to criteria that require imaging interpretation or information from several sources. A monthly sample review verifies that the supporting evidence was available and correctly interpreted before inclusion, and review is more frequent during the first cases of the site and after major protocol amendments. Recurrent uncertainty, or its concentration in one criterion, assessor, or surgeon, leads to clarification, training, revised documentation, or sponsor escalation.

### 4.5.2 Protocol deviation rate

The PD rate is read as a profile of where deviations occur, how much they matter, and whether they recur, because an aggregate percentage can hide a small number of events that affect consent, eligibility, surgery, or safety reporting. Materiality reflects the potential effect on participant rights, safety, and well-being and on the reliability of critical data. Recurrence indicates that a process may be failing, and concentration shows whether events cluster around one surgeon, assessor, visit type, or study phase.

Each domain uses an activity-specific denominator, so procedural deviations are counted per procedure, visit-window deviations per expected visit, and eligibility deviations per enrolled participant. Important deviations receive prompt case review according to their urgency, and minor deviations are trended at the monthly governance review. Repetition of an important deviation, or a cluster within one domain or pathway, triggers investigation of the underlying process through the CAPA Log, and the site then decides whether correction requires workflow redesign, retraining, additional oversight, or sponsor escalation.

### 4.5.3 Follow-up compliance

Follow-up compliance combines attendance, timing, and the completeness of content. A completed visit is methodologically usable when the CtQ assessments required for that time point are performed, documented, and obtained within the protocol window. Depending on the investigation, these assessments may include imaging, ROM, PROMs, complication review, and revision or readmission status. The PFVR records each visit against what the protocol requires at that time point, and the FUCR aggregates visits across a cohort and a period, following those completed within the window, those containing every required component, and the participants whose follow-up cannot be obtained despite documented efforts.

The site assigns each failure to its main cause, such as participant availability, scheduling, staffing, or assessment execution. This attribution links the signal to a corrective response and keeps participant retention problems separate from site execution failures. Early trend review can support reminders, scheduling changes, or staff training before later assessments are lost. Periodic spot checks also examine whether imaging, functional measurements, and PROMs follow the specified method. Continuous second-person verification would add excessive burden for the target center, so intensified review is reserved for recurrent omissions or inconsistent assessment patterns.

### 4.5.4 Safety reporting timeliness

Safety reporting timeliness measures the pathway from site awareness to initial notification of the sponsor and on to case completion, for the reportable SAEs, SADEs, and DDs defined for the investigation. The applicable site deadline is the interval stated in the CIP under ISO 14155:2026, Clause 10.8. The timelines in MDR Annex XV, Chapter II, Section 3.14 and Article 80(2) apply to the reporting of the sponsor to competent authorities, and they do not set the notification window of the site to the sponsor. The indicator must also reflect any reduced reporting requirements permitted under ISO 14155:2026, Clause 7.4.2.

Four measures separate timeliness from completeness: the proportion of cases notified within the applicable window, the time from awareness to initial notification, the completeness of the first report, and the time to completion of follow-up information. Review also records unresolved cases, and delays are attributed to recognition, internal escalation, investigator assessment, access to documentation, or transmission. Each event receives operational review as it occurs, and a monthly or quarterly trend review examines repeated delays, incomplete first reports, and prolonged open cases.

### 4.5.5 Surgeon procedural consistency

Surgeon procedural consistency is a context-sensitive panel of signals derived from the SLCT and the complication-monitoring process. It evaluates whether delivery of the investigational procedure becomes stable and remains aligned with the protocol, and it uses information the framework already collects. Its signals include unplanned component substitution, technical or protocol-relevant procedural deviations, operative duration by procedure and case complexity, additional senior assistance or proctoring, the adequacy of documentation of implant configuration and position, and early technical complication patterns.

Each signal has a limited interpretation. Component substitution may reflect planning, implant availability, case complexity, or execution, and operative duration becomes informative only within comparable procedures and case types. Additional assistance may be expected during early participation, and early complications require clinical and technical review before they can be linked to procedural delivery. The panel therefore treats no individual signal as proof of poor performance.

Interpretation centers on stability within each surgeon over time. Comparing a surgeon with the same surgeon's earlier performance reduces the case-mix problem between surgeons, but it does not remove confounding from changes in case complexity over time. Participants treated by the same surgeon are also clustered, so comparisons across surgeons require both case-mix adjustment and recognition of the clustered structure of the data [66, 67, 68]. Candidate adjustment factors include prior shoulder surgery, glenoid retroversion, age, rheumatoid arthritis, and component characteristics [86]. Review is intensified during early cases and after the introduction of a new device, technique, or indication. Persistent instability or technical drift may lead to governance review, retraining, renewed proctoring, or restriction to less complex cases, and the sponsor is informed of any restriction, because it changes the case mix of the investigation.

### 4.5.6 Critical data completeness

Critical data completeness measures whether the data that support the endpoints and the safety analysis are present, current, and consistent with their source at defined checkpoints during conduct. CtQ domain 11 covers the adequacy of the documentary system of the site for reconstructing the investigation and the consistency between source and record. The EMA reflection paper already uses delay as a risk trigger [1], and central statistical monitoring has been associated with improvement in site quality metrics, including improvement at 83% of flagged sites in one analysis [44]. These sources do not show that backlog predicts a later data-integrity finding, so the framework treats backlog as an early-warning hypothesis and a reason for review while correction remains possible.

ISO 14155:2026 governs source documents under Clause 7.5.3 and document and data control under Clause 7.8, which covers the traceability and recording of data and electronic clinical data systems. Those clauses state the condition the record has to meet, and none of them requires the site to measure how far its own record has drifted from it. The indicator does that through four measures:

1. The proportion of endpoint-critical fields completed within the interval the site defines for each visit type.
2. The count of open queries, with their median age.
3. The proportion of operative notes finalized within the interval defined by the site.
4. The proportion of sampled records in which source and CRF agree on the endpoint-critical fields checked during internal review.

The first three measures are read from records the site already keeps, and the fourth comes from the internal monitoring in Section 4.4.1, so the indicator adds one review and reuses existing records, as DR11 requires. The three backlog measures are reviewed monthly, and source agreement is reviewed at each internal monitoring cycle. Review is intensified when two or more measures worsen across two consecutive months. This rule is a provisional trigger proposed by the framework, with no published or normative threshold behind it, and it will need empirical calibration. Its structure follows the principle of periodic risk review and proportionate control in ICH E6(R3) Section 3.10.1.3 and the EMA examples of trigger metrics [1].

### 4.5.7 Closing the loop through corrective and preventive action

The pillar closes with a defined correction cycle that answers DR5, because measurement that leads to no correction changes nothing at the site. A threshold breach in any indicator, a confirmed complication signal, a recurrent important deviation, or an SLCT review concluding that action is required opens an entry in the CAPA Log, which records the extent of the problem, its root cause, the corrective and preventive action, and the owner and deadline. ISO 13485:2016 Clauses 8.5.2 and 8.5.3 define corrective and preventive action and require review of the effectiveness of the action taken.

Three features make the cycle proactive. Thresholds are defined in advance, so a signal opens the entry before any external finding does. Periodic self-inspection reviews the conduct of the site against the checks listed in Section 4.4.1 on a defined schedule, independently of any announced visit. Where the center already runs morbidity and mortality review, the two reviews are linked, so that clinical review of complications and quality review of processes inform each other. These features separate the cycle from the reactive correction the instruments describe, such as the root cause analysis and CAPA that ICH E6(R3) asks of the sponsor when significant noncompliance is found [6].

The output of the cycle returns to the SRR. A corrective action that reveals a process vulnerability adds a risk to the register, the risk defines a review trigger, the trigger is expressed as an indicator, and the indicator can open the next corrective action. ICH E6(R3) describes this cycle at sponsor level, and the framework operates it at the level of the site.

## 4.6 The quality lifecycle

The four pillars define what site-level quality management consists of, and the lifecycle defines when each element carries weight. The lifecycle answers DR9, which rests on the observation that a quality activity performed at the wrong moment does not do its job, however correct it is. A risk register created after the first participant is enrolled loses the preventive value of one created before enrollment, because the risks it was meant to anticipate have already been taken. An SLCT started after the surgeon has passed the steep part of the curve records the plateau and misses the interval that mattered, and corrective action applied at close-out arrives after the problem has produced quality events throughout follow-up.

The lifecycle organizes the work of the site into seven phases with one transversal safety stream. Every action within it belongs to a pillar and controls one or more of the CtQ domains identified in Section 3.3.

### 4.6.1 Structure and phases of the lifecycle

Two design decisions shape the structure. Safety surveillance is not a phase, because it runs continuously from the first enrolled participant to the last follow-up assessment. Treating it as a sequential stage would misrepresent how it operates and would encourage a site to think of it as something that happens at one point, so we modeled it as a transversal stream that crosses the conduct phases, with its own actions and review cadence.

Continuous improvement is the closing phase, because its main activity is retrospective. It asks what the investigation taught the site about how it runs investigations, a question that can be answered only once conduct is complete. Correction itself does not wait until the end, since corrective action operates throughout (Section 4.5.7), and the closing phase turns the accumulated corrections into changes in the standing procedures of the site. The Post-Investigation Debrief and Lessons Record (PIDLR) carries this phase.

Figure 4.1 gives each phase its predominant quality objective, together with the actions to carry out and the checks to confirm, which work as the exit condition of the phase. Phase 1 includes two actions that are often omitted and surface too late when skipped, namely the gap analysis between the current imaging protocols of the site and those the study requires, and the verification of long-term follow-up logistics. The SRR also opens in Phase 1. Phase 2 produces almost no participant data, but what is decided in it determines whether the participant data that follow will be usable, and the SLCT is prepared in this phase. Phase 4 is the phase where every action produces a record that cannot be created later, as DR6 requires. In the safety stream, causality assessment follows the three risk streams of ISO 14155:2026 Clause 6.2, and patient factors remain relevant to that assessment without forming a fourth stream of the standard. The circular layout shows that the cycle closes and that the next investigation starts from the position Phase 7 leaves.

The quality burden at close-out should decrease when controls in Phases 3 to 5 work, although Section 5.4 presents this as an expected benefit that no measurement has yet confirmed. ICH E8(R1) Section 3.3.2 supports simplifying nonessential activity and directing resources to critical areas, and that principle is the basis of the expectation.

### 4.6.2 Why the sequence matters

The order of the phases has consequences of its own. Phase 1 comes first because a capability gap discovered later cannot be closed by better process, so a site without adequate imaging capacity does not solve that problem with a better imaging procedure. Phase 2 precedes Phase 3 because delegation, credentialing, and training determine who may make the decisions that Phase 3 consists of. A site that begins enrolling while credentialing is incomplete has allowed uncontrolled decisions into its dataset, and it cannot identify afterward which decisions were affected.

The SLCT has to cover the first case, so it is prepared in Phase 2, two phases before the first surgery in Phase 4, and used from that case onward. Its value lies in the early cases, and a log started once a problem has been noticed has already missed what it was built to detect. In post-market investigations a surgeon may already have used the device outside the study, and the SLCT then records that documented experience at start, while still recording each study case from the first.

Safety runs transversally because events do not respect phase boundaries. A complication detected in follow-up may originate in a surgical decision made months earlier, and the framework can connect the two only if both were recorded when they occurred.

Some quality failures concentrate in identifiable phases and take recurrent forms. Surgical complications may cluster during the early ascending phase of the learning curve of a surgeon [22], while loss to follow-up accumulates over later years [24]. These findings support phase-sensitive control, but they do not prove that a lifecycle model is better than an even distribution of effort, so the framework treats phase organization as a design hypothesis. The hypothesis is consistent with ICH E8(R1) Section 3.3.2 and ICH E6(R3) Section 3.10.1.3, which direct quality resources toward critical activities and risks.

## 4.7 The scalability model

Academic organizations and nonacademic hospitals made up most of the registry sample (Section 3.4.4), so a framework usable only in small centers would leave out most of the organizations that take part in these investigations. The scalability model answers DR12. Its principle is that the obligations are the same for every site while the depth of implementation varies, so any site that adopts the framework can use all of its functions, with more or less structure behind each one.

### 4.7.1 Three site contexts

The three site types are design categories defined by how research sits within the organization. The registry did not measure size or staffing, so the types are not derived from it.

Type A covers large academic hospitals and established research institutions with dedicated research infrastructure. The category does not imply uniform quality maturity. Stakeholders from higher-level hospitals performed better on average in one survey, but substantial room for improvement remained [87]. Experience from academic centers also shows that infrastructure has to be built and measured, and international clinical trial units vary in the services they provide [88, 89]. Repeated institutional self-assessment is associated with improved research-conduct capability, although that relationship is correlational [30]. Variation also persists within a center, because surgeons with comparable specialist training may stabilize at different case volumes [22]. The framework is not offered as a replacement for an institutional quality system in Type A. Its CtQ domains and surgical-context instruments address the named surgeon, device, and investigation, so a research unit inside a large institution can describe its own conduct at that level.

Type B covers private clinics and specialized orthopedic centers or hospitals that take part in investigations recurrently and have staff for research tasks, but whose main focus is surgical care, so they have no system dedicated to the quality of their investigations. This is the target center described in Section 3.4.5. Implementation here is full, with every instrument in use and every function assigned, operated by a small team in which one person holds several functions and each function has a named substitute.

Type C covers smaller centers and ambulatory surgical units that take part in investigations only occasionally. They face the same problems as Type B with even fewer people, so implementation is reduced in depth to an essential core. That core comprises the delegation and training records (CCDM and TRE), an SRR limited to the highest-risk domains, the device record in the ICDL, the SART for safety events, and a record of deviations. Some of these elements put into practice requirements that ISO 14155:2026 or the MDR already establish. The instruments keep the same purpose in Type C, while the review cadence is lower, compatible records may be combined, and one person may hold more than one function. Enhanced review or proctoring is activated when the risk of the investigation or limited local experience requires it (Table 4.2).

### 4.7.2 What does not scale

Reduced depth raises an objection, because a smaller center that implements less may be more exposed at inspection. MDR Article 72(5) permits inspection at an appropriate level, and ISO 14155:2026 Clause 9.1(b) requires the sponsor to keep records showing that the investigation complies with the CIP, the standard, and applicable requirements. A smaller center is protected only to the extent that the minimum clause-linked controls and records remain complete, current, and retrievable. Scaling may reduce organizational depth, and it cannot remove the evidence required to show compliant conduct.

The invariant is the clause-linked evidence needed to report, interpret, and verify the investigation, which every site keeps whatever its type. The minimum record set includes:

- informed-consent evidence under MDR Annex XV, Chapter II, Section 3.13 and ISO 14155 Clause 10.5;
- delegation and training records under ICH E6(R3) Sections 2.3.2 and 2.3.3, ISO 14155 Clauses 10.2 and 10.3, and MDR Annex XV, Chapter I, Section 2.7;
- device-accountability records under ISO 14155 Clause 7.9 and MDR Annex XV, Chapter II, Section 3.11;
- deviation records under MDR Annex XV, Chapter II, Section 3.10 and ICH E6(R3) Section 2.5.3;
- adverse-event records under ISO 14155 Clauses 7.4 and 8.3(a)(4) and MDR Annex XV, Chapter II, Section 3.14;
- calibration evidence under ISO 14155 Clause 9.2.4.5(p) and Annex A.6.4;
- essential records under ISO 14155 Clause 8.6 and Annex E and ICH E6(R3) Appendix C;
- the source definition required by ICH E6(R3) Section 2.12.2.

MDR Article 72(3) requires investigation information to be recorded, processed, handled, and stored so that it can be accurately reported, interpreted, and verified. The framework therefore expects documentary discipline to be achievable at any site type, while the optional organizational layers scale.

### 4.7.3 Escalation and de-escalation

Depth follows the risk of the investigation as well as the type of the site. Implementation depth is raised for any site type where the investigation involves a first-in-human device, a procedure of unusual complexity, or endpoints with high observer variability. It may be reduced where an established device is used in a routine procedure with objective endpoints and an experienced surgical team. Calibration therefore follows the risk of the specific investigation, which the category of the center only approximates, and this keeps the model consistent with the risk-proportionate principle. A Type C center running a first-in-human device implements more deeply than a Type B center running a well-established one.
