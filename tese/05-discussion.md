# 5. Discussion

## 5.1 The principal contribution and the regulatory gap

A site can conduct an investigation well and still be unable to show that it operates a system, because the two depend on different things. Section 1.1 described the move from a prescriptive model of quality toward a governance model, and a governance review looks for the second.

Under the older arrangement the site supplied the conduct and the sponsor supplied the evidence that the conduct had been controlled, so a site needed no account of its own processes. ICH E6(R3) makes the investigator responsible for the conduct of the trial at the site, with qualified staff and adequate resources, and it states this as a principle without saying what the investigator produces to show it. Our reading is that a party that shares responsibility for quality has to be able to show how it carries that responsibility, and that a list of completed procedures does not show it. We use this reading to present the framework as one defensible way of evidencing site-level processes. No instrument makes it the required way, and a site could answer the same principle through another arrangement.

The target center described in Section 3.4.5 can conduct an investigation properly and still be unable to show the control a governance review looks for, because it holds no account of its own processes to produce. Section 3.2 showed that none of the three instruments we analyzed requires it to hold one. The difficulty is evidentiary before it is operational, so a site that already works carefully meets it first, when it has to show how it controlled its own conduct.

Before we settled on a framework operated by the site, we examined three other routes to the same objective. The first places more of the load on the sponsor, which already carries clinical quality management under Clause 9.1 of ISO 14155:2026 and which ICH E6(R3) addresses directly. The second works through the CRO, which many sponsors already contract for monitoring and verification. The third is commercial software, since electronic systems can carry an investigator site file, document control, and structured records. We did not evaluate the availability or practical effect of those products in the target setting. Software can carry a quality framework, but buying it does not decide which local processes are controlled or give the site a system of its own, so we treat it as a medium for implementation.

Among the parties examined in Section 3.2.1, the site is the one for which none of the standards describes the quality system it operates. Chapter 3 did not compare resources or workload across the parties, so limited site resources are a condition of the target setting, and Chapter 3 does not show that the site holds fewer resources than every other party. Adding obligations to a party that already operates a described system leaves the site without a structure of its own, and it does not keep that structure proportionate to the setting. Deadlines for data entry, system-level edit checks, and upload windows allow a sponsor to tighten a site from outside, and those controls reach the timeliness and completeness of a record. They do not reach the content of an intraoperative decision that nobody recorded when it was made.

Removing these three routes does not show that a site-owned framework is the only workable answer. Hybrid arrangements, regional site networks, and shared services remain open, and we examined none of them. Our claim is narrower. Among the routes we examined, none keeps the record in the hands of the site while also capturing information at the point of care and running on the infrastructure the target center has.

None of the instruments we analyzed tells a site to operate a quality system, so the framework cannot be justified by naming an obligation it satisfies. Its justification runs backward. Each component answers a design requirement, and each requirement derives from a regulatory gap, a CtQ domain, or an operating condition reported in Chapter 3, so a reader can check any component against the analysis that produced it, in either direction. The correspondence shows internal coherence. We derived the requirements and we assigned the components, so the mapping is not independent evidence, and it does not show that the components are necessary, that the set is complete, or that another set would perform worse.

Table 3.1 reports five site-level functions that none of the three instruments requires, and the framework proposes an operational response to each. Four of the responses follow one pattern, in which a principle the instruments state at sponsor level, or a duty they assign to the investigator as an outcome, becomes an instrument the site can run.

The four pillars answer the first gap as a whole, the absence of a structured quality-management system at site level. ICH E6(R3) describes what such a system contains and requires it to be proportionate to the nature and complexity of the trial, and the obligation falls on the sponsor. ISO 14155:2026 gives the PI substantive duties under Clause 10 and writes them as outcomes to be achieved. Many of these duties run for the whole investigation and have to be met repeatedly, so the clause describes a process without calling it one, and giving that process an explicit structure adds organization to obligations the site already carries.

Pillar 2 answers the second gap, the absence of a risk register owned by the site. ICH E6(R3) and ICH E8(R1) require a risk-management cycle oriented to CtQ factors, and both address the sponsor, while many of the execution risks at a surgical center arise from local processes. Sponsor monitoring and CRO oversight do reach the site, but both operate from outside on a schedule the site does not set, and neither leaves the site with an account of its own risks when the assigned monitor changes. A 2023 study of Portuguese clinical trial sites found that 19.6% described a standard tool that systematically captured site-level risk analysis [77]. One national sample does not establish general practice, but the figure indicates that the gap appears in practice as well as in the texts.

The IMP answers the fourth gap, the absence of a verification function started by the site, and the CAPA Log with periodic self-inspection answers the fifth, the absence of a proactive improvement cycle. Both rest on principles ICH E6(R3) already states, shared responsibility for quality and continuous improvement, and both are meant to sit alongside obligations that already exist. These four responses therefore organize duties the site already holds, or duties whose fulfillment depends on its execution, into something it can run.

The third gap is different. None of the three instruments assigns the site the case-by-case observation of each operator's proficiency during conduct. They require the investigator to be qualified at entry, and ISO 14155:2026 Clause 10.6 asks the PI to keep an adequate team throughout the investigation, which is a staffing duty. Within the corpus we analyzed, this is the one gap for which we found no counterpart obligation. The methodological literature reviewed in Section 3.4.2 does not support treating proficiency as a state fixed at baseline, since early cases differ systematically from cases performed after stabilization, and a difference of that origin can be attributed to the device when it belongs to the operator. The SLCT therefore rests on a weaker derivation than the other four responses, because it applies CtQ logic directly to an intervention delivered by a person whose execution changes over time. Baseline qualification does not control a threat of that shape, because it measures a state at one moment while the threat develops across cases. The SLCT is the element the framework adds where the instruments have no counterpart.

## 5.2 Position in the literature

Section 1.4 described four bodies of work that bear on this problem: the general quality infrastructure written for sponsors, SQA in surgical trials, the measurement of site performance, and empirical studies of how sites manage quality. The first three produced instruments and the fourth produced evidence, but none of the works we reviewed places an integrated instrument in the hands of the site for its own use. Our narrative review did find two partial precedents operated by sites, the Clinical Trial Assessment of Infrastructure Matrix and the implementation of ISO 9001 in Italian clinical trial centers [30, 31]. Neither covers the surgical and device-specific functions proposed here.

Following Section 2.3.3, we compared the framework component by component with the instruments closest to it. The SQA frameworks have a direct counterpart for the SCR, since entry criteria for surgeons are their principal control over the operator [35, 36, 37, 38]. That counterpart works at trial level, set by the trial team, and the SCR applies it at the site and keeps it current during conduct. None of these frameworks tracks the trajectory of an individual operator across cases while the trial runs, so the SLCT has no counterpart among them. The Portuguese study of site risk management found no standard site risk tool at most sites and recommended one [77], so the SRR answers a need already identified at site level, without a published instrument to compare it with. Section 1.4.1 also showed that SQA measures have rarely been validated [39, 40], and the same observation applies to the present work, which proposes and does not validate.

The Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure allows an item-by-item comparison with the indicators in Section 4.5 [41]. Its four validated domains are participant retention and consent, data completeness and timeliness, AE reporting, and protocol compliance. Data completeness and timeliness has a direct counterpart in critical data completeness, AE reporting in safety reporting timeliness, and protocol compliance in the PD rate. Participant retention has a counterpart in follow-up compliance, but consent has none among our indicators, because the framework controls consent through the minimum record set and internal review and does not measure it as an indicator. Enrollment quality has only a partial counterpart, inside protocol compliance. Surgeon procedural consistency has no counterpart in the validated set, since it addresses the delivery of the intervention, which a measure built for trial sites in general has no reason to contain.

The comparison runs against this work in one respect that matters more than the counterparts. The four validated domains survived a reduction from 126 candidates and a validation study, while our six indicators rest on the domain analysis in Section 3.3 and on argument, so a reader who asks which set is better evidenced should prefer the validated one. The two sets also differ in orientation. A validated performance measure is applied from outside to assess a site for another party and identifies the sites that are performing, while an indicator operated by the site asks whether one of its own processes has begun to drift.

The study of RBM in trials of implantable cardiac devices is the nearest prior work on implantable devices [48]. It evaluated an approach run by the sponsor, so it supports the plausibility of risk-based quality management in device investigations without saying anything about a framework operated by the site, and its results do not transfer causally to shoulder surgery.

## 5.3 Design implications of the surgical and site context

Section 3.4.1 set out five conditions that separate surgical device investigations from the pharmacological model, and each placed a demand on the design.

Operator dependence required a unit of analysis that clinical research does not normally use. Trials are organized around the participant and the case, and the surgeon is recorded as a delegated person. In a surgical investigation the surgeon is a source of correlated variance across every case that surgeon contributes, and Section 3.4.2 showed that trials control it mainly at entry. The framework therefore adds the operator as a third unit of analysis, carried by the SLCT and the surgeon procedural consistency indicator, together with the SCR and the release records in the TRE. A protocol defines a procedure in general terms and cannot standardize the many small decisions through which one surgeon implants one device in one anatomy, so the site governs this part through credentialing at entry, the SLCT during stabilization, and periodic procedural review afterward. Surgical judgment is part of the intervention, so the framework does not try to remove surgeon-dependent variation. It records the variation and makes it reviewable where it threatens participant safety or endpoint interpretability.

Irreversibility and unit-level traceability produced a demand about timing. Investigational devices are excluded from the MDR UDI system under Article 27(1), so device and production identifiers in UDI form are not mandatory during the investigation, but traceability still has to be produced. ISO 14155:2026 requires the site to record the receipt date, identification, quantity, batch, serial number or unique code of each investigational device, together with the participant and any explantation date, and the MDR requires traceability after the investigation. These records are created in the operating room under time pressure and cannot be reconstructed reliably if nobody captures them at implantation. The intraoperative decision to substitute a component behaves in the same way, and so do the technical deviation prompted by unexpected anatomy and the immediate postoperative image that may prove inadequate. DR6 states what these data points share, and the ICDL answers it, together with the immediate capture of deviations and substitutions.

Measurement reliability is a source of quality risk in this field before any site adds variance of its own (Section 3.4.1). A site-level framework cannot improve the reliability of a classification, because that problem belongs to the field. It can require the site to control the sources of measurement variance within its own reach, and the imaging instruments (SIRR and IQL) do that work together with assessor qualification and timing rules. DR7 states the boundary, and Section 4.4.2 records where the framework stops.

Follow-up burden places follow-up in the same category as the operating room. ROM and radiographic assessment require the participant to attend in person under consistent acquisition conditions, and PROMs, which can be collected remotely, still depend on the specified time points. An assessment window that closes unmet closes permanently. The PFVR and the FUCR answer that demand, and the follow-up compliance indicator reports on both.

Each of these demands concerns information generated at the site, at a moment external oversight cannot observe directly or reliably reconstruct afterward. A sponsor can require that such information be captured at the time, through protocol requirements and system controls with reporting deadlines attached, and remote systems can receive the resulting data almost in real time. The capture itself, however, is done by the site, which is the party present when the data are generated, so the instrument that captures them has to be operated there. Operating an instrument does not by itself require owning its record. The framework gives the record to the site for two further reasons, because the record survives changes of staff and of monitor (Section 5.4), and because it lets the site show how it controlled its own conduct when a question arises.

The resources of the target center set a second group of constraints, and Section 3.5 treats the operational constraint as binding whenever two requirements point to different solutions. Investigation is a secondary activity in the target center, run alongside a surgical practice (Section 3.4.5), so the organization has little reason to build infrastructure for it even where that infrastructure would be useful. We found no source showing that sponsors fund internal quality systems at sites, and we did not examine how these investigations are paid. Whether a control that prevents an avoidable error returns more than it costs is therefore a hypothesis we did not test, and testing it requires staff time per instrument on one side and the cost of the avoided events on the other.

The registry showed that a dedicated research structure cannot be assumed even among organizations that take part in industry-sponsored investigations (Section 3.4.4). The variable records publicly documented structure and does not record budget, staffing, quality maturity, or access to software, so it supports a statement about how these organizations present themselves and no statement about what they are technically able to run. An instrument that presumes a document management system, a validated electronic record, or a dedicated quality function would nevertheless fit poorly in many of them, so adoptability in a setting of limited technical complexity is a requirement separate from low cost. Most nonhospital organizations in the sample were in the United States, while the framework follows the European regulatory context, and Section 3.4.4 explains that the registry describes only the organizational profile of the sites.

Paper is the minimum medium the design assumes, and a site with an electronic investigator site file or a clinical trial management system can carry the same structures. Each instrument is a structure of fields, review triggers, classifications, and escalation rules, and none of these depends on the medium carrying it. We specified the paper form so that the center with the least infrastructure can operate the framework, and the transfer to electronic systems remains a property of the design that awaits testing.

Paper compatibility is a property of format and does not establish low operational burden. The POT and the MRL create reviews and evidence that overlap at several points, as do the SRR, the IMC, and the PFVR. Completing the instruments may lengthen some activities, and the framework expects the benefit to outweigh that time, an expectation that has not been measured.

Reduced implementation depth at a smaller center raises an objection about regulatory exposure, and the scalability model answers it conditionally. Reduced depth creates no additional exposure provided that the minimum record set in Section 4.7.2 is complete and the documentation trail is unbroken. The reasoning is that inspection assesses compliance through the completeness and internal consistency of the documentary record, and that the sophistication of the system producing it is secondary. We could not source that premise, because we found no published study comparing inspection outcomes across systems of different sophistication, and a reader who rejects it should reject the scalability argument with it.

The losses at a Type C center are real. It reviews less often than a Type B center, which lengthens the interval before a signal is noticed, and it combines records that a Type B center keeps separate, which reduces the independence between the instrument that raises a signal and the instrument that assesses it. It also assigns several functions to one person, weakening the separation between execution and oversight that DR10 requires. The escalation rules in Section 4.7.3 are meant to keep these losses proportionate to the risk of the specific investigation.

## 5.4 Expected mechanisms and operational interfaces

A lagging indicator measures an outcome after it has occurred, as a count of PDs or a revision rate does. A leading indicator measures a process before the outcome it predicts has materialized, as a trend in operative time within cases of comparable complexity does. The first establishes what happened, and the second shows whether something is beginning to happen while there is still room to act.

The six indicators combine the two classes, and DR8 states the preference for the second without making it a property of every measure. Three of them read a process while it runs, since enrollment quality operates partly before each inclusion, critical data completeness reads the current state of the record, and surgeon procedural consistency reads operative time and intraoperative adaptations as cases accumulate. The PD rate and follow-up compliance count events that have already occurred, and so does safety reporting timeliness. Each of these three can still give an early signal of a deterioration ahead, so their timing has to be defined against the outcome predicted in each case, and calling the whole set leading would misdescribe half of it.

The most characteristic instruments of the framework read process states, since the SLCT, the CSST, and the internal monitoring findings measure how work is being performed. This follows the QbD premise that quality is managed by controlling the processes that produce outcomes. A process signal becomes useful only when a rule turns it into an action, and the rule has to exist before the pattern appears, so the site applies a threshold it chose in advance.

The lifecycle carries the same logic in time. It rests on a premise this work does not test, that quality failures concentrate in predictable phases and take predictable forms, and if the premise holds, activating each instrument where it has most effect concentrates effort where the risk sits. We expect a site that defers this work to carry a larger backlog into close-out. That relationship is a mechanism we propose and did not measure, and measuring it requires unresolved queries at database lock and staff hours spent on close-out, compared between sites that operated the framework during conduct and sites that did not.

None of the six indicators has been validated. We found no evidence that a growing query backlog predicts a data integrity failure, or that operative time failing to fall within comparable cases predicts a complication. A claim about prediction without a validation study is an assumption, and a wrong assumption has a concrete cost, since a site that acts on a signal with no predictive value spends scarce effort on a process that was working. The defense of the set at the design stage rests on what each indicator measures and on when it moves. Each indicator measures an attribute with a documented route to a CtQ domain identified in Section 3.3, and each moves at a point where the site can still act. On those two grounds the set is defensible as a design, and its predictive value remains unestablished.

Calling the framework complementary to the sponsor means little until the points where the two quality systems meet are named. At a monitoring visit, the first of these points, a site operating the framework presents a maintained SRR, a current CCDM, and the CAPA Log, together with indicator data for the period. A monitor who receives those records spends less time reconstructing the conduct of the site from primary documents and reviews a site that has already characterized its own risks and documented its own corrections. Adams and colleagues describe the adoption of RBM and the part central monitoring plays in it [91]. That work does not test a site-owned risk register, so the added value of these records is a hypothesis, and measuring it means comparing monitoring time spent on reconstruction with and without the site records.

This first interface carries a disincentive, because a site that documents a weakness has created a record a monitor can read. A finding the site raised against itself is still a finding, while a site that keeps no such record has nothing to hand over. The framework asks a center to accept that exposure. The alternative leaves the same weakness in place and unrecorded, where it reaches a participant or an endpoint before anyone names it. We have no evidence on how monitors treat findings declared by the site itself.

At the second interface, a site with a functioning CAPA process answers a monitoring finding through a mechanism that already exists. It can show that it corrected the specific instance and also addressed the cause, while a site without such a process improvises an answer that leaves no record for the next monitor. The third interface is site qualification, where the sponsor verifies the qualifications of the PI and the adequacy of the site under Clause 6.8 of ISO 14155:2026. A site that arrives with its own SRR and POT gives the sponsor documentary evidence on two of the points Clause 6.8 asks it to judge, since those records show that the site has identified the risks of its own execution and defined how it will supervise them.

The sponsor works across several sites at trial level, and the site works on its own processes. Complementarity therefore excludes duplication of sponsor monitoring, which would consume resources the target center does not have, and Section 4.4.1 concentrates internal monitoring on the point-of-care domains that sponsor monitoring does not see as they happen. It also excludes the absorption of the site's quality system into the system of the sponsor, which would remove the layer this work proposes. The CEC that Clause 6.12 allows the sponsor to establish and the CSST address the same problem from two levels. A cluster confined to one center may reach the threshold for a local response before it shows as a trial-level signal, but Section 4.3.3 notes that the sources do not establish which level detects first, so this remains an expectation to test.

The objection that bears on every claim in this section is whether documenting a practice improves it, since a site can record its conduct well and conduct itself badly. No evidence in this thesis settles the question, so what follows is an argument about mechanism, with its limits. Tighter controls and documentation written for the specific activity leave fewer gaps open, so less passes unnoticed. The claim concerns the probability that a failure goes undetected and says nothing about the quality of the conduct itself.

Failures in clinical investigation have several origins, such as lapses, competing demands on the same person, inadequate study design, systems that do not fit the work, and ambiguity in the applicable requirements, and we have no observational basis for ranking them. Deliberate noncompliance and fraud form a separate category this framework does not address, since a person who intends to misrepresent conduct will misrepresent the record as readily as the act. The framework addresses the failures nobody noticed in time, whatever produced them, because a failure has to be seen before anyone can respond to it, and an instrument that brings it into view gives a person something to act on.

If the framework changed nothing in conduct and its only effect were a complete and traceable record of what the site did, that outcome would still improve reconstruction and auditability. Whether the record protects a participant depends on someone reviewing it and responding in time, so documentary visibility and effective protection are separate achievements. The framework produces the first directly and the second only through the review rules attached to each instrument.

A second mechanism concerns the people who perform the work, and we treat it as an expectation with no established effect behind it. Staff who know that their work is reviewed in detail may attend differently to what they do, and the review also teaches the standard, since a person who sees which elements are checked learns which elements matter. The expectation cuts both ways, because observation can change behavior toward the record while leaving the conduct as it was. Two features of the design reduce that risk without removing it. The instruments record decisions, identifiers, and dates with classifications attached, and they avoid narratives in which a person assesses their own performance, so a device identifier is either captured or missing. Most instruments are also read by someone other than the person who completed them.

A cost sits on the other side of this argument, and it falls on the resource Section 5.3 identifies as scarce. Every hour a coordinator spends on a record is an hour unavailable for a visit, a query, or a follow-up call, so if documentation produces no improvement in conduct, the framework has consumed what the target center can least spare. Each instrument therefore had to answer at least one design requirement, although Section 5.3 notes that some of them overlap and their total time has not been measured.

The value of the record shows most clearly when continuity breaks, for example when a coordinator leaves, a monitor changes, a PI is replaced, or a question a previous monitor had settled is reopened. A site that holds the records of the framework carries its own history through each of these events, while a site without them has no controlled record to carry across a handover. Section 3.4.5 describes this fragility in the target center.

## 5.5 Practical, ethical, and regulatory implications

A site that decides to adopt the framework faces twenty-four instruments and has to begin somewhere, and the dependencies between them set the order. The SRR comes first, because it opens during feasibility (Phase 1) and keeps its preventive value only if it exists before enrollment (Section 4.6). The seven Governance instruments follow in Phase 2, since all of them can be completed before the first participant exists, and the SLCT is prepared in the same phase and used from the first case. The IMP and internal monitoring start once cases have begun. Five of the six indicators come last, because each needs accumulated observations before a trend means anything, and enrollment quality is the exception. We propose this order as a sequence to test, and a center that adopts all twenty-four at once is unlikely to operate any of them well.

At site qualification a sponsor can ask whether the site holds its own risk register, an oversight plan, and a corrective action process, and each of those records speaks to a point Clause 6.8 asks the sponsor to judge. During conduct, the sponsor can also take the signals the site produces into account when it adjusts monitoring intensity, since RBM lets a sponsor adjust monitoring to the available evidence. Whether either change requires an amendment to the CIP depends on how the CIP and the monitoring plan are written for the specific investigation and on the sponsor-site agreement. Clause 6.7 places the monitoring strategy in the monitoring plan, which allows the adjustment without an amendment in many arrangements, although we do not claim that this holds generally.

A notified body and a competent authority meet the investigation through different objects. The notified body assesses device conformity and the clinical evaluation, while the competent authority may inspect investigation sites. The CER carries the clinical evidence, and the local framework that produced it stays at the site unless it is requested during an inspection. Within the present texts, no clause expressly requires a site to produce its own risk register, internal monitoring findings, or CAPA Log, so these records add nothing to what an authority can demand. Their value lies in the governance review of the site and in its ability to show, when a question arises, how it controlled its own conduct.

ISO 14155:2026 was published on 23 March 2026, and the gaps reported here are seen from the position of the site. The standard has a wider purpose, and several of the gaps follow from reading it from that one position, so a future revision might reasonably decline to close them. Section 4.3 described how Clause 6.2 assigns the three risk streams to the sponsor without describing the local process through which a site handles the part of those risks that its own execution creates. Section 4.4.2 described the equipment calibration that Clause 9.2.4.5(p) asks the monitor to verify, which is continuing work at the site with no clause describing the record that shows it. Clause 6.12 introduces the CEC and creates no local counterpart.

These observations differ in size. A clause requiring a site to maintain a risk register would ask for one document and one review cycle, while a clause requiring a structured quality-management system at site level would ask for much more. The framework is designed so that a Type B center can build and run that system with its own resources, starting from the order of adoption above. A sponsor could also support a site, for instance by supplying templates and training alongside the protocol and the Investigator's Brochure, as long as the site operates the instruments and keeps the records they produce.

The version of the standard that carries a presumption of conformity is the harmonized EN ISO 14155:2020+A11:2024, listed under the MDR in OJ L 2026/193. The 2026 edition represents the current state of the art while the 2020 edition remains the harmonized one, so a site in the EU works to one text while the field moves to another. The harmonization of the fourth edition would be a suitable moment to examine what the MDR requires as a minimum structure at site level, since Annex XV governs the conduct of clinical investigations and does not address how a site organizes itself to conduct them. We offer these as observations from one position and do not present them as a program for a standards body.

Clinical investigations are conducted on people who consent to accept risk for the sake of knowledge, and the quality of conduct at the site decides how well that consent is respected. A delayed safety report in an investigation of an implantable device postpones the identification of a device-related signal, and the delay affects participants at every enrolled center, including those the reporting center never saw. An incomplete traceability record can make it impossible to attribute a complication to the lot that produced it, and an eligibility error exposes a participant to a procedure the protocol meant to exclude them from, an error that Section 3.4.1 explains cannot be undone. The participant carries the risk of these failures without seeing the quality system. Fitness to conduct the investigation is judged by the sponsor at qualification, by the ethics committee at approval, and by the site itself in every case it conducts. The framework addresses the third judgment, for which the instruments examined describe no structure.

The SLCT raises a second ethical question, about the people the instruments observe. It holds performance by named surgeon and can lead to a restriction on the cases a surgeon takes, so in substance it assesses professional performance. It is therefore a quality instrument with possible professional consequences, and its use has to be governed as such. The design constrains that use by coding the surgeon identifier and leaving the key with the PI, by defining the outcomes available to a review before the first case, and by requiring the reasoning behind any restriction to be documented.

The residual risk concentrates where the site owns the practice of the surgeon it observes. In a hospital, the authority to restrict a surgeon to less complex cases sits outside the ownership of the institution, while in a surgeon-owned practice the person deciding and the person restricted may be the same person or close colleagues, and so may the person carrying the commercial consequence. When the PI is the surgeon under review, the framework therefore moves the decision on a restriction to a party outside the site, such as the medical monitor of the sponsor or the ethics committee, named before the first case. The documented reasoning and the predefined outcomes apply to that external decision as well.

## 5.6 Transferability and boundary conditions

The framework was developed for shoulder device investigations, and Section 1.3 gives the reason for narrowing to one discipline. Whether it transfers to other implant-based surgical specialties is a separate question, and the instruments supply most of the answer.

The instruments separate into a transferable structure and the content held inside it. The field structures, review triggers, and escalation logic carry no anatomical content, and neither does the decision not to set a shoulder-only range-of-motion value or a universal minimum number of cases. The complication categories and the imaging requirements do carry anatomical content and would need replacing for another specialty. The complexity classification used in the SLCT may give a useful starting point for another arthroplasty setting, but its transfer has not been validated and its content needs specialty-specific review. Section 4.8 should be read with that distinction, since its statement that the templates reflect the shoulder context refers mainly to the content of those lists.

Neither the MDR nor ISO 14155:2026 distinguishes a shoulder device from a knee device or a hand prosthesis, and both refer generally to the investigational device and to the clinical procedures the CIP requires. The obligations behind these instruments are therefore common to implant surgery. This makes structural adaptability plausible without establishing it, since an instrument can meet a general obligation through a form that suits one specialty alone.

The claim holds for implant-based surgical investigations, where the operator delivers the intervention, the device is traceable at unit level, and outcome assessment depends on imaging and functional measurement. It does not hold for nonsurgical device investigations, where operator dependence takes a different form, or for surgical investigations without an implant, where unit-level traceability does not arise.

Applying the instrument set to a hip or knee device investigation and recording which fields required alteration would show how much of the structure survives the move, and that test is direct and inexpensive. If only classification entries change, the claim holds. If review triggers, escalation rules, or field structures have to be rebuilt, the framework is a shoulder instrument that we described too broadly. Nobody has performed that test, so structural adaptability remains a property we designed for and did not demonstrate.

## 5.7 Limitations and validation priorities

This is a design-oriented thesis, and its result is an artifact justified by argument and by traceability to regulatory obligations and to the methodological literature. It is not a hypothesis tested against data. Section 2.1.1 reports four of the six DSRM activities, and the two it does not report are demonstration and evaluation.

Three questions therefore remain open: whether the SLCT detects procedural drift earlier, whether the complication monitoring design improves the completeness of AE ascertainment, and whether the indicator set detects loss of control while correction remains possible. Answering any of them requires field implementation and measurement. The absence of an implementation weakens the claims in Section 5.4 about earlier detection and about conduct, and none of them is a finding, while the regulatory analysis in Chapter 3 and the design derivation in Chapter 4 do not depend on them.

The framework asks a center to operate twenty-four instruments, and no site has operated even one of them under the framework. This limitation concerns volume and is separate from the question of effect. Whether a team of one investigator and one coordinator, with operating room staff, can sustain the full set through a complete investigation is unknown, and no evidence shows that the scalability model in Section 4.7 answers that risk.

The literature review was narrative (Section 2.3.1), so it supports a map of the problem space and cannot show that a body of work is absent from the field, and the position claimed in Section 5.2 describes the works we read. The evidence for the problem rests on the regulatory texts, the published literature, and professional guidance. We did not assess site standard operating procedures, investigation files, or audit data, because this work had no access to them, so the operational side of the gap rests on what the literature reports about practice. Field research across the three site types would calibrate the framework against observed failure modes, which the present version infers.

The framework was designed for the European regulatory context, under the MDR with ISO 14155 as the operative GCP standard, because the thesis was developed in the EU and the supervising team has its regulatory experience there. The United States has a different structure of investigator obligations, device accountability, and AE reporting. The registry placed 60 of the 65 nonhospital organizations in the United States, because ClinicalTrials.gov offered more data and better tools for this analysis than the European sources available to us. The registry therefore informs the organizational profile of the target center, and a transfer of the framework to investigations under 21 CFR 812 would need its own regulatory analysis.

Section 2.4.3 records the limitations specific to the registry analysis, and each one bounds a different inference. The classification rests on publicly documented institutional identity, which bounds the environment assignment, since an organization presenting itself as a specialized institute may operate as a general hospital department. The sample is weighted toward the United States, where registration on ClinicalTrials.gov is mandatory for a large share of studies, while it is voluntary elsewhere, and together these facts bound any statement about the global distribution of these investigations. Normalizing facility records to organizations required judgments about ownership and naming based on public sources, and no independent second assessor reviewed them, which bounds the environment assignment and every other qualitative judgment in this thesis. Eight of the 235 organizations remain unresolved, and the percentages reported here keep them in the denominator.

Among these limitations, the absence of an implementation matters most, because removing it would turn the central expectations of this discussion into findings, and removing any of the others would not. An implementation study is therefore the first piece of work this thesis leaves open. It would place the framework in a small number of Type B centers and run it through at least one full investigation, measuring indicator values against the outcomes they are meant to anticipate and the effort per instrument in staff time. It would also measure record completeness against a defined checklist and the proportion of deviations the site detected before the monitor found them, which tests most directly whether the framework does what it claims.

The design of that study meets a volume constraint. The 46 investigations in the registry sample had a mean of about seven registered locations each (335 organization-study links), and their target enrollment was not analyzed. An investigation spread across several centers leaves each center with a small number of cases over several years, which supports a feasibility study of whether the instruments can be operated and does not support validating six indicators. Feasibility and validation therefore need separate designs, and only the first is available to a single-center study. Such a study would also settle two questions this discussion could only raise, namely whether a monitor treats a finding declared by the site differently from one discovered during a visit, and whether documentation changes conduct, which requires measuring conduct as well as records.

Content review and usability testing come first in the validation sequence, because an instrument nobody can complete correctly cannot be evaluated for anything else. Feasibility and fidelity of operation come second, with workload measured in both, then signal validity, and outcome effects come last, since each stage depends on the one before it.

Indicator validation can proceed separately and needs no full deployment. A retrospective study could pair historical indicator values from existing site records with the events that followed them, and pooling records across several centers of one sponsor would supply the volume a single site cannot. Query backlog and reporting timeliness are recoverable from most trial databases, while operative time by case complexity is recoverable only where the site recorded the complexity.

The transferability test in Section 5.6 requires no new investigation, since applying the instrument set to the documentation of a completed hip or knee device investigation would show which fields required alteration. A sponsor that supplies templates and training to several of its sites, as Section 5.5 mentions, could also pilot the framework across those sites in one investigation, which would test whether the instruments carry into an electronic system without structural change.

Two smaller pieces of work would strengthen the evidence base of this thesis directly. A second coder applied to the registry classification would address the single-assessor limitation recorded above. A versioned master index of the instrument set, giving each instrument a code, a version, a pillar, a lifecycle phase, an owner, and the records it links to, would let a reader audit the claim that the framework contains twenty-four instruments.
