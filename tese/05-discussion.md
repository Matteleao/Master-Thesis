# 5. Discussion

## 5.1 The principal contribution and the regulatory gap

Conducting an investigation well and showing that one operates a system are separate accomplishments, and a site can reach the first without the second. Section 1.1 described the movement from a prescriptive model of quality toward a governance model, and a governance review looks for the second.

Under the older arrangement the site supplied the conduct and the sponsor supplied the evidence that the conduct had been controlled, so a site needed no account of its own processes. ICH E6(R3) requires the investigator to put appropriate processes in place and to make sure that qualified staff and adequate resources support quality at the site. The text states this as a principle and does not say what the investigator produces to show it. Our reading is that a party co-responsible for quality has to be able to show how it carries that responsibility, and that a list of completed procedures does not show it. We use that reading to present the framework as one defensible way of evidencing site-level processes. No instrument makes it the required way, and a site could answer the same principle through another arrangement.

The center described in Section 3.4.4 can conduct an investigation properly and still be unable to show the control a governance review looks for, because it holds no account of its own processes to produce. Section 3.2 established that none of the three instruments we analyzed requires it to hold one. The difficulty is evidentiary before it is operational, so a site that already works carefully feels it first.

The four pillars synthesize functions that ISO 13485:2016, ISO 14971:2019, and ISO 15189:2022 require of the organizations they govern (Section 2.5.1). All twelve design requirements fell inside one of the four.

Before we settled on an architecture operated by the site, we examined three other routes to the same objective. The first places more of the load on the sponsor, who already carries clinical quality management under Clause 9.1 of ISO 14155:2026 and whom ICH E6(R3) addresses directly. The second works through the contract research organization, which many sponsors already contract to conduct monitoring and verification functions. A commercial software product forms the third route, since electronic systems can carry an investigator site file, document control, and structured records. We did not evaluate the availability or practical effect of those products in the target setting. Software can carry a quality architecture, but purchase alone does not determine which local processes are controlled or create a system the site owns. It is therefore an implementation medium, not a substitute for the architecture.

Section 3.2.1 established that the site is the only party in the conformity chain without a standard describing the quality system it operates. Chapter 3 did not measure resources or workload comparatively across the parties, so limited site resources are a condition of the target setting, and Chapter 3 gives no finding that the site holds fewer resources than every other party. Adding obligations to a party that already operates a described system changes neither the absence of a site-level quality architecture nor the need to keep that architecture proportionate to the setting. Deadlines for data entry, system-level edit checks, and required upload windows allow a sponsor to tighten a site from outside, and those controls reach the timeliness and completeness of a record. They do not reach the content of an intraoperative decision that nobody recorded while it was being made.

Removing these three routes does not establish that a site-owned architecture is the only workable answer. Hybrid arrangements and regional site networks remain open, as do shared services and tooling supplied by the sponsor, and we examined none of them. Our claim is narrower. Among the routes we did examine, none keeps the record in the hands of the site while also capturing information at the point of care and running on the infrastructure the target center has.

None of the instruments we analyzed tells a site to operate a quality system. That silence is the gap this work reports, so we cannot justify the framework by naming an obligation it satisfies. The justification runs backward. Each component answers a design requirement, and each requirement derives from a regulatory gap, from a Critical-to-Quality domain, or from an operating condition reported in Chapter 3. A reader can check any component against the analysis that produced it, in either direction. That correspondence shows internal coherence and nothing more. We derived the requirements and we assigned the components, so the mapping is not independent evidence, and it does not show that the components are necessary, that the set is complete, or that another set would perform worse.

Table 3.1 reports five site-level functions that none of the three instruments requires, and the framework proposes an operational response to each of the five. Four of those responses follow one pattern, where we take a principle the instruments state at sponsor level and build the site-level instrument they omit.

The four pillars answer the first gap as a whole, which is the absence of a structured quality management system at site level. ICH E6(R3) describes what such a system contains and requires it to be proportionate to the nature and complexity of the trial, and the obligation falls on the sponsor. ISO 14155:2026 gives the principal investigator substantive duties under Clause 10 and writes them as outcomes to be achieved. Those duties run for the whole investigation and have to be met repeatedly, so what the clause describes is a process without calling it one. Giving that implicit process an explicit structure adds organization to obligations the site already carries.

Pillar 2 answers the second gap, the absence of a risk register owned by the site, on the same reasoning. ICH E6(R3) and ICH E8(R1) require a risk management cycle oriented to Critical-to-Quality factors, and both address the sponsor, while the processes that generate the most consequential execution risks at a surgical center are local. Sponsor monitoring and oversight by a contract research organization do reach the site, although both operate from outside on a schedule the site does not set, and neither leaves the site with an account of its own risks when the assigned monitor changes. A 2023 study of Portuguese clinical trial sites found that 19.6% described a standard tool that systematically captured site-level risk analysis [77]. One national sample does not establish general practice. The figure does indicate that the gap appears in practice as well as in the texts.

The Internal Monitoring Plan answers the fourth gap, the absence of a verification function that the site itself starts, and the corrective and preventive action cycle with periodic self-inspection answers the fifth, the absence of a proactive improvement cycle. Both rest on principles ICH E6(R3) already states, shared quality responsibility and continuous improvement, and both are meant to sit alongside obligations that already exist.

Those four answers share one property that governs how the framework should be read. Each creates a duty a regulator could derive from duties the site already holds, so the framework supplies organization where the obligation already exists. The third gap is where that description stops applying.

None of the three instruments requires surgical proficiency to be observed during conduct, and all three require the investigator to be qualified at baseline and stop there. Within the corpus we analyzed, this is the only one of the five gaps with no counterpart obligation. The methodological literature reviewed in Section 3.4.2 does not support treating proficiency as a baseline state, since early cases differ systematically from cases performed after stabilization, and a difference of that origin can be attributed to the device when it belongs to the operator. Surgical Learning Curve Tracking therefore rests on a weaker derivation than the other four components. Applying Critical-to-Quality logic to an intervention delivered by a person whose execution changes over time produces operator learning as a first-order threat to data validity. Baseline qualification does not control a threat of that shape, because it measures a state at one moment and the threat is a trajectory.

## 5.2 Position in the literature

The operational quality infrastructure built for clinical research in general is the first of four bodies of work that bear on this problem. Quality assurance of the surgical intervention in trials is the second, the third measures the performance of sites, and the fourth reports empirically on how sites manage quality in practice. The first three produced instruments and the fourth produced evidence. None of the four places an integrated instrument in the hands of the site for its own use. Our narrative review did identify two partial site-operated precedents: the Clinical Trial Assessment of Infrastructure Matrix and ISO 9001 implementation in Italian clinical trial centers [30, 31]. Neither provides the integrated, site-owned architecture proposed here.

TransCelerate BioPharma developed a clinical quality management system together with a set of risk-based monitoring frameworks, and the Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative issued quality by design recommendations with the methodological tools that support them. The Medical Device Innovation Consortium supplement to the Critical-to-Quality framework extends the same approach to devices and recognizes that device trials can involve learning curves in product use. This general infrastructure is technically mature and written for the sponsor. It describes how a sponsor identifies Critical-to-Quality factors and allocates monitoring resources in proportion to risk, and it also covers how oversight is coordinated across several sites. The site receives controls designed elsewhere.

A systematic review of oncology trials sorted the reported challenges to surgical quality assurance under credentialing and standardization, with monitoring as a third group, and concluded that implementation and reporting of effective measures were inadequate [35]. The ROMIO study then developed concrete methods, including entry criteria for surgeons through assessment of operative videos [36], and two consensus exercises followed. A Delphi process with 71 stakeholders produced 59 strategies, of which 14 reached consensus for use within an active randomized trial [37]. A parallel Delphi with 40 experts from 15 countries produced a 13-item checklist for gastrointestinal surgery [38]. These four use expertise criteria at entry as their principal control over the operator, and none of them tracks the trajectory of an individual operator across cases while the trial runs. A review of randomized trials of arteriovenous grafts applied the same domains outside oncology and found quality assurance highly variable and poorly described, with no improvement across three decades. No included trial held a defined pre-trial monitoring program, and none assessed technical performance [39].

A systematic review of 109 quality improvement studies in surgical oncology found that 2 of 12 randomized trials demonstrated an effect on the primary outcome, against 86 of 97 non-randomized studies. Seventy-four of the included studies were uncontrolled before-and-after designs assessed as low quality [40]. The field has proposed a great deal and validated very little. The same observation applies to the present work, which proposes and does not validate.

Recent studies have pointed out that there are no standardized instruments for assessing the performance of clinical trial sites [29]. The Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure reduced 126 candidate indicators to 18, organized into four validated domains [41]. Those domains cover participant retention and consent, data completeness and timeliness, adverse event reporting and protocol compliance. That measure and the indicator set in Section 4.5 can be compared item by item. All four validated domains have a counterpart among our six indicators, since enrollment quality and follow-up compliance cover the first and critical data completeness covers the second. Safety reporting timeliness covers the third and the protocol deviation rate the fourth. The sixth indicator has no counterpart in the validated set, since surgeon procedural consistency addresses the delivery of the intervention and a measure built for trial sites in general has no reason to contain it.

The side of the comparison that runs against this work is the more important of the two. Those four domains survived a reduction from 126 candidates and a validation study, while our six rest on the domain analysis in Section 3.3 and on argument. A reader who asks which set is better evidenced should prefer the validated one. The two sets also differ in orientation. A validated performance measure is applied from outside to assess a site for another party, and it identifies the sites that are performing. An indicator operated by the site answers whether one of its own processes has begun to drift.

Several studies describe site-level quality management as reactive and ad hoc, and they connect that character to persistent data quality problems [42, 43, 44]. In one mature research network, about half of the participating sites used a standardized method for identifying adverse events [45]. Sites value risk-based monitoring and frequently experience it as an external requirement reaching them through the sponsor and the contract research organization [46, 47]. These studies record a site that responds case by case because it holds no local system able to produce a general response.

Diani and colleagues reported that a risk-based monitoring approach improved data quality in trials of implantable cardiac devices [48]. That study is the nearest prior work that combines site-level risk management with implantable devices and investigation quality, and it supports the plausibility of the approach without transferring causally to shoulder surgery. The orthopedic shoulder context differs in the respects that carry most weight here, since outcomes depend far more on operator technique and functional and radiographic assessment introduces standardization problems with no equivalent in the automated data streams of cardiac rhythm devices.

Our search returned no published direct comparison of ICH E6(R3) and ISO 14155, as Section 3.2 reports. The review was narrative and Section 5.7 states the limit that follows. A narrative search supports a claim about what we found, and it supports no claim about what the field contains beyond our search. Within that limit, the absence indicates that the reconciliation of the two frameworks has not reached the indexed literature, and a site drawing on both performs the reconciliation informally.

## 5.3 Design implications of the surgical and site context

Section 1.3 and Section 3.4.1 set out five conditions that separate surgical device investigations from the pharmacological model. This section reports the demand each condition placed on the design.

Operator dependence required a unit of analysis that clinical research does not normally use. Trials are organized around the participant and the case, and the surgeon is recorded as a delegated person. In a surgical investigation the surgeon is a source of correlated variance across every case that surgeon contributes, which the methodological literature treats as clustering [67]. A survey of 44 United Kingdom Clinical Trials Units found that 89% managed learning by setting a minimum expertise level for the treatment provider, and one unit reported analyzing learning effects as part of the main analysis [71]. Within that survey, control at entry was near universal and observation during conduct was near absent.

The framework adds the operator as a third unit of analysis, carried by the Surgeon Learning Curve Tracking Log and by the surgeon procedural consistency indicator, together with the credentialing and device release records. Operator dependence also fixes a limit on what any protocol can achieve, since a protocol defines a procedure in general terms and cannot standardize the many small decisions through which one surgeon implants one device in one anatomy. The site has to govern what the protocol cannot standardize, through credentialing that gates entry and learning curve tracking that observes stabilization, with periodic procedural review to detect drift afterward. Surgical judgment is part of the intervention, so the framework does not try to remove surgeon-dependent variation. It records that variation and makes it reviewable at the point where it threatens participant safety or endpoint interpretability.

Investigational devices are excluded from the MDR Unique Device Identification system under Article 27(1), so UDI-DI and UDI-PI are not themselves mandatory during the investigation. Traceability still has to be produced. ISO 14155:2026 requires the site to record the receipt date, identification, quantity, batch, serial number or unique code of each investigational device, together with the participant and any explantation date, while the MDR requires traceability for implantable devices and an implant card for the patient. These records are created in the operating room, under time pressure, by staff whose attention is on the procedure, and they cannot be reconstructed reliably if nobody captures them at implantation. The intraoperative decision to substitute a component and the technical deviation prompted by unexpected anatomy behave in the same way, as does the immediate postoperative image that may prove inadequate. Irreversibility and unit-level traceability therefore produced a demand about timing. DR6 states what the four data points share, and the intraoperative checklist and the device log answer it, together with the immediate capture of deviations and substitutions.

Classifications used in shoulder investigations show fair-to-moderate inter-observer reliability. Kappa coefficients for proximal humeral fractures have been reported in the fair range even when three-dimensional models reconstructed from computed tomography are used [62, 63]. Outcome domains and assessment time points also vary across shoulder arthroplasty trials, and that variability motivated core outcome sets for shoulder disorders [64, 65, 66]. Measurement is therefore a source of quality risk in this field before any site contributes variance of its own. A site-level framework cannot improve the reliability of a classification, since that problem belongs to the field and lies outside any one center. It can require the site to control the sources of measurement variance inside its own sphere, and the imaging charter and assessor qualification do that work, as does the standardized local review process with its timing rules. DR7 states the boundary and Section 4.4.2 records where the framework stops.

Range of motion and patient-reported outcomes require the participant to attend in person, with the same assessors and the same acquisition conditions at every time point, as does radiographic assessment. An assessment window that closes unmet closes permanently, which places follow-up in the same category as the operating room. The visit window controls and the retention measures answer that demand, with the follow-up compliance indicator reporting on both.

Each of the four demands concerns information generated inside the site, at a moment external oversight cannot independently generate or reliably reconstruct afterward. One qualification keeps the claim accurate. A sponsor can require that such information be captured at the time, through protocol requirements and system controls, with reporting deadlines attached, and remote systems can receive the resulting data almost in real time. No sponsor can perform the capture itself. The party present when the data are generated is the site, so an instrument that has to operate at that moment has to belong to the site.

The resources available at that site set the second group of constraints, and Section 3.5 treats the operational constraint as binding whenever two requirements point to different solutions. Investigation is not the primary business of the target center. It runs alongside a busy elective surgical practice, and research is neither the main nor the largest source of income, so the organization has little reason to build infrastructure for a complementary activity even where that infrastructure would be useful.

Sponsors negotiate against standardized Fair Market Value schedules, which gives a second reason. A sponsor will not pay a site above the regional rate for an elaborate internal quality system, because no contractual basis exists for it. Payment above Fair Market Value also creates compliance exposure under anti-inducement rules in the United States. We report that as a constraint documented for one jurisdiction, since we did not examine the equivalent rules in the European Union and the framework is designed under Regulation (EU) 2017/745. The claim that survives the jurisdictional limit is narrower. Research compensation in this field is calculated per procedure and per participant who completes the study, and it contains no line that funds internal quality infrastructure, so an avoidable error becomes a direct loss. Whether a control that prevents such an error returns more than it costs is a hypothesis we did not test, and testing it requires staff time per instrument on one side and the cost of the avoided events on the other.

Our registry analysis identified 65 non-hospital organizations among the 235 in the sample, or 27.7%. The group is a minority setting, and we selected it for the vulnerability it shows on the second variable, since 38 of those 65 organizations declare no permanent structure for clinical research, or 58.5% of the group. That variable records a declared organizational function and does not record budget, staffing, quality maturity, or access to software, so it supports a statement about how the group presents itself and no statement about what the group is technically able to run. The figure does establish that a majority of the organizations we designed for do not present a standing research function, and an instrument presuming a document management system, a validated electronic record, or a dedicated quality function would land where no such function is declared. Adoptability in a setting of limited technical complexity is therefore a requirement separate from low cost.

Sixty of those 65 organizations are located in the United States, which is 92.3% of the group, so the framework is designed under the European regulatory environment while the institutional phenotype it targets sits almost entirely in one non-European jurisdiction. Section 3.4.4 states which of the two governs the argument, and Section 5.7 records the limitation this places on every inference drawn from the registry.

Paper is the floor of the design, not its endpoint. Each instrument is a structure of fields, review triggers, classifications, and escalation rules, and no element of that kind depends on the medium carrying it. None of the instruments was designed to live only on paper: a center with an electronic investigator site file or a clinical trial management system can carry the same structures in it. We specified the paper form so that the center with the least infrastructure can operate the framework, and nobody has yet demonstrated that transfer, so it remains a property of the design and awaits testing.

Paper compatibility is a property of format, and it does not establish low operational burden. The two claims need separate evidence. The PI Oversight Template and the Milestone Review Log each create reviews and evidence that overlap at several points, as do the Site Risk Register, the Internal Monitoring Checklist and the Participant Follow-up Visit Record. Nobody has measured the total time they take.

Reduced implementation depth at a smaller center raises an objection about regulatory exposure, and the scalability model answers conditionally. Reduced depth creates no additional exposure provided that the minimum implementation criteria for each function are met and that the documentation trail is unbroken. The reasoning is that inspection assesses compliance through the completeness and internal consistency of the documentary record, and that the sophistication of the system producing the record is secondary. We state that as a premise we could not source, because we found no published study comparing inspection outcomes across systems of different sophistication, and a reader who rejects the premise should reject the scalability argument with it.

A Type C center reviews less often than a Type B center, which lengthens the interval before a signal is noticed. It combines records that a Type B center keeps separate, which reduces the independence between the instrument that raises a signal and the instrument that assesses it. It also assigns several functions to one person, weakening the separation between execution and oversight that DR10 requires. The escalation rules in Section 4.7.3 are meant to keep these losses proportionate to the risk of the specific investigation.

## 5.4 Expected mechanisms and operational interfaces

A lagging indicator measures an outcome after it has occurred, as a count of protocol deviations or a revision rate does. A leading indicator measures a process before the outcome it predicts has materialized, as a trend in operative time within one complexity stratum does. A lagging indicator establishes what happened, while a leading indicator establishes whether something is beginning to happen while there is still room to act.

Our six indicators combine the two classes, and DR8 states the preference for the second without making it a property of every measure in the set. Three of them read a process while it runs, since enrollment quality operates in part before each inclusion, critical data completeness reads the current state of the record, and surgeon procedural consistency reads operative time and intraoperative adaptations as cases accumulate. The other three count events that have already occurred, since a protocol deviation rate and a follow-up compliance rate each measure something that has happened, as does safety reporting timeliness. Each of those three can still serve as an early signal for a deterioration ahead, so their temporality has to be defined against the outcome predicted in each case. Calling the whole set leading would misdescribe half of it.

The most distinctive instruments of the framework do read process states, since the learning curve log and the signal surveillance template measure how work is being performed, as do the internal monitoring findings. This is a direct expression of the quality by design premise, which holds that quality is managed by controlling the processes that produce outcomes. A process signal becomes operable only when a rule converts it into an action. The rule has to exist before the pattern appears, so the site applies a threshold it chose in advance and does not choose one once it has seen the pattern.

The same logic runs in time through the lifecycle, whose claim is that a quality activity performed at the wrong moment does not perform its function. The model rests on a premise this work does not test, namely that quality failures concentrate in predictable phases and take predictable forms. If the premise holds, activating each instrument where it has leverage concentrates effort where the risk sits. The lifecycle is the site-level analogue of the risk-proportionate principle ICH E6(R3) applies at sponsor level. We expect a site that defers this work to carry a larger backlog into close-out. That relationship is a mechanism we propose and did not measure, and measuring it requires unresolved queries at database lock and staff hours spent on close-out, compared between sites that operated the framework during conduct and sites that did not.

None of the six indicators has been validated. Nobody has demonstrated that a growing query backlog predicts a data integrity failure, or that operative time failing to fall within a stratum predicts a complication. A claim about prediction without a validation study is an assumption, and the cost of a wrong assumption is concrete, since a site that acts on a signal with no predictive value spends scarce effort on a process that was working.

The defense rests on what each indicator measures and on when it moves, and neither ground is psychometric. Each indicator measures an aspect of site conduct with a documented route to a Critical-to-Quality domain identified in Section 3.3, so the connection between the measure and the thing at risk is argued from the domain analysis. Each also moves at a point where the site retains the ability to act. On those two grounds the set is defensible at the design stage, and its predictive value remains unestablished.

Calling the framework sponsor-complementary means nothing until the points where the two quality systems meet are named, since those points decide whether complementarity is real. Three of them can be specified.

At a monitoring visit a site operating the framework presents a maintained risk register, a current delegation log with criticality coding, and a corrective and preventive action log, together with indicator data for the period. A monitor who receives those records stops reconstructing the conduct of the site from primary documents alone, and reviews a site that has already characterized its own risks and documented its own corrections. Adams and colleagues describe the adoption of risk-based monitoring and the part central monitoring plays within it [91]. That work does not test a site-owned risk register, so the incremental value of these records is a hypothesis, and measuring it means comparing monitoring time spent on reconstruction with and without the site records in hand.

A site that documents a weakness has created a record a monitor can read, so the first interface carries a disincentive. A finding the site raised against itself is still a finding, while a site that keeps no such record has nothing to hand over. The framework asks a center to accept that exposure, and the argument for accepting it is that the alternative leaves the same weakness in place and unrecorded, where it reaches a participant or an endpoint before anyone names it. We have no evidence on how monitors treat self-declared findings in practice.

A site with a functioning corrective and preventive action process answers a monitoring finding through a mechanism that already exists, which forms the second interface. That site can show that it corrected the specific instance and that it addressed the cause, and that difference separates a corrective action from a cosmetic one. A site without such a process improvises an answer that leaves no record for the next monitor to use.

The sponsor verifies the qualifications of the principal investigator and the adequacy of the site under Clause 6.8 of ISO 14155:2026, at the third interface. A site arriving with its own risk register and oversight plan gives the sponsor documentary evidence on two of the points Clause 6.8 asks it to judge. Those records show that the site has identified the risks of its own execution and has defined how it will supervise them. Without those records the same judgment rests on interviews and on the state of the files.

The sponsor works across several sites at the level of the trial, and the site works on its own processes. Complementarity therefore excludes duplication of sponsor monitoring, which would consume resources the target center does not have, and Section 4.4.1 concentrates the internal monitoring function on the point-of-care domains that sponsor monitoring cannot reach in time. It also excludes the opposite arrangement, where a quality system of the site is absorbed into the system of the sponsor, which would remove the layer this work exists to create. Clause 6.12 of the fourth edition allows the sponsor to establish a Clinical Events Committee, which keeps event assessment consistent across participating centers, and that committee and the Complication Signal Surveillance Template address the same problem from two levels. A cluster confined to one center reaches the threshold for a local response long before it reaches a trial-level signal, so the local instrument acts first and the committee acts on a wider base.

Whether documenting a practice improves it is the objection that bears on every claim in this section, since a site can record its conduct impeccably and conduct itself badly. No evidence in this thesis settles the question, so what follows is an argument about mechanism together with its limits. The claim we can defend is narrower than the objection assumes. Tighter controls and documentation written for the specific activity leave fewer gaps open, so less passes unnoticed. The claim concerns the probability that a failure goes undetected, and it says nothing about the quality of the conduct itself.

Failures in clinical investigation have several origins. Lapse and competing demands on the same person are two of them, while inadequate study design, systems that do not fit the work, workload and ambiguity in the applicable requirements are others, and we have no observational basis for ranking them. Deliberate non-compliance and fraud form a separate category this framework does not address, since a person who intends to misrepresent conduct will misrepresent the record as readily as the act. The framework addresses the subset of failures nobody noticed in time, whatever produced them. A failure that nobody sees is not corrected by greater diligence, because diligence cannot act on what it cannot detect, and an instrument that raises the failure into view converts it into something a person can respond to.

Suppose the framework produces no change in conduct at all and its only effect is a complete and traceable record of what the site did. That outcome is still preferable to the alternative, where the same conduct occurs and leaves no record anyone can examine. A complete record improves reconstruction and auditability directly. Whether it protects a participant depends on someone reviewing it and responding in time, so documentary visibility and effective protection are separate achievements. The framework produces the first directly and the second only through the review rules attached to each instrument.

A second mechanism concerns the people who perform the work, and we treat it as an expectation with no established effect behind it. Staff who know that their work is reviewed in detail may attend differently to what they do, and the review also teaches the standard, since a person who sees which elements are checked learns which elements matter. The expectation cuts in two directions, because observation can change behavior toward the record while leaving the conduct as it was. Two features of the design reduce that risk without removing it. The instruments record decisions, identifiers and dates, with classifications attached, and avoid narratives where a person assesses their own performance, so a unique device identifier is captured or missing and no amount of attention to the record improves it beyond capture. Most instruments are also read by someone other than the person who completed them.

A cost sits on the other side of this argument, and it falls on the resource Section 5.3 identifies as scarce. Every hour a coordinator spends on a record is an hour unavailable for a visit, a query, or a follow-up call, so if documentation produces no improvement in conduct the framework has consumed the one thing the target center cannot spare. That risk is why the instrument set was held to the minimum that answers the twelve design requirements.

The value of the record becomes clearest when discontinuity occurs: a coordinator may leave, a monitor may change, a principal investigator may be replaced, or a question a previous monitor had settled may be reopened. A site holding a documented architecture carries its own history through each of these events. A site without one has no controlled record to carry across a handover, which is the fragility Section 3.4.5 reports.

## 5.5 Practical, ethical, and regulatory implications

A site that decides to adopt the framework faces twenty-four instruments and has to begin somewhere, and the dependencies between them settle the order. The seven Governance instruments come first, because all seven can be completed before the first participant exists and start-up determines whether the participant data that follow will be usable. The Site Risk Register follows, since it has to open during feasibility to hold the preventive value Section 4.6 describes, and the Internal Monitoring Plan and the learning curve log come next, since both depend on cases having started. Five of the six indicators come last, as each needs accumulated observations before a trend means anything, with enrollment quality as the exception. We propose this order as a testable sequence, and a center that adopts all twenty-four at once will operate none of them well.

At site qualification a sponsor can ask whether the site holds a risk register of its own and an oversight plan, together with a corrective action process, and each of those records speaks to a point Clause 6.8 asks a sponsor to judge. During conduct the sponsor can adjust monitoring intensity against the signals the site produces. Risk-based monitoring asks exactly that of a sponsor, and a site without local signals cannot support it. Whether either change requires an amendment to the Clinical Investigation Plan depends on how that plan and the monitoring plan are written in the specific investigation, and on the sponsor-site agreement. Clause 6.7 places the monitoring strategy in the monitoring plan, which makes the adjustment possible without an amendment in many arrangements, and we do not claim that it holds generally.

A notified body and a competent authority encounter the investigation through different objects. The notified body assesses device conformity and clinical evaluation, while the competent authority may inspect investigation sites. The clinical evaluation report carries clinical evidence, while the local architecture that produced it remains at the site unless it is requested during an inspection. Within the present texts, an authority asking a site to produce its own risk register, internal monitoring findings, or corrective action record would be asking for documents no clause expressly requires. A site that operates the framework and a site that operates nothing therefore present the same regulatory surface.

ISO 14155:2026 was published on 23 March 2026, and the gaps reported here are seen through the lens of this thesis, which is centered on the site. The standard has a wider purpose and the fourth edition used the revision to turn existing requirements into something usable. Several of the gaps we describe follow from reading the text from one position, and a future revision might reasonably decline to close them.

No clause requires the investigational site to maintain a register of the risks arising from its own execution, although Clause 6.2 requires the sponsor to manage risk across three streams. Clause 6.2.2 covers risks related to the use of the investigational device, which remain inside the ISO 14971 framework. Clause 6.2.3 covers risks related to clinical procedures required by the Clinical Investigation Plan outside routine clinical practice, and Clause 6.2.4 covers risks related to the clinical investigation process. The second and third fall outside ISO 14971 and are assessed descriptively. The standard assigns all three streams to the sponsor, so each class of risk has a named owner. The text does not describe the local process through which a site handles the part of those risks its own execution generates.

Clause 9.2.4.5 p) requires routine monitoring visits to verify that maintenance and calibration of the equipment relevant to assessment is performed and documented, and Annex A.6.4 adds calibration to the content of the Clinical Investigation Plan. Keeping the equipment in that condition is continuing work performed at the site, and no clause describes the record through which the site demonstrates it. Clause 6.12 introduces the Clinical Events Committee and creates no local counterpart.

These observations differ in size, and the difference matters for what can reasonably be proposed. A clause requiring a site to maintain a risk register would ask for one document and one review cycle. A clause requiring a structured quality management system at site level would ask for something far larger, and expecting a center to build one voluntarily is probably unrealistic for the reasons Section 5.3 sets out. The sponsor already operates a clinical quality management system under Clause 9.1 and already supplies the site with a protocol and an Investigator Brochure, together with training, so a sponsor could supply a quality architecture on the same terms. Construction would then sit with the party that can absorb the cost and operation with the party present when the data are generated. Ownership and derivation separate that proposal from the first two routes Section 5.1 removed, since the site would operate the architecture and own the records it produces.

The version of the standard carrying a presumption of conformity is the harmonized version, EN ISO 14155:2020+A11:2024, listed under Regulation (EU) 2017/745 in OJ L 2026/193. The 2026 edition represents the current state of the art while the 2020 edition remains the harmonized one, so a site in the European Union works to one text while the field moves to another. Harmonization of the fourth edition would be the moment to examine what the MDR imposes regionally as a minimum structure at site level, since Annex XV governs the conduct of clinical investigations and says nothing about how a site organizes itself to conduct them. We offer these as observations from one position and we do not present them as a program for a standards body.

Clinical investigations are conducted on people who consent to accept risk in the service of knowledge, and the quality of conduct at the site measures how seriously that consent is honored. A delayed safety report in an investigation of an implantable device postpones the identification of a device-related signal, and the delay reaches participants at every enrolled center, including those the reporting center never saw. An incomplete traceability record can make it impossible to attribute a complication to the lot that produced it. An eligibility error exposes a participant to a procedure the protocol meant to exclude them from, and Section 3.4.1 explains why a surgical error of that kind cannot be undone. The participant carries the risk of all three failures and sees none of the quality system. Whether a site is fit to conduct the investigation is judged by the sponsor at qualification, by the ethics committee at approval, and by the site itself in the conduct of every case, and none of the three judgments reaches the person whose shoulder is opened. The framework addresses the third, which is the one currently supported by no described architecture.

Section 4.3.2 records surgeon-dependent variation, and a record of that kind is an assessment of professional performance in substance, since it holds performance by named surgeon and can lead to a restriction on the cases a surgeon takes. That raises a second ethical question, this time about the people the instruments observe. Describing the learning curve log as an instrument that does not assess professional performance is therefore inaccurate, and its use has to be governed as a quality instrument with possible professional consequences. The design constrains that use by coding the surgeon identifier and leaving the key with the principal investigator, by defining the outcomes available to a review before the first case, and by requiring the reasoning behind any restriction to be documented.

The residual risk concentrates where the site owns the practice of the surgeon it observes. In a hospital the authority to restrict a surgeon to lower-complexity cases sits outside the ownership of the institution, while in a surgeon-owned practice the person deciding and the person restricted may be the same person or close colleagues, and so may the person carrying the commercial consequence. Safeguards outside this framework remain available, among them independent review, external adjudication and the institutional ethics committee, and none of them lies inside the reach of a site-level design. Within what the framework itself can do, placing the decision with the principal investigator and requiring its reasoning to be documented is the safeguard it provides.

## 5.6 Transferability and boundary conditions

The framework was developed for shoulder device investigations and Section 1.3 gives the reason for narrowing to one discipline. Whether it transfers to other implant-based surgical specialties is a separate question, and the instruments themselves supply most of the answer.

The instruments separate into a transferable structure and the content held inside it. The field structure, review triggers, and escalation logic carry no anatomical content, and neither does the architecture’s refusal to set a shoulder-only range-of-motion value or a universal minimum number of cases for habituation to a procedure. The complication categories and the imaging charter do carry anatomical content and would need replacement for another specialty. The five complexity strata used in the learning curve log may provide a useful starting structure for another arthroplasty setting, but their transferability has not been validated and their content requires specialty-specific review. The derivation from general implant-surgery obligations makes structural adaptability plausible; it does not establish it. Section 4.8 should be read with that distinction, since its statement that the templates reflect the shoulder context refers primarily to the content of those lists.

Neither the MDR nor ISO 14155:2026 distinguishes a shoulder device from a knee device or a hand prosthesis, and both refer generally to the investigational device and to the clinical procedures the Clinical Investigation Plan requires. The obligations that produced these instruments are therefore transversal across implant surgery. That derivation makes structural adaptability plausible without establishing it, since an instrument can meet a general obligation through a form that suits one specialty alone.

The claim holds for implant-based surgical investigations, where the operator delivers the intervention, the device is traceable at unit level, and outcome assessment depends on imaging and on functional measurement. It does not hold for non-surgical device investigations, where operator dependence takes a different form, and it does not hold for surgical investigations without an implant, where unit-level traceability does not arise.

Applying the instrument set to a hip or knee device investigation and recording which fields required alteration would establish how much of the structure survives the move, and that test is direct and inexpensive. If only classification entries change, the claim holds. If review triggers, escalation rules, or field structures have to be rebuilt, the framework is a shoulder instrument we described too broadly. Nobody has performed that test, so structural adaptability remains a property we designed for and did not demonstrate.

## 5.7 Limitations and validation priorities

This is a design-oriented thesis and its result is an artifact justified by argument and by traceability to regulatory obligations and to the methodological literature. It is not a hypothesis tested against data. Section 2.1.1 reports four of the six activities of the Design Science Research Methodology, and the two it does not report are demonstration and evaluation.

Whether the learning curve log produces earlier detection of procedural drift is the first of three questions that therefore remain open. Whether the complication monitoring design improves the completeness of adverse event ascertainment is the second, and whether the indicator set detects loss of control while correction remains possible is the third. Answering any of them requires field implementation and measurement. The absence of any implementation weakens the claims in Section 5.3 about protected margin and the claims in Section 5.4 about earlier detection and about conduct. None of them is a finding, and the regulatory analysis in Chapter 3 and the design derivation in Chapter 4 do not depend on them.

The framework asks a center to operate twenty-four instruments, and no site has operated even one of them under the framework. This limitation concerns volume, a separate matter from the question of effect. Whether a team of one investigator and one coordinator, with operating room staff, can sustain the full set through a complete investigation is unknown, and no evidence shows that the scalability model in Section 4.7 answers that risk.

The literature review was narrative and Section 2.2.3 explains the choice. A narrative review supports a map of the problem space. It does not support a claim that a body of work is absent from the field, so the absence of a published direct comparison of ICH E6(R3) and ISO 14155 is a result of our search. The position claimed in Section 5.2 describes the works we read. The evidence base for the problem itself rests on published literature and regulatory guidance, with expert commentary. We did not assess site standard operating procedures, investigation files, or audit data directly, since this work had no access to them, so the gap the framework addresses rests on what the literature reports about the state of practice. Field research across the three site types would calibrate the framework against observed failure modes, which the present version infers.

The framework was designed for the European regulatory environment, under Regulation (EU) 2017/745 with ISO 14155 as the operative Good Clinical Practice standard. The United States context is governed by a different structure of investigator obligations and a different device accountability framework, with different adverse event reporting arrangements. This limitation has grown sharper since the registry analysis, which placed 60 of the 65 non-hospital organizations in the United States. The setting the framework was designed for and the jurisdiction it was designed under are two different places, and Section 3.4.4 has to state which of the two governs the argument.

The registry analysis in Section 2.3 has limitations of its own, and each one bounds a different inference. The classification rests on self-declared institutional identity, which bounds the environment assignment, since an organization presenting itself as a specialized institute may operate as a general hospital department. The sample is weighted toward the United States, with 149 of the 235 organizations located there, and registration on ClinicalTrials.gov is mandatory for a large share of studies conducted there and voluntary elsewhere, which together bound any statement about the global distribution of these investigations. Normalizing facility records to organizations required judgments about ownership and naming that rest on public sources. All classifications were verified case by case and reviewed in a second internal pass, and no independent second assessor reviewed them, which bounds the reproducibility of the environment assignment and of every other judgment in this thesis. Eight of the 235 organizations remain unresolved on the trials structure variable, and the percentages reported here keep them in the denominator.

Among all the limitations recorded here, the absence of an implementation matters most. Removing it would convert the central claims of this discussion from expectations into findings, and removing any of the others would not.

An implementation study is therefore the first piece of work this thesis leaves open. It would place the framework in a small number of Type B centers, run it through at least one full investigation, and measure four classes of variable. The first is indicator values paired with the outcomes they are supposed to anticipate. The second is effort per instrument, measured in staff time, which tests the cost argument in Section 5.3 and the risk named in Section 5.4. The third is record completeness against a defined checklist, and the fourth is the proportion of deviations the site detected before the monitor found them, which measures most directly whether the framework does what it claims.

The design of that study runs into a volume constraint. We have not recalculated the median number of sites or the median target enrollment for the 46 investigations in the current sample, so no figure is given here. An investigation spread across several centers and closed at a modest target enrollment leaves each center with a small number of cases over several years, which supports a feasibility study of whether the instruments can be operated and does not support validating six indicators. Feasibility and validation therefore need separate designs, and only the first is available to a single-center study. Such a study would also settle two questions this discussion could only raise. Whether a monitor treats a self-declared finding differently from one discovered during a visit determines whether the disincentive described in Section 5.4 is real, and whether documentation changes conduct requires measuring conduct as well as records.

Content review and usability testing come first in the validation sequence, because an instrument nobody can complete correctly cannot be evaluated for anything else. Feasibility and fidelity of operation come second, with workload measurement attached to both, and signal validity comes third, with outcome effects last, since each stage depends on the one before it holding.

Indicator validation can proceed separately and needs no full deployment. A retrospective study using existing site records could pair historical indicator values with the events that followed them, and pooling records across several centers of one sponsor would supply the volume a single site cannot. Query backlog and reporting timeliness are recoverable from most trial databases, while operative time by complexity stratum is recoverable only where the site recorded the stratum.

The transferability test described in Section 5.6 requires no new investigation. Applying the instrument set to the documentation of a completed hip or knee device investigation would establish how much of the structure survives the move between specialties, measured as the set of fields that required alteration. Section 5.5 describes a route where a sponsor builds the architecture and the site operates it. One sponsor could pilot that arrangement across several of its sites in a single investigation, which would also test whether the instruments carry into an electronic system without structural change.

Two smaller pieces of work would strengthen the evidence base of this thesis directly. A second coder applied to the registry classification would address the reproducibility limitation recorded above. A versioned master index of the instrument set, giving each instrument a code, a version, a pillar, a lifecycle phase, an owner, and the records it links to, would let a reader audit the claim that the framework contains twenty-four instruments.
