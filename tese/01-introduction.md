# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Historical Overview and Recent Updates

Quality management in clinical research has changed over the past decade [1, 2]. The older model was mainly reactive, because sites and sponsors found problems during monitoring visits and audits and corrected them case by case. These activities ran throughout the trial, but their purpose was detection and verification, and quality was not designed into the trial from the start.

Current guidance holds that quality should be considered before a study begins, built into operational decisions during conduct, and managed across the study lifecycle [3, 4]. The change can be followed through the guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH). ICH E6, adopted in 1996, defined the responsibilities of sponsors, investigators, and ethics committees through detailed procedural requirements. ICH E6(R2), adopted in 2016, added an explicit quality-management section and risk-based monitoring (RBM), in recognition that the same level of oversight cannot usefully be applied to every activity [3].

ICH E8(R1), adopted in 2021, moved the central question from how a study should be monitored to how it should be designed, so that the factors needed for reliable results are protected from the start. The guideline brought Quality by Design (QbD) into the guidance for clinical research. QbD holds that quality has to be planned from the beginning, because checking a study after it has happened cannot guarantee it. Planning means identifying the factors that matter most to participant protection and to the reliability of the study, and allocating resources in proportion to the risks that threaten them. ICH E8(R1) turned this idea into something usable through Critical-to-Quality (CtQ) factors [5].

ICH E6(R3) reached Step 4 on 6 January 2025 and pushed further in the same direction, giving greater weight to QbD, CtQ factors, and risk-proportionate oversight across the trial lifecycle [6]. It also asks investigators to have an adequate number of qualified staff and adequate facilities for the conduct of the trial [6].

Commentators have read this development as a move from a prescriptive model toward a governance model [7]. In a prescriptive model, a site shows quality mainly by documenting that it followed the required procedures. In a governance model, the responsible parties have to design, run, and improve a system that is proportionate to the risks of the investigation, so a site also has to show that its processes find and control risks and that it learns from them.

The ICH guidelines are written broadly, but their principal Good Clinical Practice (GCP) scope is interventional trials of investigational products, and their vocabulary reflects that origin [6]. Other study types therefore need instruments written for their own conditions. Clinical investigations of medical devices are governed by International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 14155, the GCP standard for that field [8].

ISO 14155 has followed a parallel path. It was first published in 1996 and revised in 2011, 2020, and 2026 [8]. The fourth edition was published on 23 March 2026, after the research for this thesis had started. Among other changes, it strengthens the treatment of procedure-related risks and the requirements on safety reporting and data integrity. It also separates more clearly the residual risks of the investigational device, which keep the logic of ISO 14971, from the risks arising from clinical procedures or investigation processes outside routine clinical practice. However, the edition carrying a presumption of conformity in the European Union (EU) remains European Standard (EN) ISO 14155:2020+A11:2024, as listed in Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/193 [9]. ISO 14155:2026 is therefore used here as the current international standard, while the harmonized European edition remains the 2020 text.

Regional regulation is what gives these standards their legal force. In the EU, Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices (MDR) governs clinical investigations and sets requirements for their authorization, conduct, documentation, and reporting [10]. The United States uses a separate statutory and regulatory framework under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act [11] and Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations [12]. The two regional frameworks are mentioned here as distinct legal contexts, and this thesis does not treat them as equivalent.

Adoption in practice is still uneven. A global survey of risk-based quality management (RBQM) maturity found that identifying CtQ factors was one of the least adopted components overall, with nonadoption concentrated in the Late Majority and Resisters cohorts, which together made up nearly half of the surveyed organizations [13]. Sites were not the object of that survey, so it does not show how sites apply CtQ principles. It does show that the first step of the QbD approach is still difficult at sponsor level, where the concept was developed and where resources are usually concentrated.

Together these instruments raise the standard that an investigational site is expected to meet, without saying how a site should organize itself to manage the risks of its own work in daily practice. The rest of this chapter examines why that question is hard in surgical device investigations and what the field has offered so far.

## 1.2 Quality Responsibility at the Investigational Site

Risk-proportionate oversight starts from the observation that the activities in a clinical study do not all carry the same risk, so monitoring, documentation, and oversight should match the importance of each activity and the consequences of its failure. In practice this asks a study team to do four things:

1. Identify and validate the CtQ factors.
2. Assess risk at study level and at site level.
3. Define controls that prevent, detect, and respond.
4. Set measurable indicators that trigger proportionate action when a signal appears.

Tools such as key risk indicators and Failure Modes and Effects Analysis give this work a traceable basis.

Sponsors keep primary responsibility for trial-level quality management and oversight [6, 8, 10]. Investigators, in turn, are responsible for the conduct and oversight of the activities delegated at their site, including having qualified staff and adequate resources [6, 8]. The investigator and the site team are therefore directly accountable for the quality of the investigation they conduct, while the quality-management responsibility of the sponsor remains intact.

This allocation reflects operational reality. A large part of a clinical investigation happens at the site, through decisions and processes that the protocol only partly specifies. Routine site work can strengthen or damage quality, from the identification of eligible participants and the consent process to the recording of adverse events (AEs) and the upkeep of source documentation. The site therefore contributes actively to the quality of the investigation.

Accountability of this kind only means something if it is operational. If a site is expected to act as an active agent of quality, the role has to be defined and structured, and it has to be verifiable. Regulatory texts set out principles and responsibilities without saying how each site should turn them into daily practice, and that translation is the subject of this thesis.

## 1.3 Surgical Device Investigations as a Distinct Methodological Context

Clinical research methodology and its regulatory infrastructure were developed largely around pharmacological interventions, whereas implantable devices raise additional problems of design and evaluation [14, 15]. In the pharmacological model, the intervention is a chemical compound given at a defined dose, on a defined schedule, and by a defined route. Surgical investigations of medical devices work under different conditions, and five of them change what quality management has to do at the operational level.

The first condition is termed irreversibility in this thesis. A prosthesis fixed to bone cannot simply be stopped or withdrawn in the way a medicinal product can, and errors during implantation may have lasting consequences. The cited literature documents the evidential and methodological consequences of implantable devices, although it does not itself use “irreversibility” as a formal category [14, 16, 17].

Operator dependence, the second condition, means that outcomes may depend substantially on the surgeon, with variation between surgeons and also within the practice of one surgeon as experience grows along the learning curve [18, 19, 20, 21, 22].

The third condition is unit-level traceability. Under the MDR, documentation and record-retention duties support the traceability of each individual investigational device [10], and ISO 14155 likewise addresses the accountability and traceability of investigational devices [8]. This condition does not rely on MDR Article 27, because that article excludes investigational devices from its Unique Device Identification (UDI) assignment requirement. In surgical investigations, the framework extends the operational record to the surgical decisions that produced the device configuration in a given patient.

Follow-up burden is the fourth condition. Range of motion (ROM) assessments, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), and radiographic evaluations often require repeated attendance over several years and consistent assessment procedures. Orthopedic studies also show that loss to follow-up is common enough to threaten interpretation when it is not defined and managed consistently [23, 24].

The fifth condition, measurement reliability, follows from the limited interobserver reliability of the radiological and clinical classifications on which eligibility and outcome assessment may depend, so measurement variability is present before any site-level variability is added [25, 26, 27].

The work was narrowed to orthopedic investigations and, within them, to the shoulder, for two reasons. The first is the availability of data. A ClinicalTrials.gov Application Programming Interface (API) search performed on 20 September 2026 for interventional studies with the condition string “shoulder arthroplasty OR rotator cuff OR shoulder prosthesis” returned 996 records. This retrieval count indicates a substantial registry base and is not the final analytic sample described in Section 2.4. The second reason is methodological. A framework is usable only if it reaches operational detail, and that level of detail cannot be achieved across several surgical disciplines at once, so the framework is developed for one discipline. Its structural logic may transfer to other implant-based surgical specialties with comparable conditions, but this thesis does not demonstrate that transfer.

The registry characterization developed for this thesis found that nonhospital organizations made up more than a quarter of the organizations taking part in industry-sponsored shoulder device investigations, and that for more than half of them no permanent research structure was publicly identifiable (Section 3.4.4). The framework was therefore designed for private centers that run investigations recurrently alongside surgical care without a system dedicated to investigation quality. The registry did not test whether such centers share staff, rooms, or workflows with routine care, and Section 3.4.5 treats these features as design assumptions.

## 1.4 What Has Been Built for Sites, and for Whom

Clinical research has built a growing body of operational quality infrastructure over the past two decades, including methods for central monitoring [28] and frameworks for assessing site readiness [29]. Much of the material reviewed for this thesis is defined at trial or network level, and the site appears in it mainly as the object of assessment, although a readiness framework of this kind can also be used by an experienced site to assess itself [29]. The Clinical Trial Assessment of Infrastructure Matrix and the implementation of ISO 9001 in trial centers are other exceptions [30, 31]. The narrower conclusion supported by the reviewed corpus is that relatively few instruments are designed for a site to operate on its own processes.

### 1.4.1 Instruments written for the sponsor and the trial

TransCelerate BioPharma developed a clinical quality management system and a set of RBM frameworks [32]. The Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative (CTTI) issued QbD recommendations, together with the methodological tools that support them [33]. Device-specific guidance is available in the Medical Device Innovation Consortium (MDIC) supplement to the CtQ framework, which recognizes that device investigations can involve learning curves in product use [34]. These initiatives are detailed and technically mature, and they are written for the sponsor. They explain how a sponsor works out its CtQ factors and then puts monitoring resources where the risk is highest, and they also cover how oversight is coordinated across several sites. In this model, the site receives controls designed elsewhere.

A separate body of work addresses the quality of the surgical intervention itself. A systematic review of oncology trials sorted the reported challenges to surgical quality assurance (SQA) under credentialing and standardization, with monitoring as a third group, and concluded that the implementation and reporting of effective measures were inadequate [35]. The Randomised Oesophagectomy: Minimally Invasive or Open (ROMIO) study then developed concrete methods, including entry criteria for surgeons based on the assessment of operative videos and a standardized operative technique monitored during the trial [36]. International expert consensus followed. A Delphi process with 71 stakeholders produced 59 strategies to overcome the challenges to SQA in oncology trials, of which 14 gained consensus within an active randomized trial [37], and a parallel Delphi process with 40 experts from 15 countries produced a 13-item checklist for gastrointestinal surgery [38].

The evidence that these measures work is limited. A systematic review of randomized trials of arteriovenous grafts applied the same domains outside oncology and found that quality assurance (QA) was highly variable and poorly described, and that it had not improved over three decades. No trial in that review had a defined pretrial monitoring program, and none assessed technical performance [39]. A systematic review of 109 quality improvement studies in surgical oncology found that only 2 of 12 randomized trials demonstrated an effect on the primary outcome, compared with 86 of 97 nonrandomized studies, and 74 of the included studies were uncontrolled before-and-after designs assessed as low quality [40]. The field has therefore validated little of what it has proposed. For this thesis, a second limitation weighs more. Each framework in this line that was reviewed is designed at trial level by the trial team and applied to the site, which is the object of the measures and does not operate them. In multicenter trials this centralization is a deliberate choice, because the aim is a common standard across sites, but it leaves the site without an instrument of its own for the same questions.

### 1.4.2 Instruments that assess the site from outside

Recent work has pointed out that standardized methods for assessing the performance of clinical trial sites remain underdeveloped, and it has begun to develop validated measures [41]. The Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure reduced 126 candidate indicators to 18, organized into four validated domains: participant retention and consent, data completeness and timeliness, AE reporting, and protocol compliance [41].

Instruments of this kind serve benchmarking and resource allocation across a network, so they assess a site on behalf of another party. A performance score can help a sponsor decide which sites need attention, but it is not built to help a site notice that one of its own processes is drifting while there is still time to act. The second purpose needs a different instrument, and it has received much less attention.

The empirical literature describes what happens at sites in the meantime. In a small Australian survey, the management of data quality at sites was often reactive and ad hoc [42], and other work links the way monitoring and quality management are organized with persistent problems of data quality [43, 44]. Sites differ widely in their rates of deviation reporting and AE reporting, and standardized approaches are still uncommon. In one mature research network, only about half of the participating sites used a standardized method for identifying AEs [45]. Sites value RBM, but they often experience it as an external requirement that reaches them through the sponsor and the contract research organization (CRO) and over which they have little control [46, 47].

The literature therefore suggests an asymmetry between the main parties to an investigation. Sponsors ordinarily operate a documented quality framework, and CROs carry out monitoring and oversight on the sponsor's behalf, while many of the site workflows described in the reviewed work were not part of an integrated local quality-management structure. This conclusion comes from the reviewed corpus and is not a prevalence estimate. It agrees with the gaps found in the regulatory texts, which Chapter 3 reports.

The closest prior work on implantable devices evaluated an RBM approach run by the sponsor in trials of implantable cardiac devices and found that it improved data quality [48]. It is a precedent for risk-based quality management in implantable-device investigations, although at sponsor level. Orthopedic investigations add the operator-dependent and learning-curve considerations documented in the surgical-methodology literature [18, 19]. The comparison identifies different operational conditions and makes no claim that one specialty is more operator dependent than another.

## 1.5 Aim, Objectives, and Scope

ICH E6(R3) and ISO 14155:2026 give the investigational site a larger share of the responsibility for quality, but most of the instruments reviewed here were built for the sponsor. This thesis derives a site-level quality framework that complements the sponsor's system, so that a site can act on the risks the sponsor cannot see in time and can show how it controlled its own conduct. The framework turns the QbD and risk-proportionate oversight principles of ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) into practices that a site can actually run across the study lifecycle, and it is meant to sit alongside the obligations set by the MDR and the requirements of ISO 14155:2026.

The work builds on the literature described in Section 1.4. SQA frameworks define what a trial should require of a site, and site performance measures define how another party should assess it, so this thesis addresses what a site should operate for itself. The work has four objectives:

1. To compare ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and MDR Annex XV clause by clause, identifying where the instruments agree, where they differ, and where none creates a structured obligation for the investigational site.
2. To identify the CtQ domains that apply at site level, as distinct from the trial-level CtQ factors defined by the sponsor.
3. To derive an explicit and traceable set of requirements for a site-level quality framework from the regulatory analysis, from the operational gaps reported in the literature, and from the operational conditions of surgical device investigations.
4. To build that framework and show how each component answers a stated requirement and how it relates to prior work that addresses the same subject at a different level.

The work stays at site level and complements the quality system of the sponsor. It deals with the processes of the site, supports sponsor-led quality activities, and claims no authority over the protocol. Its focus on shoulder device investigations follows from the reasons given in Section 1.3, although the discussion considers whether its structural logic could transfer to other surgical disciplines with comparable characteristics. Finally, this is a design study, whose principal result is an artifact built from documented evidence and reasoned requirements, and Section 2.6 states what that does and does not establish.
