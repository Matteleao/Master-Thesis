# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Historical Overview and Recent Updates

Over the past decade, quality management in clinical research has changed [1, 2]. The older model was mainly reactive. This means that sites and sponsors would find problems during monitoring visits and audits, and then corrected them case by case. These activities ran throughout the trial, but their purpose was detection and verification, and quality was not designed into the trial from the start.

Current guidance states that quality should be considered before a study begins, built into operational decisions during conduct, and managed across the study lifecycle [3, 4]. This change can be followed through the guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH). ICH E6, adopted in 1996, defined the responsibilities of sponsors, investigators, and ethics committees through detailed procedural requirements. ICH E6(R2), adopted in 2016, added an explicit quality-management section and risk-based monitoring (RBM), recognizing that the same level of oversight cannot usefully be applied to every activity [3].

ICH E8(R1), adopted in 2021, moved the central question from how a study should be monitored to how it should be designed, so that the factors needed for reliable results are protected from the start. The guideline brought Quality by Design (QbD) into the guidance for clinical research. QbD holds that quality cannot be guaranteed by checking a study after it has happened; it has to be planned from the beginning. Planning means identifying the factors that matter most to participant protection and the reliability of the study and allocating resources in proportion to the risks that threaten them. ICH E8(R1) gave this idea an operational form through Critical-to-Quality (CtQ) factors [5].

ICH E6(R3), the latest update, was released on 6 January 2025 and reinforced the direction established by ICH E8(R1). It gave greater weight to QbD, CtQ factors, risk-proportionate oversight, and quality management across the trial lifecycle [6]. It also requires investigators to maintain appropriate site processes and to ensure that qualified staff and adequate resources support trial conduct [6].

This development and reinforcement of concepts was considered a landmark, marking a shift from a prescriptive model toward a governance model [7]. In a prescriptive model, a site shows quality mainly by documenting that it followed the required procedures. In a governance model, the responsible parties have to design, run, and improve a system that is proportionate to the risks of the investigation, so a site also has to show that its processes find and control risks and that it learns from them.

Although the ICH guidelines are written broadly, their principal Good Clinical Practice (GCP) scope is interventional trials of investigational products, and their vocabulary reflects that origin [6]. Other study types therefore require instruments written for their own conditions. Clinical investigations of medical devices are governed by International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 14155, the GCP standard for that field [8].

ISO 14155 has followed a parallel path. It was first published in 1996 and revised in 2011, 2020, and 2026 [8]. The fourth edition was published on 23 March 2026, after the research for this thesis had started. It strengthens residual-risk assessment, procedure-related risks, oversight, safety reporting, missing data, estimands, clinical performance, and data integrity. It also distinguishes more clearly between residual risks of the investigational device, which retain the logic of ISO 14971, from risks arising from clinical procedures or investigation processes outside routine clinical practice. However, the edition that it’s still referenced in the European Union (EU) remains European Standard ISO 14155:2020+A11:2024, as listed in Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/193 [9].

Regional regulations supply legal force. In the EU, Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices (MDR) governs clinical investigations and establishes requirements for authorization, conduct, documentation, and reporting [10]. The United States uses a separate statutory and regulatory framework under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act [11] and Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations [12].

Despite the clear direction set by the guidelines, adoption in practice remains uneven. In a global online survey run by the Tufts Center for the Study of Drug Development in late 2022 and early 2023, Florez et al. [13] collected responses from 119 pharmaceutical and biotechnology sponsors on 32 RBQM practices and identifying CtQ factors was among the five with the lowest rates of use. The Late Majority and Resisters cohorts together made up 60 of the 119 sponsors, and 39% of the Late Majority and 68% of the Resisters did not use this practice in any trial. The survey does not show how sites apply CtQ principles, because sites were not its object. It does show that the first step of the QbD approach is still difficult at sponsor level, where the concept was developed and where resources are usually concentrated.

Together, these instruments raise the standard that a trial is expected to meet. The rest of this chapter examines why meeting that standard is hard in surgical device investigations and what the field has offered so far.

## 1.2 Surgical Device Investigations as a Distinct Methodological Context

Clinical research methodology and its regulatory infrastructure were developed largely around pharmacological investigations, whereas implantable devices raise additional design and evaluation problems [14, 15]. In the pharmacological model, the intervention is a chemical compound given at a defined dose, on a defined schedule, and by a defined route. Surgical clinical investigations of medical devices work under different conditions. Five of these conditions change what quality management has to do at the operational level.

The first condition is irreversibility. A prosthesis fixed to bone cannot simply be stopped or withdrawn in the way a medicinal product can, and errors during implantation may have lasting consequences. The cited literature documents the distinctive evidential and methodological consequences of implantable devices, although it does not itself use “irreversibility” as a formal category [14, 16, 17].

Operator dependence, the second condition, means that outcomes may depend substantially on the surgeon, with variation occurring both between surgeons and within the practice of a single surgeon as experience grows along the learning curve [18, 19, 20, 21, 22].

The third condition is traceability. Under the MDR, documentation and record-retention duties support traceability of the individual investigational device and ISO 14155 likewise addresses investigational-device accountability and traceability [10, 8]. In a surgical investigation, beyond the record of which components and sizes were implanted in each participant it also requested to explain why and based on which evidence this decision was taken.

Follow-up, the fourth condition, carries substantial operational weight,. Range-of-motion (ROM) assessments, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), and radiographic evaluations often require repeated attendance over several years and consistent assessment procedures. Orthopedic studies also show that loss to follow-up is common enough to threaten interpretation when it is not defined and managed consistently [23, 24].

The fifth condition, measurement reliability, arises because many of the assessments behind eligibility and outcomes depend on who performs or answers them. Radiological and clinical classifications show limited interobserver reliability [25, 26, 27], and clinical tests and ROM vary with the clinician or physiotherapist who performs them, while PROM scores also depend on how participants interpret each item. Measurement variability is therefore present before any site-level variability is added.

## 1.3 Quality Responsibility at the Investigational Site

Each of these conditions plays out at the investigational site, because the implant is placed in its operating room and the follow-up assessments that produce the endpoints are carried out by its staff. The standard described in Section 1.1 therefore has to be met at the site as well. Its central principle, risk-proportionate oversight, starts from the observation that the activities in a clinical study do not all carry the same risk, so monitoring, documentation, and oversight should match the importance of each activity and the consequences of its failure. In practice, this asks a study team to do four things:

1. Identify and validate the CtQ factors.
2. Assess risk at study level and at site level.
3. Define controls that prevent, detect, and respond.
4. Set measurable indicators that trigger proportionate action when a signal appears.

Sponsors retain primary responsibility for trial-level quality management and oversight [6, 8, 10]. Investigators, in turn, are responsible for the conduct and oversight of activities delegated at the site, including ensuring qualified staff and adequate resources [6, 8]. The site is therefore directly accountable for the quality of the investigation it conducts, while the sponsor’s quality-management responsibility remains intact.

A large part of a clinical investigation happens at site level, through decisions and processes that the protocol only partly specifies. Quality can be strengthened or damaged by the routine work of the site in activities such as how it identifies potentially eligible participants, obtains consent, confirms eligibility, delivers the intervention, collects outcome data, records adverse events (AEs), and maintains source documentation. The site is therefore more than the place where sponsor-designed processes are carried out. It contributes actively to the quality of the investigation.

Accountability of this kind has to be operational to be real. If a site is expected to act as an active agent of quality, then the role must be defined, structured, and made verifiable. Regulatory texts set out principles and responsibilities without saying how each site should turn them into daily practice, and that translation is the subject of this thesis.

## 1.4 What Has Been Built for Sites, and for Whom

The quality instruments that clinical research has built over the past two decades can be sorted by who uses them and on whom. The first group is written for the sponsor and the trial team, who use it to design and monitor a trial and to set the standards every site must follow, including standards for the surgical intervention. Central monitoring methods belong to this group, and Section 1.4.1 reviews it [28]. The second group measures the readiness or the performance of a site, so that a sponsor or a research network can compare sites and decide where to act, and Section 1.4.2 reviews it  [29]. In both groups the site is the party being assessed or controlled. Instruments that a site applies to its own processes are rare in the material reviewed. Two examples are the Clinical Trial Assessment of Infrastructure Matrix, a self-assessment tool for community research sites, and the implementation of ISO 9001 in clinical trial centers [30, 31].

### 1.4.1 Instruments written for the sponsor and the trial

Several organizations were created in the last two decades with the explicit aim of improving how clinical trials are run. TransCelerate BioPharma is a nonprofit organization founded in 2012 by a group of biopharmaceutical companies to develop shared solutions to problems common to their research and development, and it produced a conceptual framework for a clinical quality management system, together with a set of RBM frameworks [32]. The Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative (CTTI) is a public-private partnership founded in 2007 by the United States Food and Drug Administration and Duke University to identify and promote practices that increase the quality and efficiency of clinical trials, and it issued QbD recommendations with the methodological tools that support them [33]. The Medical Device Innovation Consortium (MDIC), founded in 2012 as a public-private partnership for regulatory science in the medical device sector, extended the CTTI approach to devices in a supplement on CtQ factors, which recognizes that device investigations can involve learning curves in product use [34].

Despite their different origins, the three initiatives write for the same reader, the sponsor. Their documents answer the questions a sponsor faces when it plans a trial, such as which factors are critical to the quality of a given protocol and where monitoring effort should go, and they also cover how oversight is coordinated across several sites. The site appears in them mainly as the place where these decisions are carried out and checked, for example through on-site and central monitoring. The documents are detailed and technically mature, but they say little about how a site should organize the quality of its own work, so the site receives controls that were designed elsewhere.

A separate body of work addresses the quality of the surgical intervention itself. A systematic review of oncology trials classified the reported challenges to surgical quality assurance (SQA) into three domains: credentialing, standardization, and monitoring. It concluded that the implementation and reporting of effective measures were inadequate [35]. The Randomised Oesophagectomy: Minimally Invasive or Open (ROMIO) study then developed concrete methods, including entry criteria for surgeons through the assessment of operative videos, standardization of operative technique, and structured monitoring [36]. A Delphi process with 21 stakeholders produced 59 strategies to overcome the challenges to SQA in oncology trials, of which 14 gained consensus within an active randomized trial [37].

These proposals have not yet translated into consistent practice. In 2024, a multinational panel reviewed randomized trials of arteriovenous grafts for hemodialysis, a surgically implanted device, and assessed their quality assurance (QA) in the domains of the oncology review, credentialing, standardization, and monitoring, together with a generic domain. QA was highly variable and often poorly described, and it had not improved across the three decades of trials examined. Few trials defined the experience a surgeon needed before taking part, and none had a monitoring program defined before the trial or assessed the technical performance of its surgeons [39]. The oncology review that first organized these domains had reached a similar conclusion, finding that effective SQA measures were inadequately implemented and reported [35]. The field has therefore proposed many quality measures for surgical trials, and the trials examined rarely carried them through to conduct.

The SQA frameworks act at the level of trial design, where the trial team defines what it expects of surgeons and sites, while the step that most often failed came afterward, during conduct, when none of the arteriovenous graft trials assessed the technical performance of its surgeons [39]. That step takes place at the site, which runs the investigation every day, while the frameworks are held by the trial team and applied to the site from outside. In multicenter trials this centralization is a deliberate choice, because the aim is a common standard across sites, but it leaves the site without an instrument of its own to observe and correct its conduct while the trial runs.

### 1.4.2 Instruments that assess the site from outside

Instruments that measure the site itself are more recent. In 2025, a multicenter study in six Italian academic hospitals noted that methods for assessing the performance of clinical trial sites remain underdeveloped and developed the Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure to fill that space [41]. Starting from 126 candidate indicators drawn from a systematic review and expert consultation, the authors used statistical validation to retain 18, organized into four domains: participant retention and consent, data completeness and timeliness, AE reporting, and protocol compliance. They present the measure as a tool for benchmarking and resource allocation across a network, including the identification of underperforming sites [41].

A performance score of this kind helps a sponsor or a network decide which sites need attention, because it compares sites against each other once their results are known. A site that wants to notice that one of its own processes is drifting, while there is still time to act, needs a different kind of instrument, one that it operates itself and reads during conduct. In the literature reviewed, this second purpose has received much less attention than the first.

Several studies describe site-level quality management as reactive and ad hoc, and they connect this to persistent problems with data quality [42, 43, 44]. Sites differ widely in their rates of deviation reporting and AE reporting, and standardized approaches are still uncommon. In one mature research network, only about half of the participating sites used a standardized method for identifying AEs [45]. Sites value RBM, but they often experience it as an external requirement. It reaches them through the sponsor and the contract research organization (CRO), and they have little control over it [46, 47].

A device trial shows how this external RBM works in practice. In 2017, BIOTRONIK, a manufacturer of cardiac rhythm devices, reported on the RBM strategy it applied as sponsor to its trials of implantable cardiac devices, which covered 133 investigator sites and 3,442 participants [48]. The company scored each site on weighted risk factors, including follow-up compliance and missing critical data, together with protocol noncompliance, and used the ranking to decide where to send on-site monitoring visits. After one year, the worst-performing sites showed statistically significant improvement in most of these factors, although participant attrition did not improve significantly. The evaluation followed trends over time, with no comparison group reported, so the improvement cannot be attributed to RBM alone. It does show that a sponsor can improve the data that reach it by ranking sites and targeting its visits, while the site takes part only as the object being ranked and visited.

The literature therefore suggests an asymmetry between the three main parties to an investigation. Sponsors ordinarily operate a documented quality framework, and CROs carry out monitoring and oversight on the sponsor’s behalf, while many of the site workflows described in the reviewed studies were handled case by case and were not part of an integrated local quality-management structure. This conclusion comes from the reviewed corpus and is not a prevalence estimate. It is consistent with the gaps found in the regulatory texts, which Chapter 3 reports.

## 1.5 Aim, Objectives, and Scope

ICH E6(R3) and ISO 14155:2026 give the investigational site a larger share of the responsibility for quality, but most of the instruments reviewed here were built for the sponsor. This thesis derives a site-level quality framework that complements the sponsor’s system, so that a site can act on the risks the sponsor cannot see in time and can show how it controlled its own conduct. The framework turns the QbD and risk-proportionate oversight principles of ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) into practices that a site can actually run across the study lifecycle, and it is meant to sit alongside the obligations set by the MDR and the requirements of ISO 14155:2026.

The work builds on the literature described in Section 1.4. SQA frameworks define what a trial should require of a site, and site performance measures define how another party should assess it, so this thesis addresses what a site should operate for itself. The work has four objectives:

1. To compare ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and MDR Annex XV clause by clause, identifying where the instruments agree, where they differ, and where none creates a structured obligation for the investigational site.
2. To identify the CtQ domains that apply at site level, as distinct from the trial-level CtQ factors defined by the sponsor.
3. To derive an explicit and traceable set of requirements for a site-level quality framework from the regulatory analysis, from the operational gaps reported in the literature, and from the operational conditions of surgical device investigations.
4. To build that framework and show how each component answers a stated requirement and how it relates to prior work that addresses the same subject at a different level.

The work was narrowed to orthopedic investigations, because the MDR classifies joint replacements as class III devices and generally requires clinical investigations for them, so a new joint replacement normally reaches the market only after investigations conducted at surgical sites [10]. Within orthopedics, the work focuses on the shoulder, because a framework is usable only if it reaches operational detail, which cannot be achieved across several surgical disciplines at once, and because the shoulder is a widely studied area [20, 21, 26, 27] with a large base of registered trials.

The registry characterization developed for this thesis found that nonhospital organizations made up more than a quarter of the organizations taking part in industry-sponsored shoulder device investigations, and that for more than half of them no permanent research structure was publicly identifiable. The framework was therefore designed for private centers that run investigations recurrently alongside surgical care without a system dedicated to investigation quality.

Finally, this is a design study, whose principal result is an artifact built from documented evidence and reasoned requirements, and Section 2.6 states what that does and does not establish.
