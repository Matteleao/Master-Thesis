# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Historical Overview and Recent Updates

Over the past decade, quality management in clinical research has changed [1, 2]. The older model was mainly reactive. This means that sites and sponsors would find problems during monitoring visits and audits, and then corrected them case by case. These activities ran throughout the trial, but their purpose was detection and verification. Quality was not designed into the trial from the start.

Current guidance takes a different position: quality should be considered before a study begins, built into operational decisions during conduct, and managed across the study lifecycle [3, 4]. This change can be followed through the guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH). ICH E6, adopted in 1996, defined the responsibilities of sponsors, investigators, and ethics committees through detailed procedural requirements. ICH E6(R2), adopted in 2016, added an explicit quality-management section and risk-based monitoring (RBM), recognizing that the same level of oversight cannot usefully be applied to every activity [3].

In the past, the main question was how a study should be monitored. When ICH E8(R1) was adopted in 2021, it asked a different question: how should a study be designed so that the factors needed for reliable results are protected from the start? The guideline formalized Quality by Design (QbD) in clinical research. QbD holds that quality cannot be guaranteed by checking a study after it has happened; it has to be planned from the beginning. Planning means identifying the factors that matter most to participant protection and the reliability of the study, and allocating resources in proportion to the risks that threaten them. ICH E8(R1) gave this idea an operational form through Critical-to-Quality (CtQ) factors [5].

ICH E6(R3) reached Step 4 on 6 January 2025 and reinforced the direction established by ICH E8(R1). It gave greater weight to QbD, CtQ factors, risk-proportionate oversight, and quality management across the trial lifecycle [6]. It also requires investigators to maintain appropriate site processes and to ensure that qualified staff and adequate resources support trial conduct [6].

Commentators have read this development as a move from a prescriptive model toward a governance model [7]. In a prescriptive model, a site shows quality mainly by documenting that it followed the required procedures. In a governance model, the responsible parties have to design, run, and improve a system that is proportionate to the risks of the investigation. The difference matters in practice. Under the second model, a site also has to show that its processes can identify risks, control them, and learn from them.

Although the ICH guidelines are written broadly, their principal Good Clinical Practice (GCP) scope is interventional trials of investigational products, and their vocabulary reflects that origin [6]. Other study types therefore require instruments written for their own conditions. Clinical investigations of medical devices are governed by International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 14155, the GCP standard for that field [8].

ISO 14155 has followed a parallel path. It was first published in 1996 and was revised in 2011, 2020, and 2026 [8]. The fourth edition was published on 23 March 2026, after the research for this thesis had started. It strengthens residual-risk assessment, procedure-related risks, oversight, safety reporting, missing data, estimands, clinical performance, and data integrity. It also distinguishes more clearly between residual risks of the investigational device, which retain the logic of ISO 14971, and risks arising from clinical procedures or investigation processes outside routine clinical practice. However, the edition carrying a presumption of conformity in the European Union (EU) remains European Standard (EN) ISO 14155:2020+A11:2024, as listed in Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/193 [9]. ISO 14155:2026 is therefore used here as the current international standard, not described as the harmonized European edition.

Regional regulations supply legal force. In the EU, Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices (MDR) governs clinical investigations and establishes requirements for authorization, conduct, documentation, and reporting [10]. The United States uses a separate statutory and regulatory framework under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act [11] and Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations [12]. The two regional frameworks are mentioned here as distinct legal contexts; this thesis does not treat them as equivalent.

Despite the clear trend and direction from the guidelines, adoption in practice is still uneven. A global survey of risk-based quality management (RBQM) maturity found that identifying CtQ factors was one of the least adopted components overall, with non-adoption concentrated in the Late Majority and Resisters cohorts, which together comprised nearly half of the surveyed organizations [13]. The survey does not show how sites apply CtQ principles, because sites were not its object. It does show that the first step of the QbD approach is still difficult at sponsor level, where the concept was developed and where resources are usually concentrated.

These instruments converge, and together they raise the standard that an investigational site is expected to meet. They also leave a question open. If a site must now operate processes that identify risks, control them, and learn from them, how should it organize itself to do that in daily practice? The rest of this chapter examines why the question is hard in surgical device investigations, and what the field has so far offered in answer.

## 1.2 Quality Responsibility at the Investigational Site

Risk-proportionate oversight starts from the observation that the activities in a clinical study do not all carry the same risk. Monitoring, documentation, and oversight should therefore match the importance of the activity, the reliability of the data it produces, and the consequences of failure. In practice this asks a study team to do four things:

1- Identify and validate the CtQ factors.

2- Assess risk at study level and at site level.

3- Define controls that prevent, detect, and respond.

4- Set measurable indicators that trigger proportionate action when a signal appears.

Key risk indicators, quality tolerance limits, Failure Modes and Effects Analysis, and risk matrices based on impact, probability, and detectability give this work a traceable basis.

Sponsors retain primary responsibility for trial-level quality management and oversight [6, 8, 10]. Investigators, in turn, are responsible for the conduct and oversight of activities delegated at the site, including ensuring qualified staff and adequate resources [6, 8]. The site is therefore directly accountable for the quality of the investigation it conducts, while the sponsor’s quality-management responsibility remains intact.

This development reflects operational reality. A large part of a clinical investigation happens at site level, through decisions and processes that the protocol only partly specifies. Quality can be strengthened or damaged by the routine work of the site in activities such as how it identifies potentially eligible participants, obtains consent, confirms eligibility, delivers the intervention, collects outcome data, records adverse events (AEs), and maintains source documentation. The site is therefore more than the place where sponsor-designed processes are carried out. It contributes actively to the quality of the investigation.

Accountability of this kind has to be operational to be real. If a site is expected to act as an active agent of quality, then the role has to be defined, structured, and made verifiable. Regulatory texts set out principles and responsibilities. They do not say how each site should turn those principles into daily practice, and that translation is the subject of this thesis.

## 1.3 Surgical Device Investigations as a Distinct Methodological Context

Clinical research methodology and its regulatory infrastructure were developed largely around pharmacological interventions, whereas implantable devices raise additional design and evaluation problems [14, 15]. In the pharmacological model, the intervention is a chemical compound given at a defined dose, on a defined schedule, and by a defined route. Surgical clinical investigations of medical devices work under different conditions. Five of these conditions change what quality management has to do at the operational level.

For the purposes of this thesis, the first condition is termed irreversibility. A prosthesis fixed to bone cannot simply be stopped or withdrawn in the way a medicinal product can, and errors during implantation may have lasting consequences. The cited literature documents the distinctive evidential and methodological consequences of implantable devices, although it does not itself use “irreversibility” as a formal category [14, 16, 17].

Operator dependence, the second condition, means that outcomes may depend substantially on the surgeon, with variation occurring both between surgeons and within the practice of a single surgeon as experience grows along the learning curve [18, 19, 20, 21, 22].

The third condition is traceability. Under the MDR, documentation and record-retention duties support traceability of the individual investigational device [10]. ISO 14155 likewise addresses investigational-device accountability and traceability [8]. This claim does not rely on MDR Article 27, because that article excludes investigational devices from its Unique Device Identification (UDI) assignment requirement. In surgical investigations, the framework extends the operational record to the surgical decisions that produced the device configuration in a given patient.

Follow-up carries substantial operational weight, the fourth condition. Range-of-motion (ROM) assessments, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), and radiographic evaluations often require repeated attendance over several years and consistent assessment procedures. Orthopaedic studies also show that loss to follow-up is common enough to threaten interpretation when it is not defined and managed consistently [23, 24].

The fifth of these conditions, measurement reliability, follows from limited inter-observer reliability in the radiological and clinical classifications on which eligibility and outcome assessment may depend, so measurement variability is present before any additional site-level variability is introduced [25, 26, 27].

Within this context, we narrowed the work to orthopaedic investigations and, within them, to the shoulder for two reasons. The first is evidential. A ClinicalTrials.gov Application Programming Interface (API) search performed on 20 September 2026 for interventional studies using the condition string “shoulder arthroplasty OR rotator cuff OR shoulder prosthesis” returned 996 records. This retrieval count indicates a substantial registry base; it is not the final analytic sample described in Section 2.4. The second reason is methodological. A framework is usable only if it reaches operational detail, and that level of detail cannot be achieved across several surgical disciplines at once. The framework is therefore developed for one discipline. Its structural logic may be transferable to other implant-based surgical specialties with comparable conditions, but this thesis does not demonstrate that transfer.

The registry characterization developed for this thesis identified 235 distinct organizations, of which 65 (27.7%) were non-hospital organizations. Among those 65, 38 (58.5%) had no documented permanent research structure, and 60 (92.3%) were located in the United States. These results support treating resource-constrained non-hospital sites as a substantial, but not majority, part of the design population. They do not establish that sites share staff, rooms, workflows, or operating lists with routine care. Those are design assumptions that the registry did not test, and Section 3.4.4 states them explicitly. The framework was therefore designed for sites whose research processes must coexist with the resources and systems used for surgical care.

## 1.4 What Has Been Built for Sites, and for Whom

Clinical research has built a growing body of operational quality infrastructure over the past two decades, including methods for central monitoring and instruments for assessing site readiness [28, 29]. Much of the material reviewed for this thesis is defined at trial or network level and applied to the site from outside. There are important exceptions, including the Clinical Trial Assessment of Infrastructure Matrix and the implementation of ISO 9001 in trial centers [30, 31]. The narrower conclusion supported by the reviewed corpus is that relatively few instruments are designed for a site to operate on its own processes.

### 1.4.1 Instruments written for the sponsor and the trial

TransCelerate BioPharma developed a clinical quality management system (QMS) and a set of RBM frameworks [32]. The Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative (CTTI) issued QbD recommendations, together with the methodological tools that support them [33]. Device-specific guidance is available in the Medical Device Innovation Consortium (MDIC) supplement to the CtQ framework, which recognizes that device investigations can involve learning curves in product use [34]. These initiatives are detailed and technically mature, and they are written for the sponsor. They explain how a sponsor identifies CtQ factors, allocates monitoring resources in proportion to risk, and coordinates oversight across several sites. In this model, the site receives controls that were designed elsewhere.

A separate body of work addresses the quality of the surgical intervention itself, and its trajectory is instructive. A systematic review of oncology trials classified the reported challenges to surgical quality assurance (SQA) into three domains: credentialing, standardization, and monitoring. It concluded that the implementation and reporting of effective measures were inadequate [35]. The Randomised Oesophagectomy: Minimally Invasive or Open (ROMIO) study then developed concrete methods, including entry criteria for surgeons through the assessment of operative videos, standardization of operative technique, and structured monitoring [36]. International expert consensus followed. A Delphi process with 21 stakeholders produced 59 strategies to overcome the challenges to SQA in oncology trials, of which 14 gained consensus within an active randomized trial [37]. A parallel Delphi process with 40 experts from 15 countries produced a 13-item checklist for gastrointestinal surgery [38].

Two findings limit what this work has achieved. A systematic review of randomized trials of arteriovenous grafts applied the same domains outside oncology and found that quality assurance (QA) was highly variable, poorly described, and had not improved over three decades. No trial in that review had a defined pre-trial monitoring program, and none assessed technical performance [39]. A systematic review of 109 quality improvement studies in surgical oncology found that only 2 of 12 randomized trials demonstrated an effect on the primary outcome, compared with 86 of 97 non-randomized studies. Of the included studies, 74 were uncontrolled before-and-after designs assessed as low quality [40]. The field has therefore proposed a great deal and validated very little. The more important limitation for this thesis is one of address. Every framework in this line is designed at trial level, by the trial team, and applied to the site. The site is the object of these measures. It is not the party that operates them.

### 1.4.2 Instruments that assess the site from outside

A third body of work measures the site. Recent studies have noted the absence of standardized instruments for assessing the performance of clinical trial sites and have begun to develop validated measures [29]. The Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure reduced 126 candidate indicators to 18, organized into four validated domains: participant retention and consent, data completeness and timeliness, AE reporting, and protocol compliance [41].

These instruments are rigorous, and they serve a real purpose. That purpose is benchmarking and resource allocation across a network, so they are applied from outside to assess a site on behalf of another party. A performance score of this kind can help a sponsor decide which sites need attention. It does not help a site notice that one of its own processes is drifting while there is still time to act. The two purposes need different instruments, and the second has received much less attention.

The empirical literature explains what fills the space in the meantime. Several studies describe site-level quality management as reactive and ad hoc, and they connect this to persistent problems with data quality [42, 43, 44]. Sites differ widely in their rates of deviation reporting and AE reporting, and standardized approaches are still uncommon. In one mature research network, only about half of the participating sites used a standardized method for identifying AEs [45]. Sites value RBM, but they often experience it as an external requirement. It reaches them through the sponsor and the contract research organization (CRO), and they have little control over it [46, 47].

The literature suggests an asymmetry between the three main parties to an investigation. Sponsors ordinarily operate a documented quality framework, and CROs conduct monitoring, verification, and oversight on the sponsor’s behalf. In a small Australian survey, site-level data-quality management was often reactive and ad hoc [42]. Within the body of work reviewed here, many site workflows were not described as parts of an integrated local quality-management structure. This is an analytical conclusion from the reviewed corpus, not a worldwide prevalence estimate. It defines the gap addressed by this thesis.

The structurally closest prior work connects site-level risk management, implantable devices, and investigation quality. It showed that RBM improved data quality in trials of implantable cardiac devices [48]. This provides a direct precedent for RBQM in implantable-device investigations. Orthopaedic investigations add operator-dependent and learning-curve considerations documented in the surgical-methodology literature [18, 19]. The comparison is used to identify different operational conditions, not to claim that one specialty is more operator dependent than another.

## 1.5 Aim, Objectives, and Scope

This thesis aims to develop an operational quality architecture that investigational sites can adopt, document, and review across the study lifecycle when they conduct clinical investigations of medical devices. The architecture translates the QbD and risk-proportionate oversight principles described in ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) into practices that a site can operate, and it is designed to complement the obligations established by International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 14155:2026 and Regulation (EU) 2017/745.

The work builds on the bodies of literature described in Section 1.4. SQA frameworks define what a trial should require of a site. Site performance measures define how another party should assess a site. This thesis defines what a site should operate for itself.

The thesis has four objectives. The first is to compare ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and MDR Annex XV clause by clause. The comparison identifies where the instruments agree, where they differ, and where none creates a structured obligation for the investigational site. The second is to identify the CtQ domains that apply at site level, as distinct from the trial-level CtQ factors defined by the sponsor. The third is to derive an explicit and traceable set of requirements for a site-level quality architecture. The derivation uses the regulatory analysis, the operational gaps already reported in the literature, and the operational conditions of surgical device investigations. The fourth is to build that architecture and to show how each component answers a stated requirement, and how it relates to the prior work that addresses the same subject at a different level.

The work has three defined boundaries. The first is that it operates at site level and stays complementary to the sponsor's quality system. It deals with the site's own processes, it supports sponsor-led quality activities, and it claims no authority over the protocol. The second is that it focuses on shoulder device investigations, for the reasons given in Section 1.3, while considering whether its structural logic could transfer to other surgical disciplines with comparable characteristics. The third is that it is a design study. Its principal result is an artifact derived from documented evidence and from reasoned requirements, and Section 2.6 states what that does and does not establish.
