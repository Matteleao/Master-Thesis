## 4.3 Pillar 2: Risk Management

Risk Management answers Gap 2, the absence of a record in which the site holds the risks that its own execution creates, and it also carries the monitoring of the learning curve that Gap 3 calls for. It answers DR2 and DR3 and part of DR6 and DR8, and because every domain carries execution risks of its own, it covers all twelve CtQ domains. The pillar works out where the site’s conduct is most likely to fail and how each failure is controlled before it reaches participants or the data, and it limits that work to the risks that threaten CtQ factors.

Risk in a device investigation sits at three levels, and the texts examined give an owner to the first two. At device level the manufacturer manages risk under ISO 14971, and under the MDR the CIP sets out the risks and clinical benefits of the device under investigation. At trial level, ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) ask the sponsor to manage the risks to critical factors as a cycle that runs from identification to review, and the nonbinding EMA reflection paper describes the same cycle in operational terms. ISO 14155:2026 keeps the risks of using the device within ISO 14971 and places two further sources outside it, the clinical procedures the CIP requires beyond routine practice, which the fourth edition added, and the conduct of the investigation itself. It gives all of them to the sponsor.

The third level, the risk that a specific site’s execution creates, has no owner in any of these texts. Much of the procedural and process risk arises from the way the site carries out the protocol, and the sponsor’s cycle cannot be built around the weaknesses of one center, because it has to work across centers that differ in staff and practice. In an orthopedic device investigation this local risk is clinical and methodological at once, since most of the structural conditions of surgical investigation act at the site, from operator dependence to the limited reliability of classifications, and the first signs of device-related complications also appear there. The site knows where its own process is strong and where it is weak, so it is the party best placed to identify these risks and control them.

### 4.3.1 The site risk register

A 2023 survey of 46 Portuguese clinical trial sites measured how far sites keep a record of their own risks [77]. Although 57.0% of the sites reported using a systematic risk management tool, only nine (19.6%) described a standard tool or document that captured the analysis of risk at site level. Most sites (87.0%) were willing to use a tool built for their own operational needs, and lack of knowledge of risk management was the main reason given against putting one in place. It is one national survey, and it is the closest measurement of Gap 2 that we found.

The site risk register holds the risks that the site’s own execution creates. Some arise around surgery, such as incomplete capture of implant identifiers or late documentation of an intraoperative deviation, and others appear afterward, when images are acquired inconsistently or follow-up visits fall outside their windows. Its value is preventive, so the site opens it before the first participant is enrolled. At that point the site turns the CtQ domains into the CtQ factors of the investigation at hand, identifying within each domain the factors that its own conduct can affect from the protocol and from what the sponsor communicates about critical factors and risks. Each local risk then enters the register with its cause and consequence and with the factor it threatens.

Each risk is rated by the impact it would have on its CtQ factor and by how likely it is to occur and to go undetected, following the risk logic of ICH E8(R1). Each risk also has a named owner from the role structure, together with the control in place and the residual risk that remains. The rating keeps the register short enough to review at each cycle, and the named owner has the authority to act on what it shows. Each risk carries its own review trigger, and the PI reads the register at each oversight cycle, so a change in the site’s conditions reaches the register before it causes a failure. A risk whose possible effect reaches beyond the site is escalated to the sponsor. The register therefore covers each element that DR2 asks for, from identification to reassessment, and the Site Risk Register (SRR) holds each risk with the factor it threatens and its control plan (Appendix H).

### 4.3.2 Operator-dependent risk and the learning curve

In a device investigation the surgical procedure is part of the intervention, so variation in how each surgeon performs it is a risk that a medicinal product does not carry. Surgeon-dependent variation reaches protocol adherence and implant traceability as well as the endpoints and clinical outcomes, and it is shared by all the cases of one surgeon, because they share that surgeon’s technique and decision thresholds. The instruments compared in this thesis control operator learning at entry and give the site no role in observing it during conduct (Gap 3). This component covers that interval and answers DR3, operator proficiency monitored during conduct, specific to surgeon, procedure, and device.

The literature links experience with shorter operative times and fewer complications, together with better PROM scores [20], but studies define and measure proficiency in different ways. A systematic review of 13 studies estimated that surgeons needed about 25 reverse total shoulder arthroplasties to reach an acceptable position on the learning curve and gave a range of 16 to 86 cases for anatomic arthroplasty, which only two studies had examined [20]. In a multicenter analysis of operative duration, three of four early-career surgeons reached a plateau between 12 and 86 cases [78]. Both figures describe how learning progressed in those samples, and the framework does not adopt them as a proficiency threshold or set a fixed case count of its own.

The tracking asks whether surgeon-dependent variation is becoming a material risk to the CtQ factors of the investigation at this site. ICH E8(R1) asks for periodic review of CtQ factors during a study to decide whether risk controls need adjusting, because new or unanticipated issues arise once the study has begun, and the learning curve is one such issue at a surgical site. SQA frameworks address the learning curve mainly at selection [37, 39], and the Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure has no indicator for a surgeon’s procedural consistency once a trial is under way [41].

Before the first case, the site fixes how cases will be compared. It defines the levels of case complexity that matter for the investigation, with the operative duration expected for comparable cases, and it states the support, such as proctoring, that the surgeon should have early on. Each case then enters with its complexity and operative duration, together with any unplanned event during the procedure, such as a component substitution or a need for senior support. A slower operation or an unplanned substitution means different things in a complex case and in a routine one, and early in a surgeon’s experience with the device and later, so each case is read against the surgeon’s own earlier cases of the same complexity. Comparing a surgeon with that surgeon’s own record avoids most of the case-mix problem that comparisons between surgeons carry, although a change in case complexity over time still has to be taken into account. The site reviews the sequence after the first cases and at set points afterward, and it opens an assessment earlier when a pattern appears, such as repeated substitutions or a cluster of early technical complications. A signal from complication monitoring is also read against this record before it is attributed to the device or to the operator.

Each review either confirms that execution is stable or leads to a response of increasing weight, from closer observation at shorter intervals to local controls such as renewed device training or temporary proctoring, and to formal escalation when a pattern is material or recurrent. A restriction of the surgeon’s scope is a change of authorization, so it goes to the credentialing decision of the Governance pillar, and a material pattern enters the site risk register or corrective and preventive action. Because the record is kept as cases occur, it can show procedural drift before it appears as an AE or a PD, and it holds data from the point of care that cannot be rebuilt later, which is part of what DR6 asks. The Surgeon Learning Curve Tracking Log (SLCT) holds the case sequence against its basis of comparison, with each review and its disposition (Appendix I).

### 4.3.3 Device-specific complication monitoring

Complication monitoring follows the clinical events of the investigation that may relate to the investigational device or to the procedure, including the way the two interact in clinical use. It covers AEs and DDs, and the AEs it follows most closely are complications, those that arise from the surgical procedure or from the device. An early complication whose relationship to the device is still undetermined is also data from the point of care that cannot be recovered once lost (Section 3.4.3). The component answers DR8 and part of DR6, and it protects CtQ domain 10, safety surveillance and adverse device event management.

Each event already has a reporting route, and the route starts at the site. ISO 14155:2026 asks the PI to record every AE and observed DD with its categorization and to report the serious ones to the sponsor without unjustified delay, with the detail the CIP specifies, and since the fourth edition an AE associated with a DD follows both the event pathway and the deficiency pathway. Under the MDR the sponsor then reports without delay any serious adverse event (SAE) that has, or may reasonably have, a causal relationship with the investigational device, the comparator, or the investigation procedure, together with device deficiencies that might have led to an SAE and new findings on these events. These duties treat each event on its own, and none of the three instruments compared in this thesis asks the site to review its events together for a pattern.

Several events that each have a plausible explanation may still share a cause, such as one implant component or one surgeon, and the shared cause becomes visible only when the events are reviewed together. If the site waited for the sponsor’s reporting cycle, several more cases could share that cause before anyone saw it, so the framework has the site review its own events on a cadence it sets, at least once a month, independent of the sponsor’s calendar. Complication monitoring starts from events that have already happened to participants. The site risk register works earlier, on weaknesses in a process, such as an implant identifier that may not be captured before the operating room workflow closes.

Each event enters a site log as it occurs, whether it is first seen in the operating room or at a later clinical contact, with references to its source records and its safety route, so the log indexes the events without repeating their content. At entry the site records a preliminary assessment of what the event may relate to, which the investigator’s final classification later confirms or replaces. At each review the site counts the events by category, and by component and surgeon, against the number of procedures performed, so each count is read as a rate. A signal is a rate above a threshold the site set in advance or a sharp rise since the previous period, and the review also flags a category of event not seen before and events that concentrate in one surgeon. Each flagged signal is assessed for its likely cause. A signal tied to one surgeon goes to the learning curve review, and one that points to the device goes to the sponsor through the deficiency route. A pattern in a site process enters the site risk register or corrective and preventive action.

Under ISO 14155:2026 the sponsor considers establishing a CEC, which reviews and classifies events across all participating centers by consensus. The site review is a local counterpart with the site’s own denominator and triggers. We found no evidence that either level detects a signal first, and the framework gives them different roles. The site acts when its own trigger is met. A local signal that may matter beyond the site goes to the sponsor, where it feeds the assessment that the CEC keeps consistent across the investigation. The Site Complication Monitoring Log (SCML) indexes each event with its route (Appendix J), and the Safety and Adverse Effect Reporting Template (SART) follows each safety case from opening to closure (Appendix K). The Complication Signal Surveillance Template (CSST) holds each periodic review with its counts and the signals it found (Appendix L).

## 4.4 Pillar 3: Operational Controls

Operational Controls set how critical activities are carried out and checked in routine operation, so that each procedure is done the same way from case to case and leaves a record from which it can be reconstructed. The pillar answers Gap 4, the absence of a monitoring function that the site starts and runs itself, together with DR4 and DR7 and part of DR6 and DR11. Under the MDR all information on the investigation has to be recorded and stored so that it can be accurately reported, interpreted, and verified, and the CIP sets the policy on deviations and on the accountability of the device. ISO 14155:2026 turns these duties into requirements on source documents and data at the site, together with the accountability of each device.

A procedure as the protocol describes it and the same procedure as a team carries it out on a given day in the operating room rarely match exactly, and this pillar works on the distance between them. Part of that distance is hard for an outside party to see in time, because local conduct can drift between sponsor visits and differences in imaging often become clear only when images from different time points are placed side by side.

### 4.4.1 Internal verification of site conduct

Internal verification is a function that the site runs itself to review its own conduct and documentation at set intervals, so that a local failure is found and corrected inside the site’s own sphere of responsibility, and it answers DR4. Under the MDR the sponsor ensures adequate monitoring through a monitor who is independent of the investigational site, and it shows compliance with GCP, for instance through internal or external inspection. ISO 14155:2026 and ICH E6(R3) likewise place monitoring within the sponsor’s quality control, and FDA guidance on risk-based monitoring addresses the same sponsor oversight [79]. The instruments compared in this thesis ask the site to support that monitoring and to apply the corrections it receives, and none of them asks the site to check its own conduct between visits (Gap 4). Internal verification covers that interval and replaces no part of the sponsor’s monitoring, which the law keeps independent of the site.

The review concentrates where execution most often goes wrong in an implant investigation, at the points where operating room practice meets implant logistics and documentation, and later in imaging and follow-up. Review effort therefore follows the risk to participants and to the data, as risk-based monitoring asks of the sponsor, and ten checks set its scope.

- Sterile release to use. Package integrity, expiry, and storage are confirmed before use, and any breach of sterile handling or intraoperative substitution is reconciled.

- Implant traceability and component reconciliation. Lot number, serial number, or UDI agree across the operative note, the accountability record, the implant labels, and the CRF, and the implanted configuration matches the plan.

- Instrumentation and operating room readiness. The correct implant system and instrument set were available, and any incompatibility or malfunction is documented as a procedural issue or, where it applies, as a DD.

- Operative documentation. The operative note has enough detail to interpret the final configuration and the intraoperative decisions later, including substitutions, deviations, and complications, because this record cannot be repaired afterward.

- Imaging usability and timing. Required imaging was obtained within its window with the correct views and quality, and it was repeated when it was not.

- Follow-up execution. Each visit produced the outputs that surveillance and endpoint assessment need.

- Endpoint data agreement. For the endpoint-critical fields that are recorded first outside the CRF, a sample of CRF entries agrees with the source, and any discrepancy is corrected and explained.

- Timeliness and closure. Notes, data entry, queries, and corrective actions close within the set times. The EMA reflection paper gives delays in data entry and in SAE reporting as examples of metrics that may trigger targeted monitoring, but it does not show that a backlog predicts a broader failure at the site, so the framework treats delay as a signal to check.

- External context. The site reviews literature, registry findings, safety alerts, and field notices for the same implant class or procedure.

- Complication monitoring. The complication reviews ran on their cadence and their signals were followed up, while the analysis of the pattern itself stays with complication monitoring.

Before the first participant is enrolled, the site sets for each check how often it is reviewed and on what sample of cases, together with the person who reviews it and the trigger for an earlier review. The reviewer should not be the person whose work is under review. Where the staff allow it, review is done by someone separate from daily execution, and in a small team it is shared with a trained peer or kept under documented PI oversight. Each review covers a period or answers a trigger, across all ten checks or a selected part of them, and for each check the reviewer records whether the planned control was carried out and whether the record can confirm it, and then whether the control worked. A negative or uncertain finding always leads to an action or a linked record, and the follow-up confirms that the correction worked. Because the review runs on a cadence independent of sponsor visits, it also serves as the periodic self-inspection that DR5 asks for, and the findings that need correction go to corrective and preventive action under Performance Oversight. The Internal Monitoring Plan (IMP) sets the scope and cadence of the review (Appendix M), and the Internal Monitoring Checklist (IMC) records each review with its findings and actions (Appendix N).

### 4.4.2 Imaging standardization

Imaging standardization controls the variation that the site itself can introduce into imaging-derived information, wherever an image supports a clinical or study decision. It answers DR7 for CtQ domain 9, imaging acquisition, interpretation and endpoint control. In a shoulder investigation images are used from the confirmation of anatomical suitability before surgery to the position of the components at follow-up, and the classifications applied to them already have limited reliability. Variation in how the images are acquired therefore adds error that the database cannot separate from real clinical difference.

Trial-imaging guidance has developed mainly outside orthopedics, in core-imaging and central-reading work that specifies acquisition, equipment, and site qualification in advance, together with the split between local and central reading [56, 61, 80]. Shoulder studies show how much the acquisition itself matters. In images reconstructed from cadaver scapulae, views only 5 degrees from true anteversion changed the critical shoulder angle by more than 2 degrees [81], and in a cadaveric study measurements stayed stable only within about 9 degrees of malrotation [82]. In routine practice only 22% of anteroposterior films met the standard projection [83]. Simulated changes in scapular position produced ranges of 14 and 17 degrees in arthroplasty inclination measures [85], while standardized outlet views reached an interobserver intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.91 for the acromiohumeral distance [84].

The component has four elements, and each one works within the sponsor’s imaging charter, so that the site never keeps a second standard of its own. First, the site maps each requirement of the charter, such as the views and the acquisition window at each time point, onto a workflow its own radiology can follow, records any gap, and submits this readiness assessment to the sponsor before activation, which keeps the decision to activate with the sponsor. Second, the people who acquire and assess the images are qualified and trained for these requirements and released through the training records of the Governance pillar, and where a measurement depends on the assessor, agreement between assessors is tested before the first participant. ISO 14155:2026 also makes the PI responsible for the maintenance and calibration of the equipment used in the investigation, and its fourth edition added calibration to the content of the CIP.

Third, before the first image is acquired, the site fixes who reviews its images locally and against which criteria, and how a non-conforming image is handled, including when a second reviewer or clinical escalation is needed. Each required image set is then logged as it is acquired, with its timing and technical quality and the reference that makes it retrievable, and an image set that falls short leads to a repeat acquisition or a second review while either can still help. Central review, where the sponsor uses it, needs coordination across sites and stays outside the site’s control, so the site can claim only the consistency of its own review. Fourth, the site schedules each image within the window the CIP sets and at a moment when a reproducible position can be obtained. The studies above show that positioning drives the measurement and that inadequate positioning is common, but we found no study on how often pain, dressings, or a sling prevent a reproducible film after surgery, so the framework treats this as an operational inference. Where a scheduled time proves unsuitable, the site documents it and raises it with the sponsor, which can define an acceptable window. The Site Imaging Readiness Record (SIRR) holds the mapping of the charter and the readiness assessment (Appendix O), and the Imaging Quality Log (IQL) holds the record of each image set (Appendix P).

### 4.4.3 Eligibility and follow-up records

Two points in an investigation depend on records that the site makes about one participant at a time, the decision to enroll and each follow-up visit. They protect CtQ domains 2 and 7, participant selection and follow-up retention, and the eligibility record answers the part of DR7 that concerns structured verification of eligibility. In a shoulder investigation eligibility often depends on anatomy read from images and on the planned procedure, and an error at inclusion can become irreversible once the implant is in place.

The site documents each eligibility decision criterion by criterion against the current CIP, with the source of the evidence behind each criterion and the date it became available. Consent comes before any assessment that requires it, and the decision is recorded before enrollment, so the record shows that the evidence existed when the decision was made. A criterion that depends on imaging interpretation or on information from several sources receives a second, independent review. An unresolved disagreement goes to the PI, and until it is resolved the decision is not ready and the participant is not enrolled. The investigator then signs the decision, and the record allows it to be reconstructed later without relying on undocumented knowledge.

Every person considered for the investigation also enters a site log with a code and the outcome of screening, including the reason for any non-inclusion or withdrawal, while the key that links the code to the person is kept apart. ISO 14155:2026 requires each site to keep a log of all enrolled subjects with a code linked to their identity, and it allows the log to cover everyone pre-screened. At each review the site reconciles the log with the eligibility records, so that no candidate disappears without a recorded reason, and the counts of the reasons for non-inclusion go to the enrollment quality indicator under Performance Oversight.

Each follow-up visit has its own record, which states whether the visit took place within its window and which of the components that the CIP requires for that time point were completed, with the result of each and any exception with the action it triggered. A visit counts as usable only when the CtQ assessments required for that time point were done by the specified method and within the window, since attendance alone does not show that a visit was complete. A visit that is late or incomplete is recorded with its reason and its effect on the data, and an unexpected finding or a concern raised by the participant goes to clinical review and, where it applies, to the safety route. The review of follow-up across participants and periods belongs to Performance Oversight.

The Participant Screening and Eligibility Assessment Record (PSEAR) holds each eligibility decision (Appendix Q), and the Subject Identification and Failure Screening Log (SIFSL) holds the log of everyone screened (Appendix R). The Participant Follow-up Visit Record (PFVR) holds each visit (Appendix S).

### 4.4.4 Source documentation and parallel records

Source documentation belongs to the site’s own documentary system, and the framework works through that system. The duty to keep investigation information verifiable, with which this pillar opened, applies here most directly. ISO 14155:2026 requires the site team to create and maintain source documents whose type and location are documented, with documents and data kept traceable, and ICH E6(R3) asks the investigator to define, before the trial begins, what counts as a source record and where it is kept, together with how data are captured, and to avoid unnecessary transcription. The site therefore decides, for each critical datum, which record is its source, whether a clinical record or, where the CIP allows it, the CRF itself, and it adapts its existing system so that the records the CtQ domains depend on, from device traceability and the operative record to imaging and follow-up, are captured and linked. Practical guidance on E6(R3) makes the same point, since a source definition agreed in advance prevents uncertainty over which record is authoritative [86]. Each instrument of the framework then points to that source, and the framework adds no documentation system of its own. This work serves CtQ domain 11, data capture, source documentation and data-flow integrity.

The operating room is the one place where the framework adds a source record, because the data generated there cannot be rebuilt afterward. During each procedure the site completes an intraoperative checklist and device log, which captures the identifiers of each implanted component and the checks of each critical step as they happen, together with any deviation or substitution and the immediate review after surgery. The log is the source for what it captures first and points to the operative note and the device accountability record for everything else, so it supports both without replacing either, and it answers the part of DR6 that concerns intraoperative traceability and deviation capture.

Any site-level framework risks turning its instruments into a second, divergent record of information that already exists, and this one introduces enough records for the risk to be real, among them a risk register and a learning curve log. Some of its instruments hold data that has no other home, such as the sequence of a surgeon’s cases or the analysis of a complication signal, which no medical record or CRF collects. Others record how the site works, such as delegation and oversight, and have no counterpart in the participant-level systems. In both cases, no instrument may become a parallel record of a datum that already has a defined source, so an instrument that needs participant-level information refers to the source. Where it carries a value for a count or a comparison, it cites the source, and the source prevails if the two differ. The learning curve log records that a component substitution occurred and points to the operative note, and the complication log records the classification and the reporting action without becoming a second clinical narrative. Where the same event is still documented differently in two places, the discrepancy is itself a finding for internal verification.

Keeping to the site’s existing records follows from DR11, since a parallel documentation system would add work and cost that the conditions assumed for the target center make hard to sustain, and it would create the duplication that the rule above prevents. The framework therefore stays additive, and its instruments are built around identifiers and decisions. The Intraoperative Checklist and Device Log (ICDL) holds the record of each procedure (Appendix T).
