# 5. Discussion
## 5.1 How the framework differs from prior work
The works we reviewed offer the site four kinds of support. Two of them reach the site through other parties, since risk-based quality management comes through the sponsor and the control of operator learning through the trial team, and the other two reach it directly, through measures of site performance and the quality structures that some sites already hold. We returned to these works throughout the design, from the first drafts to the finished framework, to take from each what could serve the site and to see where the framework stood against them. In each case the support stops short of a structure that the site runs for itself during conduct, and the comparison that opens the discussion follows the four themes to show what the framework adds to each of them (Table 5.1).
Table 5.1. Prior work on quality in clinical investigations, grouped by theme, with the response of the framework to each theme.
{{T51}}
Risk-based quality management was developed for the party that designs and oversees a trial, as the EMA reflection paper and the CTTI principles show when they describe how a sponsor identifies critical factors and directs its oversight toward them^{1,27}. The MDIC supplement carries the same reasoning to devices^{28}. Sites meet this work through the sponsor, as a monitoring plan that arrives with the protocol^{49}. The survey of Portuguese sites found few with a risk tool of their own^{48}, and the RBQM adoption survey placed the identification of CtQ factors among the least used practices, even among sponsors^{12}. These findings shaped the Site Risk Register (SRR). It applies the sponsor's risk cycle to the risks created by the site's own execution and starts from the CIP, so it can be built even where the sponsor never formalized its critical factors. In those investigations the SRR may be the first place where anyone applies CtQ reasoning to local processes.
Surgical methodology controls operator learning mainly before the trial starts, through credentialing and standardization. Reviews of surgical quality assurance and of trials involving surgery found that monitoring during the trial is rarely implemented and that learning is almost never analyzed^{29,33,70}, and the consensus frameworks that add monitoring during the trial specify it for the trial team^{31,32}. The IDEAL recommendations had already proposed statistical process control to follow learning curves^{16}. They were written, however, for researchers evaluating an innovation, so the method did not reach the site as a quality control. Our response is the Surgeon Learning Curve Tracking Log (SLCT), which keeps each surgeon's case sequence during conduct and compares each case with the same surgeon's earlier cases of the same complexity, so that a pattern leads to the credentialing decision. It uses a structured clinical review in place of formal process control, because a single site rarely has enough cases to set control limits.
Site performance is mostly measured for the use of other parties, as in the Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure, which networks can use to benchmark sites and identify those that underperform^{36}. Our indicators serve the site itself. They tell it that one of its processes has started to drift, without comparing sites or surgeons. The validated measure includes consent, which our framework covers only through the eligibility record, so a site whose register rates consent as a high risk should add a measure for it. Central monitoring gives a reason to expect that a signal at the site is worth having, since sites improved after statistical monitoring flagged them^{37} or a sponsor ranked them^{38}. In both cases, however, the signal reached the site from outside. The framework moves the origin of that signal inside the site, earlier in the conduct, and whether it has a comparable effect is one of the expected effects of the framework (Table 5.3).
The few quality structures that sites hold today depend on resources, from the self-assessment of research infrastructure that the CTAIM supports^{39} and ISO 9001 certification^{40} to the trial units and research management systems of academic institutions^{41,42}. A small independent clinic has no trial unit or research office behind it. Some independent practices receive standard procedures and staff from site management organizations or site networks, but this support is built for trials in general and does not carry the CtQ content of a device investigation. The CTAIM, the tool that comes closest to a scale, assesses what a site has, but it does not say what a site with less can safely leave out^{39}. The framework was built so that its instruments carry part of the quality function that an academic hospital receives from its trial unit, without software or dedicated staff.
## 5.2 Novelty of the framework and its components
The comparison with earlier work shows what existed before the framework, and the next question is what the framework adds to it. DSRM, the methodology we followed, guides a design study from the identification of a problem to the communication of the result^{53}. Gregor and Hevner proposed a way to state what such a result adds to what was already known^{91}. They ask two questions, how well the problem was understood before the study and whether solutions for it already existed, and from the answers they obtain four types of contribution: routine design, improvement, exaptation and invention. Routine design applies existing knowledge to a problem that is already well understood. An improvement gives such a problem a new solution, expected to work better than those in use. An exaptation takes a solution that is already used in one field and applies it in another, where the same kind of problem exists and that solution had not yet been tried. Invention, the last type, is a new solution for a problem that had not been recognized before.
Seven of the 24 instruments are routine design, because they are records or functions that ICH E6(R3) and ISO 14155:2026 already ask of the investigator^{3,6}. They cover delegation and training (RACI, DTRM, TRE), the screening and eligibility of candidates (SIFSL, PSEAR), the follow-up visit (PFVR) and the safety case (SART). The knowledge behind them is not new. Studies of site practice show, however, that these duties are not always carried out in a standard way, and in one research network only about half of the sites used a standard method to identify adverse events^{45,47}. The framework therefore gives each of these records a ready format and a fixed place in the investigation, so that a site can apply them in practice and carry out each duty in a standard way, with attention to the points that matter for the quality of the investigation.
Fourteen instruments are exaptations, because the idea behind each of them already works somewhere else in clinical research or in the device industry, usually in the hands of the sponsor, and the framework brings it to the site, as the SRR does with the risk cycle of the sponsor. Nine of them adapt controls of the sponsor: the SRR, internal monitoring (IMP, IMC), the indicators (PIR, FUCR) and the joint review of events, used here for complications (SCML, CSST) and for the oversight cycle of the PI (POT, MRL)^{1,3,6}. The other five come from further away. The imaging records (SIRR, IQL) follow central reading^{58,62}, the corrective action log and the lessons record (CAPA, PIDLR) follow ISO 13485:2016^{56}, and the SLCT follows surgical research^{16}. None of these ideas could be copied exactly, and each needed some adaptation to the reality of a site or to the rest of the framework.
The Criticality-Coded Delegation Matrix (CCDM), the Surgical Credentialing Record (SCR) and the Intraoperative Checklist and Device Log (ICDL) are improvements, because each of them answers a known problem with a solution different from the ones currently in use. A conventional delegation log shows who is authorized to perform each task, but it does not show which tasks need the closer attention of the PI, although ICH E6(R3) asks for oversight in proportion to the importance of each activity^{3}. The CCDM gives each task a criticality grade, and the grade defines how closely the PI supervises the task and which event has to be reported to the PI at once. In the same way, the SCR turns the qualification of the surgeon, normally checked once before the investigation, into a decision that the site reviews during conduct. The ICDL records the component identifiers and any deviation during the operation, while the usual operative note is written afterward.
The way the instruments are connected is also an improvement, and we consider it the main contribution of the design. Sites already keep many records, and their quality management is still described as reactive and handled case by case^{45,46}. In the framework, the records are read together at a review, and what the review decides becomes a rule that the following records are checked against (Figure 4.3). This lets a site act before a monitor or an auditor points to a problem. The cycle also repeats in every review period, so what the site learns in one period changes how it works in the next. We found no instrument for sites in device investigations that links their records to reviews and decisions in this way.
The scalability model is the last improvement, since it answers a documented problem, the dependence of site quality on resources^{39–42}, which the texts leave to the principle of proportionality^{3}. The model fixes a minimum of 12 instruments for the nonhospital organization, and for every instrument left out it names a proxy, another record that the site already keeps and that takes over its function (Table 4.2). We found no earlier framework for sites that scales in this way, by keeping the functions and reducing the instruments.
We classify no component as an invention, because in every case either the problem had already been described or the solution already existed somewhere. The five improvements will be confirmed once sites use the framework and show that each works better than what it replaces.
Two results of the thesis fall outside this classification, the site-level CtQ domains and the design requirements, because they came before the instruments and are not solutions in themselves. Both, however, can be reused in later work. The domains tell a site where to look for the critical factors of any protocol it receives. The design requirements were written before the instruments existed, so someone else could answer them with a different set or use them to improve an existing one. Gregor and Hevner also separate an artifact built for one situation from knowledge that can be used in other situations, which they call nascent design theory^{91}. The 24 templates belong to the first kind, because their content was written for shoulder device investigations. The domains and the design requirements belong to the second, and so does the connection between the instruments, because none of them depends on the shoulder or on a particular template.
## 5.3 Putting the framework to work
### 5.3.1 What running the framework asks of a site
Most of the preparation happens at start-up. Fifteen of the twenty-four instruments are included in this phase, although only five are already in use at that point (Figure 4.2). The other ten are only prepared, with their rules of operation and review defined at this stage. This preparation supports the rest of the lifecycle, and because it takes time, a site building the structure for the first time will probably declare itself ready later than one that only signs the protocol, but most of that cost falls on the first investigation, because much of what is prepared at start-up is kept or reused in the next one.
During conduct, the framework asks less of the team than the number of instruments might suggest. Only five records are filled in at the clinical moment: the PSEAR, IQL, ICDL, SART and PFVR. The others use those records later, as the SLCT does when it takes each case from the ICDL. For a small clinic, the heavier work will therefore probably come at start-up and during periodic reviews, more than in the daily work with participants. How much time those reviews take is one of the first things an implementation study should measure.
The framework fixes the timing of reviews only when necessary: after each surgeon has completed a specified number of operations and at the reporting intervals of the CIP. For the remaining reviews, the framework allows the site to choose its own rhythm at start-up, for example monthly, quarterly, yearly or at defined set points. Events that meet an escalation trigger in the CCDM, however, go straight to the PI without waiting for the next review. But the freedom to choose a review rhythm also has a downside. If the gap between reviews is too long, central monitoring may pick up a trend before the site does, weakening the framework's purpose of helping the site identify and act on problems early.
At the minimum level, much of this review work rests on the oversight meeting. Without the SLCT and the instruments that review complication signals and indicators, the meeting has to bring the information together itself. It reads the substitutions in the ICDL alongside the SART cases and checks follow-up through the FUCR. Each meeting should start with safety, so that complications are reviewed before the team interprets a surgeon's operative times. If the team decides to restrict a surgeon's activities, the decision must be recorded in the POT and carried into the credentialing and delegation records. The ICDL then reflects that restriction at the surgeon's next case. The minimum level therefore depends on these meetings to bring the information together. If a meeting is missed, that review does not happen, and no record makes the gap visible.
### 5.3.2 Where implementation is likely to fail
We looked at the framework the same way it asks a site to look at its own conduct. Going through the lifecycle phase by phase, we asked where a site could run the instruments and still lose what they are meant to do. The failures we consider most likely are listed by phase, with what each one costs and the guard the design provides (Table 5.2). The list is our own assessment, made without data from a site, and it may not be complete. Each site should therefore also look for the points where its own implementation is likely to fail, since its conditions may create failures that the table does not show.

In a small team, the independence of review is hard to protect. The framework only requires that nobody reviews their own work. In a surgeon-owned practice, however, the coordinator who reviews the surgeon's operative documentation may be the surgeon's employee. One possibility is for the sponsor's monitor to look at the internal reviews at each visit. This is not part of the current design, however, and each site should consider whether it needs such an arrangement, or another one, given who reviews whom in its own team.
### 5.3.3 What the framework is expected to change
The effects of the framework depend on its review cycle closing in time, and that depends on the people who run it as much as on the design. Each expected effect is listed with the phase in which it should first appear (Table 5.3). In the first months, a site that starts checking its own conduct will probably record more deviations, because it is now looking for them, so an early rise says more about the framework working than about conduct getting worse. For this reason each site decides at start-up, together with its other review rules, how many review periods it will wait before judging whether an effect is there.

Some of these effects are followed through the six indicators of the framework. Part of them watch a process while it is running, such as the completeness of critical data, and the rest count events after they happen, such as safety reports sent late, which can still show a problem before it reaches a participant or an endpoint. In a small clinic, however, the numbers behind every indicator stay small. A clinic that includes few participants in an investigation cannot produce the same statistical signal as a hospital that enrolls many more, and a single event is often enough to move an indicator from control to closer review or action. The indicators will therefore serve mainly to make the team stop at set intervals and look at each case against the rules it fixed at start-up, to find the failure in the process behind it, so their value probably lies more in that review than in any statistical trend.
### 5.3.4 Documentation and conduct
One of the aims of the framework is to improve how the site documents its own conduct, on the premise that many failures in clinical investigations come from lapses and competing demands on the same person, or from not having a tool that shows an error in time. Controls written for a specific activity leave fewer gaps open, so a failure is less likely to go unnoticed, and an instrument that brings a failure into view gives someone the chance to respond to it. Documentation is meant here as a way to improve conduct, and whether it does so in practice is what the expected effects in Table 5.3 are meant to show over time. Deliberate misconduct is outside the reach of the framework, because a person who wants to misrepresent conduct can misrepresent the record as well.
A complete and traceable record of what the site did is valuable in its own right. It allows a case to be reconstructed years later and supports the manufacturer, whose post-market obligations depend on it, and it protects participants when someone reviews it and acts in time.
Knowing that the work is reviewed may also lead a surgeon or a nurse to pay closer attention to it and to learn from the review which elements matter. The same pressure can lead people to fill in the fields while the practice stays as it was, and the design limits that risk, because its instruments record decisions and identifiers with their dates, so a device identifier is either captured or missing whatever attention is paid to the form, and because most records are read by someone other than their author.
## 5.4 Scaling and transfer
The conditions of the small independent clinic were considered throughout the design. Research duties shared among clinical staff and the absence of a dedicated research or quality function entered the design requirements (Table 3.4), and the same conditions set the minimum implementation level, at which an existing record takes over the function of each instrument left out (Table 4.2). A site with more capacity, or an investigation with higher risk, adds instruments to that minimum. Running the framework is a voluntary effort, since ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026 and the MDR prescribe none of its instruments^{3,6,8}, so a site at the minimum level already brings to the investigation a structured control of its own quality, in proportion to its resources, as a site running the full set does. Two further questions concern the reach of the framework, namely how its instruments serve a site that conducts several investigations and how far the framework can be used in other specialties and under other regulatory regimes.
### 5.4.1 Instruments that outlive the investigation
The framework is described for a single investigation, and many sites conduct several, either at once or one after another, so part of the work done for one investigation can serve the next. Some instruments belong to the site and are kept across investigations, some keep a site structure that each investigation fills with its own content, and the rest are opened again for every investigation (Table 5.4).
Table 5.4. The twenty-four instruments by persistence across investigations.
{{T54}}
The instruments opened for each investigation are mostly records of participants, procedures or visits, so their effort grows with the number of cases, which is the work a site budget prices per participant and per procedure. Setting up the structure is a different kind of work, concentrated in the instruments the site keeps or reuses, and a site with a steady portfolio spreads that cost across investigations and, as it refines the instruments, spends less on running them each time.
A site running investigations at the same time can keep one corrective action log and hold one oversight meeting for all of them, while the participant records remain separate for each investigation. The same site could also share one role structure and one training history across its investigations, which would make the work faster and more cost-efficient, above all at start-up, when the RACI and the TRE are prepared. This would require adapting both instruments, since they were designed to be opened for each investigation, so that the documents of different investigations and sponsors stay apart and a specific record is easy to find. Safety reporting timeliness and critical data completeness measure processes shared by all of the site's investigations, as does the deviation rate by area, so the site can pool their counts to enlarge the small denominators of a single investigation. Surgeon consistency and complication patterns depend on the device, so the site follows them separately for each device, and because each sponsor's data are confidential, pooled counts serve the site's internal review. A surgeon's learning with a device also continues across investigations, so the SLCT of a later investigation can start from the earlier cases the site holds.
### 5.4.2 Transfer to other specialties
Most of what ties the framework to the shoulder sits in a few parts of the instruments, namely the complication categories, the imaging requirements and the complexity levels of the SLCT, so adapting them to another joint means changing these parts while the structure stays the same. Specialties can also differ in more than content. In spine surgery one procedure may implant several components, which multiplies the traceability work and the ICDL entries per case, and in robot-assisted or navigated arthroplasty part of the execution moves from the surgeon to a system, so the basis of comparison of the SLCT has to separate experience with the procedure from experience with the system. Surgical investigations without an implant, where unit traceability does not arise, and device investigations without surgery, where operator dependence takes another form, would need adaptations of their own. These adaptations change the content of some instruments, while the obligations behind them stay the same, since the MDR and ISO 14155:2026 apply to a knee, hip or spine device the same requirements for clinical investigation that they apply to a shoulder device^{6,8}. The pillars, the lifecycle and the link between records, reviews and decisions would therefore carry over to other implant surgery with small adaptations. Applying the instrument set to the documentation of a completed hip or knee device investigation, and recording which fields had to change, would confirm the transfer at low cost.
### 5.4.3 Transfer to other jurisdictions and editions
Most of the nonhospital organizations in the registry operate in the United States, and the framework was designed under the MDR and ISO 14155:2026. Both regimes leave the organization of the site to the site itself, so a closer analysis of the regulations of the United States, or of another jurisdiction, would most likely add elements to the framework or adjust its terminology case by case, while what has already been built stays in place. In the United States, for example, the investigator reports an unanticipated adverse device effect to the sponsor and to the institutional review board within ten working days (21 CFR Part 812)^{11}, and requirements of this kind would enter the instruments as specific additions. The steps that produced the framework would also serve this analysis, adding the regulations of the new jurisdiction to the clause comparison and checking the CtQ domains and the design requirements against them, so that its specific needs are incorporated into the framework.
In principle, the framework should also be consistent with those regulations, since it rests on ISO 14155 and ICH E6(R3), two international texts written for use across jurisdictions. As under the MDR, the framework complements what the regulations require, so in another jurisdiction it would also work as an addition to the site's own obligations, one that sponsors and inspectors would probably welcome.
Within the European Union, the version of ISO 14155 with a presumption of conformity under the MDR is still EN ISO 14155:2020+A11:2024^{9}, while the 2026 edition represents the current state of the art. The fourth edition revised the third mainly by clarifying and adding requirements, such as the management of risks from clinical procedures required by the CIP and the clinical events committee^{6}, so a framework built on it is aligned with the harmonized version in use today and already includes what a future harmonization of the 2026 edition would bring. The transfer to the new edition has therefore already been made.
## 5.5 Implications
### 5.5.1 For the investigational site
For the site, the main gain is control of its own conduct while the investigation runs, held in an account that the site can show. That account also has economic and reputational value, and because the organization keeps it, it survives changes of people. When a coordinator leaves or a monitor is replaced, or when a question settled by an earlier monitor comes back, the site still holds the decisions and the reasons behind them.
The work the framework asks of the site can be priced, because research payments from industry are expected to reflect the fair market value of the work performed^{92} and site budgets are negotiated item by item with the sponsor. In ten industry-sponsored pediatric trials at one Swedish center, the final budgets exceeded the sponsors' initial proposals by 59% on average, and the costs of preparing the trial were among the items that changed most^{93}. The quality work a site performs for an investigation can therefore enter the budget as an item of its own, within the fair market value of the activity, and a function such as a data quality reviewer can be justified as a budget line in the same way. As the site repeats and refines the instruments, the time they take should fall, while the payment for the activity stays at least the same.
The framework may also make the income of the site more stable. Closer control over assessment and enrollment should lose fewer eligible patients to incomplete records or missed assessments, and each inclusion is paid, while fewer errors mean fewer costs of correction, such as a screen failure caused by an incomplete eligibility record or a missed imaging window. A site that can show how it controls its conduct may also gain standing with the sponsors that select it for later investigations, which an implementation study could test.
The benefits also reach other parties, since the sponsor gains easier monitoring and more reliable data and the manufacturer relies on the operating room records for traceability. A site deciding where to begin should start with the instruments that protect data that cannot be recovered, meaning the five records made at the clinical moment and the SRR, because the same records protect its revenue. When the site records a weakness of its own, together with the action it took, a monitor reading that record sees a site that knows its weaknesses and acts on them before an external review points them out.
### 5.5.2 For sponsors and monitors
The framework sits alongside the sponsor's quality system and organizes the processes through which the site conducts each investigation. The sponsor oversees quality across the trial, while the site uses the CIP, with what the sponsor communicates about critical factors and risks, to identify the risks of its own work and set controls in proportion to them (Figure 5.1). Part of the framework stays with the site across investigations, and the rest is opened for each investigation and follows its CIP and the systems the sponsor requires (Table 5.4). A signal that needs the sponsor's assessment reaches the sponsor with its evidence and with any local action already taken, so that the sponsor can read it across sites and return any change to the site's conduct.

At qualification, ISO 14155:2026 asks the sponsor to judge the qualifications of the PI and the adequacy of the site (Clause 6.8)^{6}, and a site that arrives with its own risk register and oversight records gives documentary evidence on both. At a monitoring visit the same site can hand over its delegation records and corrective action log, with its indicator results, so the monitor reviews a site that has already described its own risks. Risk-based monitoring relies on certain prerequisites at the site^{46}, and the framework builds some of them, so a shorter monitoring time is one of the expected effects of the framework (Table 5.3).
The two systems can also be aligned more closely than they are today. If the sponsor gave each site, at initiation, a summary of the critical factors and main risks it identified, the SRR and the sponsor's monitoring would address the same targets. Where the CIP allows it, the ICDL can be named as the source of implant identifiers and intraoperative events, so source data verification compares the CRF with a structured record made during the procedure. The site's indicators read at one center the processes that the sponsor's key risk indicators read across the trial, and they could feed central monitoring as long as each datum keeps its source. The clinical events committee that ISO 14155:2026 allows the sponsor to establish^{6} addresses the same events as the site's complication review, at a different level, and a local action already taken is information the committee can use.
Only one of 247 randomized surgical trials analyzed learning effects^{70}. The SLCT holds the case sequence of each surgeon with complexity and duration, so a CIP that planned for it could use the log to examine learning in the main analysis, provided the surgeon's data are protected.
A sponsor could go further and supply templates and help set up the framework across its sites, as it already supplies the protocol and training. This would move part of the cost of construction to the sponsor, but a site working with three sponsors would then receive three frameworks and run three quality systems at once. The more stable division keeps a structure owned by the site, into which each investigation brings its content and, where the sponsor wishes, its templates, while the operation of the framework and the ownership of its records stay with the site.
### 5.5.3 For standards and regulators
Reading ISO 14155:2026 from the position of the site shows points that a future revision could consider^{6}. All three risk streams of Clause 6.2 belong to the sponsor, and a revision could describe how a site handles the part of those risks that its own execution creates. Clause 9.2.4.5 p) asks monitors to verify that equipment relevant to assessment is maintained and calibrated, and the same revision could describe the record through which the site shows this work.
A clause asking a site to keep a risk register would ask for one document and one review cycle, while a requirement for a site-level quality system would ask for much more. Annex XV of the MDR governs the conduct of clinical investigations^{8}, and the harmonization of the fourth edition would be a natural moment to examine how a site organizes itself to conduct them. Recognition schemes for research sites already exist, since the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency accredits units that run phase I trials against requirements beyond good clinical practice^{94}, and the Association for the Accreditation of Human Research Protection Programs accredits the programs through which research organizations protect participants^{95}, while ISO 15189:2022 gives the medical laboratory a quality system that can be accredited^{57}. A site running device investigations could be recognized in the same way, and a framework of the kind proposed here could become the content of a future standard or accreditation for such sites. A standard of this kind could also reduce the fragmentation that frameworks supplied by sponsors create, because a site working for several sponsors would run one structure recognized by all of them. A future revision of ICH E6 or ISO 14155 could, more modestly, give the site an explicit proactive role in quality without moving final responsibility, and the framework shows one form that role could take.
If later revisions move to the site part of the quality requirements that now rest on the sponsor, as the direction of ICH E6 suggests, a site running the framework will already hold much of what it is asked for, since it can show how it controlled its conduct without reconstructing it from primary documents.
### 5.5.4 Ethical implications
The SLCT and the consistency indicator hold performance by surgeon and can lead to a restriction of the cases a surgeon takes in the investigation, so in substance they assess professional performance. What they record, however, is performance with a device or technique that is new to the surgeon, under the conditions of the investigation, and a result below expectation in that setting says nothing about the surgeon's usual practice. Poor practice is a different matter, which belongs to the clinical governance of the organization. A signal recorded in these instruments, or in the complication review, could still follow a surgeon beyond the investigation and harm a career if it were read out of context, so the framework keeps every action that results from these records, from closer observation to a restriction, within the scope of the investigation.
The design codes the surgeon and keeps the key with the PI, which protects the surgeon when other staff, reviewers or patients see the documents by chance, and it fixes before the first case the outcomes that a review can reach, with the reasoning behind any restriction documented. Who may access these records, and for what purpose, also has to be defined before the first case. They are personal data of the professional and are handled under the data protection law that applies.
In a surgeon-owned practice, the site belongs to the surgeon it observes, and the PI who decides on a restriction may be the surgeon under review or a close colleague who shares the commercial consequence. The same structure affects internal verification, where the reviewer may be the employee of the person reviewed. Every function has a named substitute in the role structure, so when the PI is the surgeon under review, the decision passes to that substitute on a route fixed before the first case.
## 5.6 Evaluation, limitations, and further research
### 5.6.1 The evaluation this thesis performed
The demonstration and evaluation activities of the DSRM use the artifact to solve instances of the problem and measure how well it does so^{53}, and both were left for later work. The design itself, however, was evaluated before use. The design-science literature distinguishes evaluation before an artifact is used, which tests whether the design decisions are grounded, from evaluation after its use, which tests whether the artifact works^{96}. It also separates formative evaluation, which improves a design while it is still being shaped, from summative evaluation, which judges a finished artifact, and artificial evaluation, carried out away from real use, from naturalistic evaluation in the setting where the artifact will run^{97}. Our traceability from each component to a requirement, and from each requirement back to its origin, is an evaluation before use, formative and artificial. It is the expected first step for a design at this stage, because it checks that every part rests on a stated reason before anyone spends resources on implementation. Whether the framework works in a site is a question for a naturalistic evaluation, and a review of the mapping by someone outside the design would strengthen it, since we derived the requirements and assigned the components ourselves. The framework should therefore move next to a naturalistic formative evaluation, in which a site uses the instruments and the design is corrected, and only then to a summative one that judges its effect.
### 5.6.2 Limitations
Some choices of this thesis are delimitations made on purpose, such as the focus on the shoulder and on the European regulatory framework and the special consideration given in the design to the small independent clinic, and they set the scope of the claims. The limitations that follow each weaken an inference within that scope. The framework has not been implemented, so its expected effects, the economic argument and the estimate of effort remain expectations. The gaps and the design requirements do not depend on any expected effect, so this limitation leaves the derivation intact and bears on the claims about effect, which a feasibility study can begin to test.
The rules and thresholds of the indicators are provisional, because we found no published values for site-level indicators of this kind. The order of the work also influenced the minimum level. The conditions of the small independent clinic shaped each instrument through DR10 to DR12, while the minimum set was chosen after the full set existed, so it was reached by removing instruments and naming a proxy for each one.
Any statement that a body of work is absent from the field rests on the works we found and read, so a systematic search can test the position of this work in the literature. We reduced the risk by searching the bodies of work most likely to contain a site-level framework and by reporting the closest works we found.
Our evidence on the state of site practice comes from published studies and regulatory guidance, with some expert commentary. We had no access to the internal documents of any site, from its procedures to its audit data, which weakens the claim that the failures the framework addresses are the ones that occur most often. Field research in sites of each organizational category can test that claim and calibrate the framework against observed failures.
We did not consult experts formally during the design. The framework was discussed informally with professionals working in clinical operations, and some of their criticism changed it, but these conversations were neither planned nor recorded, so they cannot count as evidence. The claim that the instruments can be completed as intended therefore rests on the design alone, until a structured content review tests it.
In the registry analysis, one researcher classified each organization from its public self-description, with a second internal pass and no independent assessor. This weakens the assignment of organizations to environments and the reproducibility of the counts in Table 3.3, which a second coder can test. Because the sample is weighted toward the United States, the inference that weakens most is the one from the organizational profile of the sample to European sites, and a registry analysis based on European data, which Eudamed did not provide, would test it.
### 5.6.3 Further research
The framework has completed the development phase of a complex intervention, and the phases that follow, from feasibility to implementation, do not have to come in strict order^{98}. The shortest next step is the demonstration activity of the DSRM, which needs no participants. Running the full set through a simulated or retrospective case series, from feasibility to the lessons record, shows whether the instruments connect as the design intends and whether any record lacks a reader.
A content review by sites and monitors, with surgeons included, and a usability test of the templates should come next, because every later evaluation depends on instruments that sites can complete correctly. A feasibility study in one small independent clinic and one hospital can then run the minimum set through a complete investigation. It should measure staff time per instrument, separating the work done once from the work that grows with cases, and compare the completeness of the records with a defined checklist. The share of deviations that the site detected before the monitor did shows most directly whether the framework does what it claims.
The evaluation of effect needs a concurrent comparison, because before-and-after comparisons at one site tend to report an improvement. In surgical oncology, 86 of 97 non-randomized studies of quality improvement reported a positive effect, against 2 of 12 randomized trials^{34}. A sponsor could introduce the framework in its sites in a staggered order within one investigation, letting the sites that have not yet started serve as comparators. Where that is not possible, a site could follow its indicators over a long series before and after adoption, as the IDEAL recommendations propose when randomized designs are not feasible^{16}. A design led by a sponsor of this kind also tests whether a sponsor can supply templates while the site keeps the operation and ownership of its records.
Indicator validation needs another design, because an investigation spread across several centers leaves each center with few cases over several years. It can instead draw on existing records, since a retrospective study can pair historical values of the indicators with the events that followed them, and pooling the records of several centers of one sponsor provides the volume that one site cannot. Query backlog and reporting timeliness can be recovered from most trial databases, while operative time by complexity level is available only where the site recorded the level.
