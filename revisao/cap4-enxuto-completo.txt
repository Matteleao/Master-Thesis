4. Results: The Quality Framework

4.1 Overview and design rationale

This chapter reports the third DSRM activity, the design and development of a framework that meets DR1 to DR12, in enough detail for a site to implement and operate each component.

The Site-Centered Operational Quality Management Framework has four pillars, each holding a set of instruments, and three further elements that govern how the instruments are used. A lifecycle assigns every instrument to the phase where it acts (DR9, Section 4.6), and a scalability model sets which instruments a site runs, according to the kind of organization and the risk of the investigation (DR12, Section 4.7). The third element, a role structure that assigns each quality function to a defined person and keeps authority apart from task-level work (DR10), belongs to the Governance pillar (Section 4.2.2).

The four pillars, Governance, Risk Management, Operational Controls, and Performance Oversight, answer DR1 to DR8 (Sections 4.2 to 4.5). Of the two organizations that produce the evidence behind the clinical evaluation report, only the site works without a quality system of its own (Section 3.2.1). We therefore gave it the functional shape of the systems that the manufacturer runs under ISO 13485:2016 and ISO 14971:2019 and that the medical laboratory, another organization whose results others rely on, runs under ISO 15189:2022 [52, 53, 54]. None of the three standards uses this exact division. Governance rests on their management clauses (ISO 13485 Clause 5, ISO 14971 Clause 4, and ISO 15189 Clause 5), and Risk Management on ISO 13485 Clauses 4.1.2 and 7.1, ISO 14971 Clauses 5 to 7, and ISO 15189 Clauses 5.6 and 8.5. Operational Controls draws only on ISO 13485 Clause 7, including Clause 7.5, and on ISO 15189 Clause 7, because ISO 14971 does not address the control of routine processes. Performance Oversight draws on the measurement and review clauses of all three (ISO 13485 Clauses 8.2.4, 8.4, and 8.5, ISO 14971 Clauses 9 and 10, and ISO 15189 Clauses 8.7 to 8.9).

The instruments form one set, designed to work on paper or within an electronic system the site already uses (DR11), and this constraint prevails wherever it conflicts with another requirement (Section 3.5), so no component depends on new software or new staff. We designed the instruments once the functions of all four pillars had been defined, so each instrument has a home pillar but often reads records kept under another.

The framework was designed for the target center of Section 3.4.5, a nonhospital organization, where it can assume the least organizational support, and the conditions of that center shaped the instruments and set the minimum implementation level. The components are described as functions, so a center with another staffing structure can map its own posts onto them.

Each instrument describes a control function, so a site that already keeps a record performing that function uses it and does not open a new one. The instruments are not regulatory requirements and replace neither sponsor procedures nor the controls defined in the CIP, so a record that the regulations already require stays as it is, and a new record is justified only where a critical process would otherwise not be controlled or not be evidenced.

4.2 Pillar 1: Governance

Governance answers Gap 1, because the instruments give the investigator several quality duties, such as supervising delegated activities and making sure that the site team is qualified, but none of them requires a structure that holds those duties together. This pillar provides that structure for DR1 and DR10, in CtQ domains 1 and 6, and leaves each duty with the party that already holds it. It sets who holds authority for quality at the site and how that authority reaches the people who do the work, together with the conditions they must meet before they act. These conditions weigh more in a surgical device investigation, because results depend on the surgeon as much as on the device.

The oversight model sets how the PI exercises that authority and delegation sets who may act on it, while device training documentation and, for surgeons, qualification set the competence required to act. Governance decides whether a surgeon is authorized, and the learning curve that follows once the surgeon operates is tracked under Risk Management (Section 4.3.2).

4.2.1 The oversight model

During design we compared three ways of allocating oversight at the site, which reflect different conditions of staffing and capacity. The descriptions of trial-site operations we reviewed confirm that these functions have to be covered [73, 74] but do not sort them into these arrangements, so we present them as design options.

In the first arrangement the PI intervenes directly in most critical activities and personally reviews most documentary and operational workflows. It looks like close supervision but becomes hard to sustain as activity grows, because site PIs already carry scientific, regulatory, recruitment, supervisory, and administrative duties, and their contribution is often under-recognized [73]. Administrative review then takes the attention that the PI's clinical judgment is needed for, such as confirming surgical appropriateness, interpreting an intraoperative deviation, or assessing whether a complication is related to the investigational device. The arrangement also scales poorly, because the PI would have to follow every area of a surgical investigation, from the operating room and radiology to follow-up, implant accountability, AE management, and data QC.

In the second arrangement the study coordinator runs daily management and the PI keeps final authority. This is workable, since the clinical research coordinator is a core site function [74] and ICH E6(R3) allows delegation while the investigator keeps ultimate responsibility and proportionate oversight [6], as FDA guidance on investigator supervision also does [75]. It fails when oversight becomes nominal, because the site has not said which decisions need the PI and has set no review structure, no review frequency, and no escalation route. In a surgical investigation those decisions include borderline eligibility, an unplanned intraoperative change such as a component substitution, the interpretation of a revision, and any inconsistency that the documentation alone does not explain.

The framework adopts a third arrangement, a tiered model of PI oversight adjusted to risk, which keeps the delegation of the second and adds what made it nominal, so that the PI's attention goes to the clinical decisions. The PI's involvement in each task follows the risk the task poses to participant safety, procedural integrity, device accountability, and data reliability. This applies the CtQ logic of ICH E8(R1) to the investigator's own attention [5], with the critical points taken from the CtQ factors that the site identifies when the investigation starts (Section 4.3.1). The cost is that the site has to set thresholds and a review schedule before the first participant is enrolled, and someone has to keep the meetings running for the whole investigation. The model runs through five mechanisms, which answer DR1 by turning the PI's oversight into a documented and scheduled practice, and the grading of delegated activity by criticality gives the other four their criterion.

- Risk-tiered involvement. For each type of activity, the site states in advance what needs the PI's own judgment and what the PI can review afterward. The line depends on the device and the procedure and moves as the team gains experience, so the site revisits it at each oversight cycle. A line set too low returns the PI to the first arrangement, and one set too high turns oversight into a formality. The Criticality-Coded Delegation Matrix (CCDM) holds the level of supervision set for each task (Appendix A).

- Criticality grading of delegated activity. Each delegated task carries a grade that sets its level of supervision and its escalation trigger, and the grade depends on context. Coordinating a routine visit is administrative until a scheduling choice places a follow-up outside the protocol window, and an intraoperative note is clerical until it becomes the record that later reconstructs a disputed complication. The grades therefore come from the CtQ factors of the investigation at hand, because a scheme copied from another site misses the tasks that carry this hidden weight in this investigation and with this team. The CCDM records the grades.

- Milestone review. The PI reviews the investigation formally at four points where the most consequential threats concentrate: procedural implementation, the immediate postoperative period, the approach to endpoint assessment, and close-out. Each point comes after the events whose problems it has to catch and early enough to stop a correctable pattern before it runs through several more cases. The four points reflect the risk profile of a shoulder implant investigation and would need revision for another device or procedure. The Milestone Review Log (MRL) records each decision (Appendix B).

- Event-triggered escalation. Events that threaten participant safety or causal interpretation cannot wait for the next scheduled review, so they go to the PI as soon as they occur. The CCDM sets the trigger for each task, and the PI Oversight Template (POT) records the PI's decision and the action it triggers (Appendix C).

- Oversight meetings. The review runs in a recurring meeting of the site team, so oversight no longer depends on ad hoc contact. The meeting transposes to the site the consensus-based review of events that ISO 14155:2026 assigns to a sponsor's clinical events committee [8], together with the periodic risk review described in the EMA reflection paper [1]. We found no study showing that such forums improve oversight at site level, and they are hardest to sustain in the nonhospital organizations this framework targets, where the people at the meeting are the same people doing the work it reviews. Each meeting closes one cycle of the POT.

4.2.2 Delegation, roles, and functions

Delegation turns the oversight model into daily practice, because the PI's attention can follow the grade of each task only if the site knows who performs it and whether that person is authorized and trained. An escalation trigger also works only when everyone knows who has to act on it. The role structure answers DR10, which asks that quality functions be assigned to defined people, with authority kept apart from task-level responsibility and no CtQ function depending on a single individual.

A conventional delegation log records who performs which trial-related activity. ICH E6(R3) asks for that record in proportion to the significance of the activity and accepts that work done as part of clinical practice may need none (Section 2.3.3), and ISO 14155:2026 requires the names, signatures, functions, and authorizations of new team members to be documented. Neither asks for a criticality grade or for evidence of competence in the same record. The framework needs both, because the oversight model sets its review by the criticality of each task, and a person may act only after release through training or, for a surgeon, through qualification. Delegation records are also examined directly at inspection, and the investigator remains accountable for delegated work [76], so the framework gives the record three properties.

- Task-based and graded. Each entry describes a task with its grade under the oversight model, so a person's authorization covers specific tasks, and the grade sets how closely the PI reviews each one. A research nurse may be well placed to coordinate visits and keep screening logs and still not be the right person to resolve a clinically meaningful discrepancy in operative documentation.

- Competency-gated. An entry becomes active only when the person has been released for that task after training or, for a surgeon, qualified and authorized for the procedure (Sections 4.2.3 and 4.2.4).

- Version-controlled. Each change of person, task, or grade is dated, so the record shows who was authorized at any point of the investigation and survives the departure of the people named in it, as DR10 requires.

The framework defines a role structure of six functions that cover the work of an orthopedic implant investigation, each with a defined limit of authority (Table 4.1). The PI holds the central position, because under ISO 14155:2026 delegation transfers execution while responsibility stays with the PI. The limits of authority keep authority apart from task-level work, as DR10 asks, and send clinical judgments to the PI or the surgeon and endpoint adjudication to the central reader where one is used.

[Table 4.1. The six functions of the role structure, with the main responsibilities and the limit of authority of each.]

A site maps its own posts onto the six functions, and in a nonhospital organization, where the clinical staff also carry the research work, one person may hold several. Each function is therefore assigned to a named person, whatever the number of people involved, and has a named backup, so that no CtQ function depends on one individual. Services outside the study team that the site relies on, such as radiology or physiotherapy, are delegated activities under ICH E6(R3) and fall under the same proportionate oversight, which the site coordinates with the sponsor where the sponsor selects or supplies the provider. The CCDM records each delegated task with its grade, prerequisite, supervision level, and escalation trigger, and the Responsibility Assignment Matrix (RACI) maps each activity to its current holder and backup, with the limit of that holder's authority (Appendix D).

4.2.3 Device training documentation

Monitors and inspectors read the training records of a site as evidence that critical activities were done by people who were both delegated and prepared for this investigation. The site uses the same records as a control, because it assigns a task only once the training it requires is in place.

Under the MDR, the people conducting an investigation have to be adequately trained in the proper use of the investigational device, the CIP, and GCP, and the sponsor verifies and documents this training, arranging it where necessary [10]. ISO 14155:2026 adds that new members of the site team start only after documented training and makes the PI responsible for training the team, while the sponsor ensures that the training is documented and verified. As GCP guidance, ICH E6(R3) limits trial-related training to what the delegated activities require beyond the person's usual training, and ICH E8(R1) asks that it match each role and take place before the person becomes involved in the study.

The sponsor also defines the content of the training. The MDR requires the CIP to state the training and experience needed to use the device and the IB to include any relevant training, and ISO 14155:2026 asks the same of both documents and asks the sponsor to consider how far the risk controls require training, such as cadaver training or support to users during the investigation. The texts examined therefore place the content and the verification of training with the sponsor and the duty to train the team with the PI, and none of them says which training each role at the site needs, when a trained person is ready to act alone, or what makes the training no longer current. This component organizes the site's duty around those three questions and adds no obligation to it.

Medicinal-product GCP also requires personnel familiar with the investigational product, so a device investigation differs in the functions linked to the device that the training has to reach. The framework groups them into five training areas, each protecting one or more CtQ domains.

- Protocol and procedural training: the study objectives, the key eligibility restrictions, the visit windows that matter for endpoints, PDs, and the decision points specific to the study.

- Device training: the investigational system, its instrumentation and implant components, preparation and handling, and any constraint the study places on the workflow.

- Operative and perioperative documentation: intraoperative records, component reconciliation, capture of implant lot and UDI, documentation of substitutions and deviations, and chain of custody.

- Assessment training: radiographic acquisition standards and imaging timing, ROM measurement, and PROM administration where these bear on an endpoint.

- Safety reporting: recognition and documentation of AEs and DDs, and the escalation pathway for clinically important findings.

Each record identifies who was trained, in which area, when, by whom, and on which version of the controlled document, so that after a revised IFU or an amended CIP the site can see at once who was trained on the earlier version.

The site records the completion of training and the release to perform a task as separate events, because attending a session shows that training took place and does not show that the person can perform the task alone. For tasks graded as critical in the delegation record, particularly those involving the device, imaging, or outcome assessment, the site records an explicit release decision once training is complete. This release is the competency gate of the delegation record, and for surgeons it is one of the inputs to the authorization decision. A role that performs only routine clinical care receives no device-specific training unless the CIP requires it.

The site retrains people when the work changes, through a protocol amendment, a revised IFU, a new device iteration, or a change in workflow. It also retrains when deviation records or monitoring findings show a task being done poorly, as with a safety signal or repeated documentation errors, following ICH E8(R1), which recommends retraining when issues related to CtQ factors arise during the study. The component supports DR10, because each function of the role structure acts only with a defined training requirement and a recorded release. The Device and Study Training Requirements Matrix (DTRM) sets the training each role or task requires, and the Training Record Evaluation and Release (TRE) holds each person's training and release (Appendices E and F).

4.2.4 Surgeon qualification and continued authorization

Under the MDR, everyone conducting an investigation has to be suitably qualified, and the sponsor has to show that the investigator and the site are capable of conducting it. ISO 14155:2026 divides this work, with the sponsor setting the selection criteria and verifying qualification before the start, while the PI documents the qualifications of the team and keeps the team adequate throughout, which includes making sure that only authorized users handle the device. The sponsor therefore sets the entry threshold, through the CIP and its training requirements and in line with the study risk assessment, and the site shows that each surgeon given a study procedure meets it.

The framework turns that threshold into a documented decision for each surgeon, because a specialty credential on file shows general standing as an orthopedic surgeon and says little about competence in the procedure or readiness to use this device. ISO 14155:2026 already separates standing from device training for the PI, who has to be experienced in the field of application and trained in the use of the investigational device (Clause 10.2(b)). A surgeon can be well established in a comparable procedure and still uncertain in the technique of this device, so the site judges how much of that uncertainty the entry criteria and the release on device training resolve and how much its own decision has to close before the first case. The decision places the surgeon in one of four categories, credentialed, conditional, restricted, or not credentialed, and is linked to the delegation record, so the surgical tasks assigned to a surgeon always match the current authorization.

Where the risk assessment or the expected learning curve calls for more supervision, the site may add temporary controls in proportion to the surgeon's experience with the procedure and the device, such as proctoring or co-surgery, or a first assignment to simpler cases. The surgeon then holds a conditional authorization until the conditions set in the decision are met.

Qualification stays current for the whole investigation, because the site re-evaluates it at least once a year while the surgeon operates on study participants and after a protocol amendment, a safety signal, a cluster of complications, or evidence of drift in performance. The evidence of drift comes from the surgeon's own sequence of cases, which the learning curve log and the complication monitoring of Risk Management provide, and the decision on that evidence is made here. Each re-evaluation uses the same four categories, so an authorization can be narrowed or withdrawn and later restored. None of the three instruments says how observed performance should enter the PI's duty to keep the team adequate (Gap 3), so the site's contribution is the link between observed performance and continued authorization, which supports DR3 by giving the monitoring of the learning curve a decision to act on. The Surgical Credentialing Record (SCR) holds each decision with its evidence, and the RACI carries the resulting authorization into the delegation record (Appendix G).

4.3 Pillar 2: Risk Management

Risk Management answers Gap 2, the absence of a site record of the risks that its own execution creates, and it carries the monitoring of the learning curve that Gap 3 calls for. It answers DR2 and DR3 and part of DR6 and DR8. Every domain carries execution risks of its own, so the pillar covers all twelve CtQ domains, and it works out where the site's conduct is most likely to fail and how each failure is controlled, limited to the risks that threaten CtQ factors.

Risk in a device investigation sits at three levels, and the texts examined give an owner to the first two. At device level the manufacturer manages risk under ISO 14971, and the CIP sets out the risks and benefits of the device. At trial level, ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) ask the sponsor to run a risk cycle from identification to review, which the nonbinding EMA reflection paper describes in operational terms. ISO 14155:2026 places two further sources beside the device, the clinical procedures the CIP requires beyond routine practice and the conduct of the investigation, and gives all of them to the sponsor.

The third level, the risk that a specific site's execution creates, has no owner in any of these texts. Much of the procedural risk comes from the way the site carries out the protocol, and the sponsor's cycle cannot be built around the weaknesses of one center when it has to work across many. In an orthopedic device investigation this local risk is clinical and methodological at once, since most of the structural conditions of Chapter 3 act at the site, and the first signs of device-related complications also appear there. The site knows where its own process is weak, so it is the party best placed to identify these risks and control them.

4.3.1 The site risk register

A 2023 survey of 46 Portuguese clinical trial sites found that 57.0% reported using a systematic risk management tool, but only nine (19.6%) described a standard tool or document that captured risk at site level [77]. Most sites (87.0%) were willing to use a tool built for their own needs, and lack of knowledge of risk management was the main reason given against one. It is one national survey, and the closest measurement of Gap 2 that we found.

The site risk register holds the risks that the site's own execution creates. Some arise around surgery, such as incomplete capture of implant identifiers or late documentation of an intraoperative deviation, and others afterward, when images are acquired inconsistently or visits fall outside their windows. The register is preventive, so the site opens it before the first participant is enrolled. At that point the site turns the CtQ domains into the CtQ factors of the investigation, using the protocol and what the sponsor communicates about critical factors and risks, and enters each local risk with its cause, its consequence, and the factor it threatens.

Each risk is rated by its impact on the CtQ factor and by how likely it is to occur and to go undetected, following the risk logic of ICH E8(R1). It has a named owner from the role structure, a control, and a residual risk. The rating keeps the register short enough to review at each oversight cycle, when the PI reads it, and each risk carries its own review trigger, so a change in the site's conditions reaches the register before it causes a failure. A risk whose effect may reach beyond the site goes to the sponsor. The register therefore covers what DR2 asks, from identification to reassessment, and the Site Risk Register (SRR) holds each risk with the factor it threatens and its control plan (Appendix H).

4.3.2 Operator-dependent risk and the learning curve

In a device investigation the surgical procedure is part of the intervention, so the way each surgeon performs it is a risk that a medicinal product does not carry. It reaches protocol adherence, implant traceability, and the endpoints, and it is shared by all the cases of one surgeon. The instruments compared in this thesis control operator learning at entry and give the site no role in observing it during conduct (Gap 3). This component covers that interval and answers DR3.

Experience is linked with shorter operative times, fewer complications, and better PROM scores [20], but studies define and measure proficiency in different ways. A systematic review of 13 studies estimated that about 25 reverse total shoulder arthroplasties were needed to reach an acceptable point on the learning curve, with a range of 16 to 86 cases for anatomic arthroplasty, which only two studies had examined. In a multicenter analysis of operative duration, three of four early-career surgeons reached a plateau between 12 and 86 cases [78]. These figures describe learning in those samples, and the framework does not adopt them, or any fixed case count, as a threshold.

The tracking asks whether the variation between a surgeon's cases is becoming a material risk to the CtQ factors at this site. ICH E8(R1) asks for periodic review of CtQ factors during a study, because new issues arise once it has begun, and the learning curve is one such issue at a surgical site. SQA frameworks address the learning curve mainly at selection [37, 39], and the Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure has no indicator for a surgeon's consistency once a trial is under way [41].

Before the first case, the site fixes how cases will be compared. It defines the levels of case complexity that matter, with the operative duration expected for each, and the support, such as proctoring, that a new surgeon should have. Each case then enters with its complexity, its duration, and any unplanned event, such as a component substitution or a call for senior support. A slow operation means something different in a complex case and in a routine one, and early or late in a surgeon's experience with the device, so each case is read against the same surgeon's earlier cases of the same complexity. This avoids most of the case-mix problem of comparing surgeons, although changes in case complexity over time still have to be considered. The site reviews the sequence after the first cases and at set points afterward, and earlier when a pattern appears, such as repeated substitutions or a cluster of early technical complications. A signal from complication monitoring is also read against this record before it is attributed to the device or to the operator.

Each review either confirms that execution is stable or leads to a response of increasing weight, from closer observation to renewed training or temporary proctoring, and to formal escalation when a pattern is material or recurrent. A restriction of the surgeon's scope is a change of authorization, so it goes to the credentialing decision, and a material pattern enters the risk register or corrective and preventive action. Because the record is kept as cases occur, it can show procedural drift before it appears as an AE or a PD, and it holds point-of-care data that cannot be rebuilt later, which is part of what DR6 asks. The Surgeon Learning Curve Tracking Log (SLCT) holds the case sequence with its basis of comparison and each review (Appendix I).

4.3.3 Device-specific complication monitoring

Complication monitoring follows the clinical events that may relate to the investigational device or to the procedure, including how the two interact. It covers AEs and DDs and follows most closely the complications, those that arise from the procedure or the device. An early complication whose relationship to the device is still unclear is point-of-care data that cannot be recovered once lost (Section 3.4.3). The component answers DR8 and part of DR6, and it protects CtQ domain 10.

Each event already has a reporting route that starts at the site. ISO 14155:2026 asks the PI to record every AE and observed DD with its categorization and to report the serious ones to the sponsor without unjustified delay, and since the fourth edition an AE associated with a DD follows both pathways. Under the MDR, the sponsor then reports any SAE with a possible causal relationship to the device, the comparator, or the investigation procedure, together with device deficiencies that might have led to an SAE. These duties treat each event alone, and none of the three instruments asks the site to review its events together for a pattern.

Several events that each have a plausible explanation may share a cause, such as one implant component or one surgeon, and the shared cause shows only when the events are reviewed together. If the site waited for the sponsor's reporting cycle, more cases could share that cause before anyone saw it, so the site reviews its own events at least once a month, on its own calendar. Complication monitoring starts from events that have already happened. The risk register works earlier, on weaknesses in a process, such as an implant identifier that may not be captured before the operating room workflow closes.

Each event enters a site log as it occurs, whether first seen in the operating room or later, with references to its source records and its safety route, so the log indexes the events without repeating them. At entry the site records a preliminary view of what the event may relate to, which the investigator's final classification confirms or replaces. At each review the site counts the events by category, component, and surgeon against the number of procedures, so each count is a rate. A signal is a rate above a threshold set in advance or a sharp rise since the previous period, and the review also flags a category not seen before and events that concentrate in one surgeon. Each signal is assessed for its likely cause. A signal tied to one surgeon goes to the learning curve review, one that points to the device goes to the sponsor through the deficiency route, and a pattern in a site process enters the risk register or corrective and preventive action.

Under ISO 14155:2026 the sponsor considers establishing a CEC, which reviews and classifies events from all centers by consensus. The site review is a local counterpart with its own denominator and triggers. We found no evidence that either level detects a signal first, so the two have different roles: the site acts when its own trigger is met, and a local signal that may matter beyond the site goes to the sponsor, where it feeds the assessment of the CEC. The Site Complication Monitoring Log (SCML) indexes each event with its route (Appendix J), the Safety and Adverse Effect Reporting Template (SART) follows each safety case from opening to closure (Appendix K), and the Complication Signal Surveillance Template (CSST) holds each periodic review with its counts and signals (Appendix L).

4.4 Pillar 3: Operational Controls

Operational Controls set how critical activities are carried out and checked, so that each procedure is done the same way from case to case and leaves a record from which it can be reconstructed. The pillar answers Gap 4, the absence of a monitoring function that the site starts and runs itself, together with DR4, DR7, and part of DR6 and DR11. Under the MDR all investigation information has to be recorded and stored so that it can be accurately reported, interpreted, and verified, and the CIP sets the policy on deviations and device accountability. ISO 14155:2026 turns these duties into requirements on source documents, data, and device accountability at the site.

A procedure as the protocol describes it and the same procedure as a team performs it on a given day rarely match exactly, and this pillar works on the gap between them. Part of that gap is hard for an outside party to see in time, because local conduct can drift between sponsor visits and differences in imaging often show only when images from different time points are placed side by side.

4.4.1 Internal verification of site conduct

Internal verification is a function the site runs itself to review its own conduct and documentation at set intervals, so that a local failure is found and corrected inside the site, and it answers DR4. Under the MDR the sponsor ensures monitoring through a monitor independent of the site, and ISO 14155:2026 and ICH E6(R3) also place monitoring within the sponsor's quality control, as does FDA guidance on risk-based monitoring [79]. These texts ask the site to support that monitoring and to apply the corrections it receives, and none asks it to check its own conduct between visits (Gap 4). Internal verification covers that interval and replaces no part of the sponsor's monitoring, which the law keeps independent of the site.

The review concentrates where execution most often goes wrong in an implant investigation, where operating room practice meets implant logistics and documentation, and later in imaging and follow-up. Its effort follows the risk to participants and to the data, as risk-based monitoring asks of the sponsor, and ten checks set its scope.

- Sterile release to use. Package integrity, expiry, and storage are confirmed before use, and any breach of sterile handling or intraoperative substitution is reconciled.

- Implant traceability and component reconciliation. Lot number, serial number, or UDI agree across the operative note, the accountability record, the implant labels, and the CRF, and the implanted configuration matches the plan.

- Instrumentation and operating room readiness. The correct implant system and instrument set were available, and any incompatibility or malfunction is documented as a procedural issue or, where it applies, as a DD.

- Operative documentation. The operative note has enough detail to interpret the final configuration and the intraoperative decisions later, including substitutions, deviations, and complications, because this record cannot be repaired afterward.

- Imaging usability and timing. Required imaging was obtained within its window with the correct views and quality, and it was repeated when it was not.

- Follow-up execution. Each visit produced the outputs that surveillance and endpoint assessment need.

- Endpoint data agreement. For the endpoint-critical fields that are recorded first outside the CRF, a sample of CRF entries agrees with the source, and any discrepancy is corrected and explained.

- Timeliness and closure. Notes, data entry, queries, and corrective actions close within the set times. The EMA reflection paper gives delays in data entry and SAE reporting as possible triggers for targeted monitoring but does not show that a backlog predicts a broader failure, so the framework treats delay as a signal to check.

- External context. The site reviews literature, registry findings, safety alerts, and field notices for the same implant class or procedure.

- Complication monitoring. The complication reviews ran on their cadence and their signals were followed up, while the analysis of the pattern itself stays with complication monitoring.

Before the first participant is enrolled, the site sets for each check how often it is reviewed, on what sample, by whom, and what triggers an earlier review. The reviewer should not be the person whose work is under review. Where the staff allow it, someone separate from daily execution does the review, and in a small team it is shared with a trained peer or kept under documented PI oversight. For each check the reviewer records whether the planned control was carried out, whether the record confirms it, and whether the control worked. A negative or uncertain finding always leads to an action or a linked record, and the follow-up confirms that the correction worked. Because the review runs on the site's own cadence, it also serves as the periodic self-inspection that DR5 asks for, and findings that need correction go to corrective and preventive action. The Internal Monitoring Plan (IMP) sets the scope and cadence of the review (Appendix M), and the Internal Monitoring Checklist (IMC) records each review with its findings and actions (Appendix N).

4.4.2 Imaging standardization

Imaging standardization controls the variation that the site itself can introduce into imaging-derived information, and it answers DR7 for CtQ domain 9. In a shoulder investigation images are used from the confirmation of anatomical suitability before surgery to the position of the components at follow-up, and the classifications applied to them already have limited reliability. Variation in how images are acquired therefore adds error that the database cannot separate from real clinical difference.

Trial-imaging guidance has developed mainly outside orthopedics, in core-imaging and central-reading work that specifies acquisition, equipment, and site qualification in advance, together with the split between local and central reading [56, 61, 80]. Shoulder studies show how much the acquisition itself matters. In images reconstructed from cadaver scapulae, views only 5 degrees from true anteversion changed the critical shoulder angle by more than 2 degrees [81], and in a cadaveric study measurements stayed stable only within about 9 degrees of malrotation [82]. In routine practice only 22% of anteroposterior films met the standard projection [83]. Simulated changes in scapular position produced ranges of 14 and 17 degrees in arthroplasty inclination measures [85], while standardized outlet views reached an interobserver intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.91 for the acromiohumeral distance [84].

The component has four elements, all working within the sponsor's imaging charter, so the site never keeps a second standard of its own. First, the site maps each requirement of the charter, such as the views and the acquisition window at each time point, onto a workflow its own radiology can follow, records any gap, and submits this readiness assessment to the sponsor before activation, which keeps the decision to activate with the sponsor. Second, the people who acquire and assess the images are trained for these requirements and released through the training records, and where a measurement depends on the assessor, agreement between assessors is tested before the first participant. ISO 14155:2026 also makes the PI responsible for the maintenance and calibration of the equipment used in the investigation.

Third, before the first image is acquired, the site fixes who reviews its images locally, against which criteria, and how a non-conforming image is handled. Each required image set is logged as it is acquired, with its timing, technical quality, and retrieval reference, and an image set that falls short leads to a repeat acquisition or a second review while either can still help. Central review, where the sponsor uses it, stays outside the site's control, so the site can claim only the consistency of its own review. Fourth, the site schedules each image within the CIP window at a moment when a reproducible position can be obtained. Positioning drives the measurement and inadequate positioning is common, but we found no study on how often pain, dressings, or a sling prevent a reproducible film after surgery, so the framework treats this as an operational inference. Where a scheduled time proves unsuitable, the site documents it and raises it with the sponsor, which can define an acceptable window. The Site Imaging Readiness Record (SIRR) holds the mapping of the charter and the readiness assessment (Appendix O), and the Imaging Quality Log (IQL) holds the record of each image set (Appendix P).

4.4.3 Eligibility and follow-up records

The decision to enroll and each follow-up visit depend on records that the site makes about one participant at a time. They protect CtQ domains 2 and 7, participant selection and follow-up retention, and the eligibility record answers the part of DR7 that concerns structured verification of eligibility. In a shoulder investigation eligibility often depends on anatomy read from images and on the planned procedure, and an error at inclusion can become irreversible once the implant is in place.

The site documents each eligibility decision criterion by criterion against the current CIP, with the source of the evidence behind each criterion and the date it became available. Consent comes before any assessment that requires it, and the decision is recorded before enrollment, so the record shows that the evidence existed when the decision was made. A criterion that depends on imaging interpretation or on information from several sources receives a second, independent review. An unresolved disagreement goes to the PI, and the participant is not enrolled until it is resolved. The investigator signs the decision, so it can be reconstructed later without relying on undocumented knowledge.

Every person considered for the investigation also enters a site log with a code and the outcome of screening, including the reason for any non-inclusion or withdrawal, with the key to the code kept apart. ISO 14155:2026 requires each site to log all enrolled subjects with a code linked to their identity and allows the log to cover everyone pre-screened. At each review the site reconciles the log with the eligibility records, so no candidate disappears without a recorded reason, and the reasons for non-inclusion feed the enrollment quality indicator.

Each follow-up visit has its own record, which states whether the visit took place within its window and which components required by the CIP for that time point were completed, with the result of each and any exception. A visit counts as usable only when the CtQ assessments for that time point were done by the specified method and within the window, since attendance alone does not show that a visit was complete. A late or incomplete visit is recorded with its reason and its effect on the data, and an unexpected finding goes to clinical review and, where it applies, to the safety route. The review of follow-up across participants belongs to Performance Oversight.

The Participant Screening and Eligibility Assessment Record (PSEAR) holds each eligibility decision (Appendix Q), and the Subject Identification and Failure Screening Log (SIFSL) holds the log of everyone screened (Appendix R). The Participant Follow-up Visit Record (PFVR) holds each visit (Appendix S).

4.4.4 Source documentation and parallel records

Source documentation belongs to the site's own documentary system, and the framework works through that system. ISO 14155:2026 requires the site team to keep source documents whose type and location are documented, with documents and data traceable, and ICH E6(R3) asks the investigator to define before the trial what counts as a source record, where it is kept, and how data are captured, avoiding unnecessary transcription. A source definition agreed in advance prevents uncertainty over which record is authoritative [86]. The site therefore decides, for each critical datum, which record is its source, a clinical record or, where the CIP allows it, the CRF itself, and adapts its existing system so that the records the CtQ domains depend on, from device traceability to imaging and follow-up, are captured and linked. Each instrument then points to that source, and the framework adds no documentation system of its own. This work serves CtQ domain 11.

The operating room is the one place where the framework adds a source record, because the data generated there cannot be rebuilt afterward. During each procedure the site completes an intraoperative checklist and device log, which captures the identifiers of each implanted component and the checks of each critical step as they happen, together with any deviation or substitution and the immediate review after surgery. The log is the source for what it captures first and points to the operative note and the device accountability record for everything else, so it supports both without replacing either. It answers the part of DR6 that concerns intraoperative traceability and deviation capture.

Any site-level framework risks turning its instruments into a second, divergent record of information that already exists, and this one has enough records, among them a risk register and a learning curve log, for the risk to be real. Some instruments hold data with no other home, such as the sequence of a surgeon's cases or the analysis of a complication signal, and others record how the site works, such as delegation and oversight. In both cases no instrument may become a parallel record of a datum that already has a defined source, so an instrument that needs participant-level information refers to the source, cites it when it carries a value for a count or a comparison, and gives way to it if the two differ. The learning curve log records that a component substitution occurred and points to the operative note, and the complication log records the classification and the reporting action without becoming a second clinical narrative. Where the same event is still documented differently in two places, the discrepancy is itself a finding for internal verification.

Keeping to the site's existing records follows from DR11, since a parallel documentation system would add work and cost that the target center can hardly sustain and would create the duplication that this rule prevents. The framework therefore stays additive, and its instruments are built around identifiers and decisions. The Intraoperative Checklist and Device Log (ICDL) holds the record of each procedure (Appendix T).

4.5 Pillar 4: Performance Oversight

Performance Oversight turns the conduct of the investigation into signals that the site can measure and act on, and it closes the loop through correction. It answers Gap 5, the absence of a proactive improvement cycle at site level, through DR5, and DR8 through a small set of indicators, drawing on the measurement and improvement clauses of the quality management standards on which the framework was modeled.

An indicator is included when it lets the site recognize a loss of control while correction is still possible. Six indicators meet this criterion, each reading a process whose failure reaches participant protection or the validity of the investigation. Enrollment quality, follow-up compliance, safety reporting timeliness, and critical data completeness answer domains 2, 7, 10, and 11, from which Table 3.4 derives DR8. The PD rate answers domain 12, whose recurring deviations feed the correction cycle of DR5, and surgeon procedural consistency answers domains 5 and 6, summarizing for each period the learning curve that DR3 asks the site to follow.

The indicators are local signals that the site defines for its own processes, and they differ from the quality tolerance limits that the sponsor sets for the trial as a whole. The site fixes the rule of each indicator before the first participant, with the values that separate a process under control from one that needs closer review or action, and does not change a rule once a result is known. These values are provisional, because we found no published site-level threshold for most of them, and the site calibrates them with experience. With the small numbers of a single site, each result is shown as a count over its denominator, and as a percentage only when the denominator is large enough to keep it stable.

The indicators read records that the framework already keeps, from the eligibility and visit records to the safety and learning curve records, so the pillar collects no new data. Where a signal or a finding of internal verification calls for correction, the corrective and preventive action cycle traces it to its cause and checks that the correction worked, and at close-out the site turns what it learned into changes to its standing procedures. The Performance Indicator Record (PIR) holds the rule, the result, and the decision of each indicator for each review period (Appendix U).

4.5.1 Enrollment quality

Enrollment quality measures whether each inclusion was correct and supported by evidence available before the decision. It answers CtQ domain 2, where an error affects participant safety and the comparability of the population. The eligibility record carries out the control, and the indicator shows whether the control worked. Recruitment volume stays outside the indicator, in the screening log.

At each review period the indicator takes every inclusion decision of the period as its denominator, since a single site enrolls few participants and a sample would leave most decisions unread. It counts the inclusions in which every critical criterion was evidenced before the decision, the eligibility errors or clarifications found after enrollment, and the important deviations related to eligibility, and it flags a criterion that proves ambiguous across several decisions. Reviews are more frequent during the first cases and after a major protocol amendment.

The process is under control when every critical criterion was evidenced before every decision. Evidence completed after the decision but before surgery calls for closer review, and any eligibility error found after enrollment, or any important eligibility deviation, calls for action. Recurrent uncertainty, or uncertainty that concentrates in one criterion or one assessor, leads to clarification and training, and goes to the sponsor when the criterion itself is unclear. A failure that recurs after that response enters corrective and preventive action.

4.5.2 Protocol deviation rate

The PD rate is read as a profile of where deviations occur, how much they matter, and whether they recur or concentrate, because a single percentage can hide a few events in consent or safety reporting that matter more than many minor ones. It is reported by area, each with its own denominator, and it answers CtQ domain 12. Whether a deviation is important follows the sponsor's criteria, which ICH E6(R3) describes as deviations that may significantly affect the reliability of trial data or a participant's rights, safety, or well-being, and at the site the PI applies those criteria.

The indicator reads the deviation record kept under the CIP's policy on deviations, whether the site or the sponsor holds it, and each deviation is entered when it is found, including in the operating room. Procedural deviations are counted per procedure and visit-window deviations per expected visit. Recurrence shows that a process may be failing, and concentration shows whether deviations cluster around one surgeon, one assessor, a type of visit, or a phase of the study.

An important deviation receives prompt case review, and minor deviations are trended at the monthly oversight meeting. The process is under control when no important deviation has occurred and the minor ones stay below the number set in advance for each area. Several minor deviations in one area, or a minor type that recurs, call for closer review, and any important deviation calls for action. A repeated important deviation or a cluster within one area, as defined in advance, leads to an investigation of the underlying process, and a correction that needs more than retraining or closer oversight enters corrective and preventive action or goes to the sponsor.

4.5.3 Follow-up compliance

Follow-up compliance measures whether the follow-up schedule produces usable data, which depends on attendance, timing, and the completeness of each visit together. It answers CtQ domain 7 and follows from the operational weight of follow-up, since ROM, imaging, and PROMs require the participant to return over several years. The MDR asks the CIP to set procedures for participants lost to follow-up and the sponsor to complete their follow-up, and the indicator shows whether the site delivers its share. It reads the visit records and applies their definition of a usable visit.

At each review period the indicator counts the visits completed within their window over the visits expected, and the visits holding every component the CIP requires over the visits completed. It also counts the visits missing a component needed for an endpoint, over the visits that required it, and the participants whose follow-up could not be obtained despite documented efforts, over the participants due. From the reason recorded at each visit, the review attributes each failure to its main cause, the participant, the site's own process, or the protocol design, so that retention problems are not treated as site failures and the response reaches the cause. The method by which components were obtained is checked when this is planned in advance or when omissions recur, since checking every visit would be hard to sustain at the target center.

The process is under control when the share of visits within window stays at or above a value set in advance and no component needed for an endpoint is missing. A share between that value and a second, lower one, or one visit missing such a component, calls for closer review. A share below the lower value, two or more visits missing such a component, or a participant lost despite documented efforts calls for action. A missing component that no endpoint depends on calls for closer review when it recurs. The trend is read early, while reminders, scheduling changes, or retraining can still protect the later visits, and a cause in the protocol design goes to the sponsor. A failure that recurs after that response enters corrective and preventive action. The Follow-up Compliance Review (FUCR) holds this review for each period, with the attribution of causes (Appendix V).

4.5.4 Safety reporting timeliness

Safety reporting timeliness measures whether each reportable event reaches the sponsor within the interval the CIP sets, with a complete first report and follow-up information completed afterward. It answers CtQ domain 10. Complication monitoring asks what the events show together, and this indicator asks whether the reporting route worked for each one. It covers the SAEs and the DDs that could have led to a SADE. The MDR asks the sponsor to have an agreement under which the investigator reports these events in a timely manner, without fixing an interval, and ISO 14155:2026 leaves the detail to the CIP, which may also reduce the recording or reporting of AEs when the ethics committee approves it in advance. The indicator therefore applies the window and the scope of the CIP, and the sponsor's reporting to the authorities lies outside it.

For each reportable event the indicator reads the SART and notes whether the initial notification reached the sponsor within the CIP interval, counted from when the site became aware of the event, and whether the first report was complete. It also notes whether follow-up information arrived within its interval and which cases remain open at the end of the period, each count read over the reportable events of the period. A late or incomplete report is traced to the stage where the delay arose, from recognition of the event to transmission, and the correction is directed at that stage. A recurring delay in recognition, for example, leads to retraining in safety reporting.

Here the line between control and action is the CIP interval itself, so the site does not choose a value of its own. The process is under control when every initial notification was made within that interval and every first report was complete. A first report that needed correction, or late follow-up information, calls for closer review, and any late initial notification, or a case open beyond the interval the CIP allows, calls for action and goes to the PI. A late initial notification is also entered in the deviation record, where the sponsor's criteria decide whether it is important. The trend is read at the monthly review of complications, where delays that repeat at one stage lead to a change in that stage, and a delay that recurs after that change enters corrective and preventive action.

4.5.5 Surgeon procedural consistency

Surgeon procedural consistency summarizes, for each surgeon and review period, whether the delivery of the investigational procedure is stable and aligned with the CIP. It answers CtQ domains 5 and 6, follows from operator dependence, and gives Performance Oversight a periodic reading of the learning curve that DR3 asks the site to follow. It reads the learning curve log, the intraoperative records, and the complication review, and it reports for each surgeon the unplanned substitutions and procedural deviations, the operative duration against comparable cases, the support or proctoring used, whether implant configuration and position were documented, and the early technical complications.

Each of these signals has more than one possible cause. A substitution may reflect planning, implant availability, case complexity, or execution, operative duration means something only against cases of the same complexity, and senior support is expected early in a surgeon's experience with the device. An early complication is linked to procedural delivery only after clinical and technical review. The indicator therefore reads the signals together, against the surgeon's own earlier cases, treats no single one as proof of poor performance, and does not rank surgeons. A comparison between surgeons would have to adjust for case mix, including previous shoulder surgery, glenoid retroversion, younger age, rheumatoid arthritis, and implant size [87], and treat the participants of one surgeon as a cluster [67, 68, 69], which the few cases of a single site seldom allow. With those numbers a signal prompts a structured clinical review and is not a statistical test.

The review is more frequent during a surgeon's first cases and after a new device, technique, or indication. The process is under control when support followed the plan and any substitution or deviation was isolated and had a non-technical cause. One signal with an unclear cause, one operation outside the expected duration for its complexity, or a gap in the documentation of implant position calls for closer review. The same signal twice in one period, an early complication in which the device or technique is suspected, or operative duration outside the expected range at two reviews calls for action, which is an assessment of the surgeon's sequence in the learning curve log. That assessment decides the response and returns the matter to the credentialing decision when the authorization may need to change.

4.5.6 Critical data completeness

Critical data completeness measures whether the data behind the endpoints and the safety analysis are present, current, and consistent with their source while the investigation runs. It answers CtQ domain 11. ISO 14155:2026 sets the condition that the site's records must meet (Section 4.4.4), but we found no clause that asks the site to measure how far its records have drifted from it, and this indicator does so.

Three of its measures are read monthly from records the site already keeps: the share of endpoint-critical fields completed within the interval set for each visit type, the number of open queries with their median age, and the share of operative notes finalized on time. The fourth is read at each cycle of internal verification, as the share of sampled CRF entries that agree with their source, among the endpoint-critical fields recorded first outside the CRF. Measuring during conduct matters because a problem found early can still be corrected. Among sites flagged by central statistical monitoring and then followed up, data quality improved at 83%, against 56% of sites in historical studies without that monitoring [44]. That evidence comes from sponsor-level monitoring and supports acting early on a data signal, which this indicator does at the site.

The process is under control when each monthly measure stays within the value set in advance and every sampled entry agrees with its source. A single measure outside its value, such as one late operative note, calls for closer review. A shortfall beyond a second, lower value, repeated late notes, an uncorrected discrepancy between source and CRF, or two monthly measures worsening for two consecutive months calls for action. A failure that recurs after that response enters corrective and preventive action.

4.5.7 Closing the loop through corrective and preventive action

Each indicator ends in corrective and preventive action when its first response does not resolve a failure, and the same route receives the findings of internal verification, the signals of complication monitoring and of the learning curve, and the findings of the sponsor's monitoring, audits, or inspections. The indicators and controls detect a failure, and this cycle removes its cause, so it is the part of the pillar that answers Gap 5 through DR5. ISO 14155:2026 already asks the PI to determine the cause of significant non-compliance and to put corrective and preventive action in place, and the monitor checks that such actions were implemented and work, but that duty starts from a non-compliance that is already significant.

The site's cycle starts earlier, because the thresholds of each indicator and review are set before the first participant, so a signal opens a corrective action before an external finding does and before a failure becomes significant. The cycle also runs on the site's own calendar. Where the center already holds a morbidity and mortality review, complications are discussed there too, so the clinical review of an event and the quality review of the process behind it inform each other.

Each corrective action follows the steps that ISO 13485:2016 requires of a manufacturer, adapted to the site. The site reviews the problem and determines its cause, decides whether action is needed to prevent recurrence, plans and documents the action, checks that it does not compromise compliance with the CIP or participant safety, and reviews whether it worked. When an indicator opened the action, the action worked if that indicator returns under control in the following reviews. An action closes only when that review shows it was effective, or when a documented limitation explains why its effect cannot yet be judged. Preventive action follows the same steps for a failure that has not yet happened, such as a weakness found in one process that may also exist in a similar one.

The result returns to the site risk register, because a corrective action that reveals a weakness in a process adds a risk to it. The risk receives a control and a review trigger, and a signal from that trigger opens the next corrective action, so the output of correction becomes an input of risk management. ICH E6(R3) asks the sponsor to run this cycle for the trial, with root cause analysis and corrective and preventive action when non-compliance is significant, and the framework runs it at the level of the site. The Corrective and Preventive Action Log (CAPA) holds each action with its cause, the review of its effectiveness, and its closure (Appendix W).

4.6 The quality lifecycle

Current guidance asks that quality be planned before a study begins and managed across its lifecycle [3, 4], and the lifecycle of the framework applies that idea to the work of the site. It answers DR9, which asks that each instrument be assigned to the phase where it has most effect, because a control that is correct but mistimed does not do its job. Once an implant is fixed, an error made at surgery cannot be corrected like a transcription error, so the controls on the surgical pathway have to be in place before the first procedure. A surgeon learns during conduct, so a learning curve log started after the first cases records the plateau and misses the interval that mattered. Data generated at the point of care, such as the identifiers of an implanted component, are lost if they are not recorded when they arise (DR6).

The milestone reviews of the oversight model mark points of decision and the review cadences set how often a control runs, so the lifecycle adds the moment at which each instrument starts. It divides the work of the site into seven phases, with a safety stream across the phases of conduct, and each phase gathers the CtQ domains whose work takes place in it.

4.6.1 Phases, gates, and the safety stream

The seven phases fall into three groups (Figure 4.1). Preparation covers feasibility and start-up, conduct covers enrollment, surgery, and follow-up, and closure covers close-out and continuous improvement. Feasibility is the site's own assessment of whether it can conduct the investigation, which informs the sponsor's selection and covers CtQ domain 1. Start-up sets up the four pillars before any participant data exist, from training and credentialing (domain 6) to the reception and storage of the device. Enrollment covers domains 2 and 3, surgery domains 4 and 5, and follow-up domains 7, 8, and 9, while data capture and deviation management, domains 11 and 12, run through every phase.

The phases of preparation and closure belong to the site and follow one another, and each ends when the PI confirms its exit condition at a milestone review. The phases of conduct belong to each participant, so one participant may be in screening while another is in follow-up, and for the site each phase opens with its first case and closes with its last. The passage that carries most weight is the end of start-up, when the site confirms that it is ready to enroll and records its implementation level (Section 4.7).

Safety surveillance runs as a stream across the phases of conduct, from the first enrolled participant to the last follow-up assessment, and protects CtQ domain 10. It is the only control bound to external reporting intervals and the only one that links events from different phases, since a complication seen in follow-up may come from a decision made at surgery. Its review is held at least once a month, and the record of each implanted unit stays current for as long as the participant is followed.

Continuous improvement closes the cycle, because the question of what an investigation taught the site about how it runs investigations can be fully answered only once conduct is complete. Correction does not wait for it, since corrective and preventive action runs from the first case and a weakness found in one investigation reaches the others that the site is running. The closing phase turns what the corrections and the risk register recorded into changes to the site's standing procedures, so the lessons do not depend on the memory of people who may have left, as DR10 requires. The Post-Investigation Debrief and Lessons Record (PIDLR) holds the debrief and its lessons (Appendix X), and the next investigation starts from the procedures that Phase 7 leaves in place.

4.6.2 Where each instrument acts

Fifteen of the twenty-four instruments act in start-up (Figure 4.2), a phase that produces almost no participant data and in which the site decides whether the data that follow will be usable. The training and credentialing decisions, the risk register, and the readiness of imaging are completed there, and the rules of most other instruments, from the criticality grades to the indicator thresholds, are fixed before the first participant. The records of each participant open only in the phase where their data arise, and the indicators and the complication review say little until cases accumulate. Only the milestone reviews act in every phase, and the debrief acts only in the closing phase.

[Figure 4.2. The twenty-four instruments placed in the phases of the lifecycle, with an open marker where an instrument is prepared and its rules are fixed and a filled marker where it is in use.]

Within start-up, the order follows what each instrument reads from another. The training requirements and the criticality grades come before anyone is released for a task, the release on device training comes before the credentialing decision that draws on it, and the plan for internal verification comes before its first review.

Four orders follow from the gaps that the framework answers, and none of the instruments compared in Chapter 3 imposes them. The risk register opens before the first participant, because a register opened later records risks already taken (Gap 2). The learning curve log fixes its basis of comparison before the first case, so each surgeon's curve starts with that surgeon's first case (Gap 3). Internal verification has its scope and cadence set before conduct, in its plan or on its checklist, so it runs on the site's own calendar (Gap 4). The rule of each indicator in use is fixed before the first participant, so a signal can open a corrective action before an external finding does (Gap 5).

Whether failures also concentrate in particular phases is a separate question, which the evidence answers only in part. Surgical complications may cluster early in a surgeon's learning curve [22], and loss to follow-up accumulates over the later years [24], but these findings do not show that a site that activates its controls by phase does better than one that acts when the sponsor asks. The organization by phases is therefore a design hypothesis, consistent with ICH E8(R1), which directs quality resources toward critical activities and risks. If the controls of conduct work, close-out should also find less to correct, a benefit this thesis has not measured.

4.7 The scalability model

The framework was designed for the nonhospital organization, whose conditions set the minimum implementation level, but hospitals hosted 239 of the 347 site records in the registry sample (68.9%), so a framework that fitted only the target center would leave out most of the settings where these investigations take place. The scalability model answers DR12, which asks that the depth of implementation follow the resources of the center and the risk of the investigation while the documentation trail stays complete at every depth. Scaling to risk applies the proportionality that ICH E6(R3) asks of quality management, and scaling to resources is a choice of this design, which holds only because the documentation trail does not scale. What scales is the number of instruments in use, and each instrument keeps the same form at every level. The three levels follow the organizational environments of Table 3.3, by what the framework can assume stands behind the site, and the risk of each investigation adds instruments within each level.

The framework can scale because each instrument describes a control function, so a function that a site does not run as a separate instrument can be covered by another record it already keeps. Each level therefore has a recommended minimum set, twelve instruments for a nonhospital organization, eighteen for a nonacademic hospital, and all twenty-four for an academic organization, and each instrument left out has a proxy, another record that the site keeps and that takes on its function, named in Table 4.2. A site that can run more instruments than its minimum should run them.

The minimum level is that of the target center, where no hospital system or university stands behind the site and the clinical staff carry the research work. Its twelve instruments cover the duties the site already holds and give each gap at least one answer. Oversight, delegation, training, and credentialing rest on the POT, the RACI, the TRE, and the SCR, the risk register opens before the first participant, and every safety case is followed in the SART. The eligibility decision, the record of each procedure, and the log of each image set are kept, internal verification runs through its checklist, follow-up is reviewed in the FUCR, and the lessons are drawn in the PIDLR. The six functions may be held by few people, each with a named substitute, so no CtQ function depends on one individual, and where the separation that DR10 asks for cannot be complete, the operational constraint prevails (Section 3.5). Review is never done by the person who did the work, and it never falls below the cadence that the pillars fixed, such as the monthly review of safety cases.

In a nonacademic hospital, the hospital system stands behind the site with services such as radiology and controlled storage, and more people can share the review. The recommended minimum rises to eighteen, and the six added instruments are those that need time for analysis and planning: the learning curve log, the complication log with its signal review, the plan for internal verification, the indicators, and the corrective action log. With them every gap in Table 3.1 has its full answer.

In an academic organization, a university stands behind the hospital, and the site may already have a research unit and an institutional quality system. All twenty-four instruments are recommended, and the six added at this level give formal structure to larger teams, from the graded delegation matrix and the milestone review log to the training requirements matrix and the site's records of imaging readiness, screening, and visits. Where the institution already keeps training records or a corrective action system, the instruments refer to them, as DR11 asks, and add what such a system was not built to hold, the conduct of the site at the level of the named surgeon, device, and investigation. Clinical trial units still differ in the services they provide [89, 90].

[Table 4.2. The scalability model, with the recommended minimum set of instruments at each of the three implementation levels, which follow the organizational environments of Table 3.3, and the proxy for each instrument left out.]

The categories of Table 3.3 describe what kind of organization a site is and say little about how it is prepared for research. In one survey, stakeholders from higher-level hospitals performed better on average, but substantial room for improvement remained [88], and shortages of staff and infrastructure are also reported in hospitals and academic centers (Section 3.4.4). The level therefore states what the framework can assume. A site that lacks the support its category assumes runs the set of the level below, and a nonhospital organization with research capacity of its own can run a higher one. The PI records the level at the review that closes start-up and revisits it when the capacity of the site changes, for example when the coordinator leaves. Repeated institutional self-assessment of this kind has been associated with better capability to conduct research, although the association could not be shown to be causal [30].

Within each level, the risk of the investigation adds instruments, domain by domain, where the risk register rates a domain as high. Strong operator dependence, as with a new implant design or technique, calls for the learning curve log, limited classification reliability for the imaging and training controls of the higher levels, and a long follow-up for closer review of retention. A nonhospital site that implants a new design therefore adds the learning curve log to its twelve instruments. Where the risk calls for more than the site can run, the site draws on the sponsor's support, such as proctoring, or declines the investigation at feasibility.

What does not scale is the evidence that the investigation was conducted as required. The MDR requires all investigation information to be recorded, handled, and stored so that it can be accurately reported, interpreted, and verified, and it requires Member States to inspect investigation sites. A site at the minimum level is therefore protected only as far as its records stay complete, consistent, and retrievable. The records required by ISO 14155:2026 and the MDR, from consent, delegation, and training to device accountability, deviations, adverse events, and source documents, stay the same at every level and degree of risk, and the framework only fixes where each one sits. The instruments in use also keep their rules at every level, so the risk register covers all twelve domains wherever it is kept, and the record of each procedure is made in the operating room.

4.8 The operational instrument set

Since none of the regulatory texts examined requires a site to operate a quality system (Gap 1), Section 3.5 argued that the framework can be justified only by tracing each of its parts back to its origin. Sections 4.2 to 4.5 traced that origin instrument by instrument (Table 4.3). A site deciding whether to adopt the framework would raise another objection, because it already keeps many records and a set of well-founded forms can still hold a problem that nobody reads. A site can note an unplanned component substitution in the operative note and again in the device accountability record, and the substitution can recur in later cases until a monitor finds it, since nobody at the site read the two records together. The framework therefore has to be justified as a whole. Its records have to connect, so that a record made in one place reaches a decision in another, and its parts have to be complete and necessary, with a control for every domain and an origin for every instrument.

Each instrument works at one of three levels (Figure 4.3). At the lowest, a record holds the evidence of one event or one person, such as one procedure in the ICDL or the release of one person in the TRE. Above it, a synthesis reads several of these records together, such as the learning curve log for one surgeon or the follow-up review for one period. The decisions about how the site runs, from the delegation matrix to the risk register, are at the top and rest on those syntheses. Information rises through the levels, and a decision returns to the lowest one as the requirement that the next record is checked against. In a site that runs the full set, the substitution above takes this path. The ICDL records it in the operating room and the learning curve log reads it in the sequence of that surgeon's cases, so the indicator of surgeon procedural consistency can report it for the period. If the pattern holds, the PI records a decision in the POT, which may return the surgeon to proctoring through the credentialing record, and from then on the ICDL of that surgeon's cases is checked against the new condition. The event is therefore recorded under Operational Controls and decided under Governance, after being read under Risk Management and Performance Oversight, and for this reason the instruments were designed only after the functions of all four pillars had been defined.

Only five instruments ask for work at the clinical moment, the PSEAR, the ICDL, the IQL, the PFVR, and the SART, and they sit where Chapter 3 placed the data that cannot be recovered once lost. The other nineteen work on records that already exist, either those five or the clinical records of the site, so the set meets DR11 and the rule on source documentation. The same division explains why the minimum sets of Section 4.7 still close the loop. In a smaller set, the synthesis that a dedicated instrument would carry, such as the learning curve log or the indicators of the PIR, falls to the oversight meeting held under the POT. The records of the clinical moment stay at every level, four of them as instruments and the visit record through its proxy, so a site at a lower level gives up the dedicated reading of a signal and keeps the evidence that would show it. How much periodic work the nineteen represent for a small team has not been measured.

Across the full set, every CtQ domain has at least one instrument that carries its control, and every design requirement that one instrument can answer on its own has an instrument that answers it directly (Figure 4.4). No domain is therefore left to a general duty, and every instrument either carries the control of a domain or answers a requirement directly, so none is in the set without an origin. Some instruments answer no design requirement on their own and are in the set only because they carry the control of a domain, as the training and credentialing records do for domain 6 and the visit record does for follow-up. An indicator works the other way, since it measures whether the control carried by another instrument worked, so the PIR and the FUCR are in the set because DR8 asks the site for indicators that expose drift. A site that checked the set only against the requirements would therefore leave out the records that carry the control of a domain, and a site that checked it only against the domains would leave out the indicators. DR9 is met by the phase assigned to every instrument (Figure 4.2), and DR11 and DR12 by the way the set is used, since the instruments work through the documentary system of the site and the minimum sets of Table 4.2 keep at every level the records that the regulations require.

Domains 5, 6, 8, and 9 rest wholly or partly on requirements that none of the regulatory texts examined assigns to the site, so their control is carried by instruments of the site's own, from the credentialing record and the learning curve log to the ICDL and the imaging records. Most of these directly answer the requirements that Section 3.5 derived from the four domains, so they are the controls that the framework adds to what the regulations ask of the site. In the other domains the control falls mostly where the regulations already place a duty on the site, such as the delegation of tasks or the reporting of adverse events, so there the framework organizes work that the site already owes. Where it adds a control in these domains, the addition answers a requirement from Chapter 3, as the review of complications as a pattern answers DR8.

Consent rests on the eligibility record alone, because the regulations already set its process in detail and Chapter 3 gave it no requirement beyond the risk register. The control of endpoint assessment is thinner, since it rests on the visit record alone, which the follow-up review reads, while the qualification and consistency of the assessor, which the domain also covers, sit in the training records. When the endpoints of an investigation depend on readings that vary between observers, the site therefore adds the DTRM on the basis of its risk register, as Section 4.7 describes. The design shows that the records connect and that each part has an origin, but it cannot show whether a site reads what it records in time to act, because that depends on the people who run the set and on the time they have.

Draft templates for all twenty-four instruments are in Appendices A to X. They were developed for shoulder device investigations, and their content reflects that context mostly in the complication categories and the imaging requirements, and in the complexity levels of the learning curve log. Their structure, from the fields to the escalation logic, was kept as general as that context allowed, so adapting an instrument to another surgical specialty would in most cases mean changing its classification lists, although no such adaptation has been tested. The templates are provided in an open, editable format, so a site can match them to the layout of its own documents and add or remove fields, while the function that each instrument carries stays the same.
