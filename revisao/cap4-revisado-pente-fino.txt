4. Results: The Quality Framework

4.1 Overview and design rationale

This chapter reports the third DSRM activity, the design and development of the framework to meet DR1 to DR12. It describes each component in enough detail for a site to implement and operate it.

The Site-Centered Operational Quality Management Framework consists of four pillars, each with a set of instruments. A lifecycle assigns the instruments to the phases in which they are prepared and used (DR9, Section 4.6), while a scalability model determines which instruments a site runs according to its organizational resources and the risk of the investigation (DR12, Section 4.7). The role structure within Governance assigns each quality function to a defined person and separates authority from responsibility for individual tasks (DR10, Section 4.2.2).

Governance, Risk Management, Operational Controls, and Performance Oversight together answer DR1 to DR8 (Sections 4.2 to 4.5). Section 3.2.1 showed that, of the two organizations producing the evidence behind the clinical evaluation report, only the site works without a quality system of its own. We therefore drew on the functions of the systems that the manufacturer operates under ISO 13485:2016 and ISO 14971:2019. We also drew on ISO 15189:2022, which gives a quality system to another organization whose results others rely on, the medical laboratory [52, 53, 54]. The four pillars bring those functions into a site-level structure, although none of the three standards uses this exact division.

Governance draws on the management clauses of the three standards (ISO 13485 Clause 5, ISO 14971 Clause 4, and ISO 15189 Clause 5). Risk Management draws on ISO 13485 Clauses 4.1.2 and 7.1, ISO 14971 Clauses 5 to 7, and ISO 15189 Clauses 5.6 and 8.5. Operational Controls draws on ISO 13485 Clause 7, including Clause 7.5, and ISO 15189 Clause 7. ISO 14971 does not contribute to this pillar because it does not address the control of routine processes. Performance Oversight draws on the measurement and review clauses of all three standards (ISO 13485 Clauses 8.2.4, 8.4, and 8.5, ISO 14971 Clauses 9 and 10, and ISO 15189 Clauses 8.7 to 8.9).

The instruments form one set that can work on paper or within an electronic system the site already uses (DR11). This operational constraint takes precedence wherever it conflicts with another requirement (Section 3.5), so no component depends on new software or new staff. We designed the instruments after defining the functions of all four pillars. Each instrument therefore belongs to one pillar but can use records produced under another.

We developed the framework for the target center described in Section 3.4.5, a nonhospital organization where it could assume the least organizational support. The conditions of that center shaped the instruments and set the minimum implementation level. Because the components are described as functions, centers with different staffing structures can assign their own posts to those functions.

Each instrument describes a control function, so a site that already keeps a record performing that function uses it without opening a new one. The instruments are not regulatory requirements and do not replace sponsor procedures or the controls defined in the CIP. Records already required by the regulations remain in place. A new record is justified only where a critical process would otherwise lack a control or a record of its execution.

4.2 Pillar 1: Governance

The regulatory instruments examined assign several quality duties to the investigator, including supervision of delegated activities and assurance that the site team is qualified. However, none requires a structure that brings those duties together (Gap 1). Governance provides that structure for DR1 and DR10, in CtQ domains 1 and 6, while leaving each duty with the party that already holds it. It defines who has authority for quality at the site, how that authority is delegated, and the conditions that staff must meet before undertaking their tasks. These conditions are especially relevant in a surgical device investigation, where results depend on the surgeon as well as the device.

The oversight model defines how the PI exercises this authority, and the delegation structure identifies who may perform each task. Device training documentation establishes the preparation required for those tasks, with a separate qualification and authorization decision for surgeons. Once a surgeon starts operating, Risk Management tracks the learning curve and provides evidence for subsequent authorization decisions (Section 4.3.2).

4.2.1 The oversight model

During design, we compared three ways of allocating oversight under different conditions of staffing and capacity. The descriptions of trial-site operations we reviewed confirm that these functions need to be covered [73, 74], but they do not classify them into these arrangements. We therefore use the three arrangements as design options.

In the first arrangement, the PI intervenes directly in most critical activities and personally reviews most documentary and operational workflows. This can appear to provide close supervision, but it becomes difficult to sustain as activity grows. Site PIs already carry scientific and regulatory duties alongside recruitment, supervision, and administration, and their contribution is often under-recognized [73]. Routine administrative review can then take time from decisions that need the PI's clinical judgment, such as confirming surgical appropriateness, interpreting an intraoperative deviation, or assessing whether a complication is related to the investigational device. The arrangement also scales poorly because the PI would have to follow every area of the investigation, from the operating room and radiology to follow-up, implant accountability, AE management, and data QC.

In the second arrangement, the study coordinator manages daily work while the PI retains final authority. This is a workable allocation: the clinical research coordinator is a core site function [74], and ICH E6(R3) permits delegation while the investigator retains ultimate responsibility and proportionate oversight [6]. FDA guidance on investigator supervision takes the same position [75]. Oversight becomes nominal, however, if the site leaves the decisions requiring the PI undefined and establishes neither a review structure and frequency nor a route for escalation. In a surgical investigation, the PI may need to decide on borderline eligibility, an unplanned intraoperative change such as a component substitution, or the interpretation of a revision. The PI also needs to assess inconsistencies whose clinical meaning cannot be established from the documentation alone.

We adopted a third arrangement, a tiered model of PI oversight adjusted to risk. It retains delegation and adds the review structure, schedule, and escalation route missing from the second arrangement, so the PI can concentrate on decisions requiring clinical judgment. The PI's involvement in each task follows the risk it poses to participant safety, procedural integrity, device accountability, and data reliability. This applies the CtQ logic of ICH E8(R1) to the allocation of the investigator's attention [5], using the CtQ factors identified when the investigation starts (Section 4.3.1). The site must therefore set thresholds and a review schedule before enrolling the first participant and assign someone to keep the meetings running throughout the investigation.

Five mechanisms make this oversight a documented and scheduled practice, as DR1 requires. The criticality grade assigned to each delegated task provides the basis for the other mechanisms.

- Risk-tiered involvement. For each type of activity, the site states in advance which decisions require the PI's own judgment and which activities the PI can review afterward. This distinction depends on the device and procedure and may change as the team gains experience, so the site revisits it at each oversight cycle. Requiring direct involvement too often recreates the workload of the first arrangement, while requiring it too rarely can leave the PI without meaningful oversight. The Criticality-Coded Delegation Matrix (CCDM) records the supervision level for each task (Appendix A).

- Criticality grading of delegated activity. Each task receives a grade that determines its level of supervision and its escalation trigger in the context of the investigation. Coordinating a routine visit is administrative work, but a scheduling decision can place a follow-up outside the protocol window. Similarly, an intraoperative note may later be needed to reconstruct a disputed complication. The grades therefore come from the CtQ factors of the investigation itself, because a scheme copied from another site may overlook risks specific to its procedures or team. The CCDM records these grades.

- Milestone review. The PI formally reviews the investigation at four points where the most consequential threats concentrate: procedural implementation, the immediate postoperative period, the approach to endpoint assessment, and close-out. A review must take place after the events whose problems it needs to identify, but early enough to correct a pattern before it affects further cases. These points reflect the risk profile of a shoulder implant investigation and would need revision for another device or procedure. The Milestone Review Log (MRL) records each decision (Appendix B).

- Event-triggered escalation. Events that immediately threaten participant safety or causal interpretation require prompt notification to the PI, whatever the scheduled review date. The CCDM defines the trigger for each task, and the PI Oversight Template (POT) records the PI's decision and the resulting action (Appendix C).

- Oversight meetings. The site team meets regularly to conduct the review, giving oversight a structure beyond ad hoc contact. We adapted the consensus-based review of events that ISO 14155:2026 assigns to a sponsor's clinical events committee (CEC) [8] and the periodic risk review described in the EMA reflection paper [1] to this site-level meeting. We found no study showing that such forums improve oversight at site level. Maintaining them is particularly difficult in the nonhospital organizations targeted here, because the people attending are also responsible for the work being reviewed. Each meeting closes one cycle of the POT.

4.2.2 Delegation, roles, and functions

The PI can adjust supervision to a task's criticality only if the site knows who performs it and whether that person is authorized and trained. Staff also need to know who must act when an escalation trigger is met. The role structure makes these responsibilities explicit under DR10, which requires defined holders for quality functions and a distinction between authority and task-level responsibility, so that no CtQ function stops when one person is unavailable.

A conventional delegation log records who performs each trial-related activity. ICH E6(R3) asks for this record in proportion to the significance of the activity and accepts that work performed as part of clinical practice may need no delegation record (Section 2.3.3). ISO 14155:2026 requires the names, signatures, functions, and authorizations of new team members to be documented. Neither requires a criticality grade or evidence of competence in the same record. The framework needs both to connect the supervision assigned to each task with the training or qualification that permits a person to perform it. Delegation records are examined directly at inspection, and the investigator remains accountable for delegated work [76]. The record therefore has three properties.

- Task-based and graded. Each entry identifies a specific task and its criticality grade, which sets how closely the PI reviews it. Authorization for one task does not imply authorization for another. A research nurse may be well placed to coordinate visits and keep screening logs but may not be qualified to resolve a clinically meaningful discrepancy in operative documentation.

- Competency-gated. An entry becomes active only after the person has been released for the task following training or, for a surgeon, qualified and authorized for the procedure (Sections 4.2.3 and 4.2.4).

- Version-controlled. Each change of person, task, or grade is dated. The record can therefore show who was authorized at any point in the investigation, including after the people named in it have left, as DR10 requires.

The role structure comprises six functions covering the work of an orthopedic implant investigation, each with a defined limit of authority (Table 4.1). Under ISO 14155:2026, delegation transfers execution while the PI retains responsibility. The limits of authority distinguish responsibility for individual tasks from the authority to make decisions. Clinical judgments remain with the PI or surgeon, and endpoint adjudication belongs to the central reader where one is used.

[Table 4.1. The six functions of the role structure, with the main responsibilities and the limit of authority of each.]

A site assigns its own posts to the six functions. In a nonhospital organization, where clinical staff also perform research work, one person may hold several functions. Each function must still have a named holder and backup so that no CtQ function depends on one individual. Services outside the study team, such as radiology or physiotherapy, fall under the same proportionate oversight of delegated activities in ICH E6(R3). Where the sponsor selects or supplies the provider, the site coordinates that oversight with the sponsor. The CCDM records each task's grade and prerequisite, together with the required supervision and escalation trigger. The Responsibility Assignment Matrix (RACI) identifies the current holder and backup for each activity and the limit of the holder's authority (Appendix D).

4.2.3 Device training documentation

Before assigning a task, the site uses the training records to check that the person has received the preparation it requires. Monitors and inspectors use the same records as evidence that critical activities were performed by staff who were both delegated and prepared for the investigation.

Under the MDR, people conducting an investigation must be adequately trained in the proper use of the investigational device, the CIP, and GCP. The sponsor verifies and documents this training and arranges it where necessary [10]. ISO 14155:2026 makes the PI responsible for training the site team and requires new members to complete documented training before starting their assignment. The sponsor ensures that this training is documented and verified. ICH E6(R3) limits trial-related training to what delegated activities require beyond a person's usual training and experience, while ICH E8(R1) asks that training match each role and take place before the person becomes involved in the study.

The sponsor defines the training content through the investigation documents and risk controls. The MDR requires the CIP to state the training and experience needed to use the device and the IB to include information on any relevant training. ISO 14155:2026 asks the same of both documents and requires the sponsor to consider how far risk controls depend on training, such as cadaver training or support to users during the investigation. The texts examined thus place the content and verification of training with the sponsor and the duty to train the team with the PI, but none of them specifies the training needed for each site role and task, how readiness for independent work is decided, or when training needs updating. This component organizes the site's existing duty around those decisions.

Medicinal-product GCP also requires personnel familiar with the investigational product. In a device investigation, training must additionally cover the functions linked to the device and its use. The framework groups these into five training areas, each protecting one or more CtQ domains.

- Protocol and procedural training: the study objectives, the key eligibility restrictions, the visit windows that matter for endpoints, PDs, and the decision points specific to the study.

- Device training: the investigational system, its instrumentation and implant components, preparation and handling, and any constraint the study places on the workflow.

- Operative and perioperative documentation: intraoperative records, component reconciliation, capture of implant lot and UDI, documentation of substitutions and deviations, and chain of custody.

- Assessment training: radiographic acquisition standards and imaging timing, ROM measurement, and PROM administration where these bear on an endpoint.

- Safety reporting: recognition and documentation of AEs and DDs, and the escalation pathway for clinically important findings.

Each training record identifies the person and training area, the date and trainer, and the version of the controlled document used. After a revised IFU or amended CIP, the site can therefore identify everyone trained on the earlier version.

The site records completion of training separately from release to perform a task. Attendance at a session shows that training took place, but does not establish that the person can perform the task independently. For tasks graded as critical in the delegation record, particularly those involving the device, imaging, or outcome assessment, the site records an explicit release once training is complete. This release activates the competency gate in the delegation record and, for surgeons, provides one input to the authorization decision. A person performing only routine clinical care receives no device-specific training unless the CIP requires it.

The site retrains staff when the work changes through a protocol amendment, revised IFU, new device iteration, or altered workflow. Deviation records and monitoring findings may also indicate a need for retraining, for example after a safety signal or repeated documentation errors. Responding to these findings follows ICH E8(R1), which recommends retraining when issues related to CtQ factors arise during the study. These records support DR10 by linking each function to a defined training requirement and a recorded release. The Device and Study Training Requirements Matrix (DTRM) specifies what each role or task requires, and the Training Record Evaluation and Release (TRE) records each person's training and release (Appendices E and F).

4.2.4 Surgeon qualification and continued authorization

Under the MDR, everyone conducting an investigation must be suitably qualified for their tasks, and the sponsor must show that the investigator and site are capable of conducting it. ISO 14155:2026 assigns the sponsor the selection criteria and verification of qualification before the investigation starts. The PI documents the team's qualifications, maintains an adequate team throughout the investigation, and ensures that only authorized users handle the device. The sponsor therefore sets the entry threshold through the CIP and its training requirements, in line with the study risk assessment. The site documents that each surgeon assigned a study procedure meets that threshold.

A specialty credential establishes a surgeon's general professional standing, while competence in the specific procedure and readiness to use the investigational device require separate consideration. ISO 14155:2026 already distinguishes experience in the field of application from training in the investigational device for the PI (Clause 10.2(b)). A surgeon experienced in a comparable procedure may still be unfamiliar with the technique required for this device. Before the first case, the site therefore assesses how far the sponsor's entry criteria and the surgeon's release on device training establish readiness, and whether anything remains open. The resulting decision places the surgeon in one of four categories: credentialed, conditional, restricted, or not credentialed. Its link to the delegation record ensures that assigned surgical tasks match the current authorization.

Where the risk assessment or expected learning curve calls for closer supervision, the site may add temporary controls proportionate to the surgeon's experience with the procedure and device. These may include proctoring, co-surgery, or an initial assignment to less complex cases, within the requirements of the investigation. The surgeon holds a conditional authorization until the conditions recorded in the decision have been met.

The site reviews qualification at least once a year while the surgeon operates on study participants, and after a protocol amendment, safety signal, cluster of complications, or evidence of drift in performance. Learning curve tracking and complication monitoring under Risk Management provide evidence from the surgeon's own sequence of cases for this review. Each reassessment uses the same four categories, allowing authorization to be restricted or withdrawn and later restored. None of the three regulatory instruments examined specifies how observed performance should inform the PI's duty to maintain an adequate team (Gap 3). The framework closes that gap by linking performance to continued authorization through defined triggers and graded decisions, giving the monitoring required by DR3 a route to action. The Surgical Credentialing Record (SCR) records each decision and its evidence, and the RACI carries the resulting authorization into the delegation record (Appendix G).

4.3 Pillar 2: Risk Management

The site needs to identify where its own conduct may fail and control those risks before they affect participants or the data. Risk Management provides the site risk record missing in Gap 2 and the monitoring of operator learning called for by Gap 3. It answers DR2 and DR3, together with parts of DR6 and DR8. Because execution can affect any of the twelve CtQ domains, the pillar covers all of them, but includes only risks that threaten a CtQ factor.

The texts examined address risk at device and trial levels. The manufacturer manages device risk under ISO 14971, and the MDR requires the CIP to describe the risks and clinical benefits of the device under investigation. At trial level, ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) ask the sponsor to manage risks to critical factors through a cycle from identification to review. The nonbinding EMA reflection paper describes that cycle in operational terms. ISO 14155:2026 keeps the risks of using the device within ISO 14971 and identifies two further sources: the clinical procedures required by the CIP beyond routine practice, which the fourth edition added, and the conduct of the investigation itself. It assigns management of these risks to the sponsor.

The third level concerns the risks created by the way an individual site carries out the protocol. None of these texts assigns an owner to a separate risk cycle at this level. The sponsor's cycle has to work across centers with different staff and working practices, so it cannot be built around the weaknesses of one center. In an orthopedic device investigation, these local weaknesses can affect both clinical outcomes and the methods used to assess them. Most of the structural conditions described in Chapter 3 act at the site, including operator dependence and the limited reliability of classifications, and the first signs of device-related complications also appear there. The site's knowledge of its own processes therefore puts it in a position to identify and control these local risks.

4.3.1 The site risk register

A 2023 survey of 46 Portuguese clinical trial sites found that 57.0% reported using a systematic risk management tool, but only nine (19.6%) described a standard tool or document that captured risk analysis at site level [77]. Most sites (87.0%) were willing to use a tool built for their own needs. Among the reasons given against using one, lack of knowledge of risk management was the main concern. Although limited to one national survey, this was the closest measurement of Gap 2 that we found.

The register records risks arising from the site's own execution of the investigation. Around surgery, these may include incomplete capture of implant identifiers or late documentation of an intraoperative deviation. Later, they may arise from inconsistent image acquisition or follow-up visits outside their windows. The site opens the register before enrolling the first participant so that controls can be put in place before these failures occur. It uses the protocol and the sponsor's information on critical factors and risks to identify, within each CtQ domain, the factors that its own conduct can affect. Each local risk is then linked to the factor it threatens, with its cause and consequence recorded.

The site rates each risk by its impact on the CtQ factor and by how likely it is to occur and remain undetected, following the risk logic of ICH E8(R1). The rating helps keep the register focused enough for the PI to review at each oversight cycle. Each entry also names an owner from the role structure and records the control in place, the residual risk, and the trigger for reassessment. The named owner is responsible for acting on the entry, and the trigger lets a change in local conditions prompt a review before a failure occurs. A risk with possible effects beyond the site goes to the sponsor. The Site Risk Register (SRR) documents this process from identification to reassessment, as DR2 requires, and holds each risk with its CtQ factor and control plan (Appendix H).

4.3.2 Operator-dependent risk and the learning curve

In a surgical device investigation, the procedure forms part of the intervention, adding a source of operator-dependent risk that a medicinal product does not carry. The surgeon's technique and decision thresholds can affect protocol adherence and implant traceability as well as endpoints and clinical outcomes, with effects across the cases performed by that surgeon. The instruments compared in this thesis control operator learning at entry but give the site no role in observing it during conduct (Gap 3). The framework therefore includes monitoring during conduct that is specific to the surgeon, procedure, and device, as DR3 requires.

Experience is associated with shorter operative times and fewer complications, together with better PROM scores [20], although studies define and measure proficiency differently. A systematic review of 13 studies estimated that about 25 reverse total shoulder arthroplasties were needed to reach an acceptable point on the learning curve. For anatomic arthroplasty, which only two studies had examined, the range was 16 to 86 cases. In a multicenter analysis of operative duration, three of four early-career surgeons reached a plateau between 12 and 86 cases [78]. These figures describe learning in those samples, so the framework does not use them as proficiency thresholds and sets no fixed case count of its own.

The site tracks each surgeon's cases to assess whether variation is becoming a material risk to its CtQ factors. This follows ICH E8(R1), which calls for periodic review of CtQ factors as new issues arise during a study and risk controls may need adjustment. The learning curve is one such issue at a surgical site. Existing SQA frameworks deal with it mainly at selection [37, 39], while the Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure has no indicator for a surgeon's procedural consistency during a trial [41].

Before the first case, the site defines the levels of case complexity relevant to the investigation, the operative duration expected for comparable cases, and the support a surgeon should receive early on, such as proctoring, and these definitions form the basis for comparison. The record of each case then includes its complexity and duration, with any unplanned event such as a component substitution or a need for senior support. A slow operation has a different meaning in a complex case and in a routine one, and its interpretation also depends on the surgeon's experience with the device. Each case is therefore compared with the same surgeon's earlier cases at the same level of complexity. This avoids most of the case-mix problem of comparisons between surgeons, although changes in complexity over time still need to be considered.

The site reviews the sequence after the first cases and at set points afterward. A pattern such as repeated substitutions or a cluster of early technical complications prompts an earlier assessment. Signals from complication monitoring are also checked against the case sequence before they are attributed to the device or the operator, so that normal variation is not taken for procedural drift.

The review may confirm stable execution or lead to closer observation, renewed device training, or temporary proctoring. A material or recurrent pattern requires formal escalation, and a material pattern also enters the risk register or corrective and preventive action. If the response would restrict the surgeon's scope, it returns to the credentialing decision under Governance because it changes the authorization to operate. Recording cases as they occur can reveal drift before it appears as an AE or a PD and preserves point-of-care data that cannot be reconstructed later, and so answers part of DR6. The Surgeon Learning Curve Tracking Log (SLCT) holds the case sequence, its basis of comparison, and each review with the resulting decision (Appendix I).

4.3.3 Device-specific complication monitoring

The site reviews clinical events for possible relationships with the investigational device, the procedure, or their interaction in use. Monitoring covers AEs and DDs, with particular attention to complications arising from the procedure or device. An early complication whose relationship to the device remains unclear is one example of the point-of-care data that cannot be recovered once lost (Section 3.4.3). This component answers DR8 and part of DR6 within CtQ domain 10, safety surveillance and adverse device event management.

Each event already has a reporting route that begins at the site. ISO 14155:2026 asks the PI to record and categorize every AE and observed DD and to report the serious ones to the sponsor without unjustified delay, with the detail specified in the CIP. Since the fourth edition, an AE associated with a DD follows both the event and deficiency pathways. Under the MDR, the sponsor then reports without delay any serious adverse event (SAE) that has, or may reasonably have, a causal relationship with the device, the comparator, or the investigation procedure. This duty also covers device deficiencies that might have led to an SAE and new findings on these events. These requirements deal with each event on its own, and none of the three instruments compared in this thesis asks the site to review its events together for a pattern.

Several events with plausible individual explanations may share a cause, such as one implant component or one surgeon. Reviewing them together can reveal that relationship while the site can still act on it. If the review waited for the sponsor's reporting cycle, further cases could be affected before the pattern was recognized. The framework therefore requires the site to review its own events at least once a month, on a schedule independent of the sponsor's. This review starts from events that have occurred, while the risk register works on weaknesses that may cause a future failure, such as an implant identifier that may not be captured before the operating room workflow ends.

The site logs each event when it occurs, whether it is first observed in the operating room or at a later clinical contact. The entry refers to the source records and reporting route, allowing the log to index events without repeating their clinical narrative. It also records a preliminary assessment of what the event may relate to, which the investigator's final classification later confirms or replaces.

At each review, the site counts events by category and examines their distribution by component and surgeon. Each count is read against the number of procedures performed. A rate above a threshold set in advance, or a sharp rise since the previous period, constitutes a signal. The review also flags new event categories and events concentrated in one surgeon. The site assesses each signal for its likely cause and directs it to the relevant process. A signal tied to a surgeon goes to the learning curve review, while one that points to the device goes to the sponsor through the deficiency route. A pattern in a local process enters the risk register or corrective and preventive action.

Under ISO 14155:2026, the sponsor considers establishing a CEC to review and classify events from all participating centers by consensus. The local review uses the site's own denominator and triggers. We found no evidence that either level detects a signal first, so the design gives the site a defined point for action without assuming that it detects signals sooner. When a local trigger is met, the site acts, and signals that may matter beyond the site go to the sponsor to inform the CEC's assessment. The Site Complication Monitoring Log (SCML) indexes events and their routes (Appendix J), while the Safety and Adverse Effect Reporting Template (SART) follows each safety case from opening to closure (Appendix K). The counts and signals from each periodic review are recorded in the Complication Signal Surveillance Template (CSST) (Appendix L).

4.4 Pillar 3: Operational Controls

Critical activities need consistent methods and records that allow the site to reconstruct what happened. Operational Controls define how these activities are carried out and checked, in answer to Gap 4, the absence of a monitoring function started and run by the site itself. The pillar answers DR4 and DR7, together with parts of DR6 and DR11. Under the MDR, investigation information has to be recorded and stored so that it can be accurately reported, interpreted, and verified, and the CIP sets the policy on deviations and device accountability. ISO 14155:2026 specifies the requirements for source documents, data, and device accountability at the site.

The controls address differences between the procedure described in the protocol and its execution by the team. Some differences are difficult for an outside party to identify in time, because local conduct can drift between sponsor visits and variation in imaging may become apparent only when images from different time points are compared. The site therefore needs to check its own work during conduct. It also needs to control the records used for those checks, since additional forms can introduce conflicting versions of the same information.

4.4.1 Internal verification of site conduct

The site reviews its conduct and documentation at set intervals to identify and correct failures within its own responsibilities, as DR4 requires. Under the MDR, the sponsor ensures monitoring through a monitor independent of the site. ISO 14155:2026 and ICH E6(R3) also place monitoring within the sponsor's quality control, as does FDA guidance on risk-based monitoring [79]. These texts require the site to support monitoring and apply the corrections it receives, but none requires it to check its own conduct between visits (Gap 4). Internal verification covers this interval alongside the sponsor's monitoring, which remains independent of the site.

The review focuses on the points where operating room practice meets implant logistics and documentation, and on later imaging and follow-up. Its effort follows the risk to participants and data, applying at the site the principle that risk-based monitoring uses for sponsor oversight, and ten checks define its scope.

- Sterile release to use. Package integrity, expiry, and storage are confirmed before use, and any breach of sterile handling or intraoperative substitution is reconciled.

- Implant traceability and component reconciliation. Lot number, serial number, or UDI agree across the operative note, accountability record, implant labels, and CRF, and the implanted configuration matches the plan.

- Instrumentation and operating room readiness. The correct implant system and instrument set were available, and any incompatibility or malfunction is documented as a procedural issue or, where applicable, a DD.

- Operative documentation. The operative note contains enough detail to interpret the final configuration and intraoperative decisions, including substitutions, deviations, and complications. The record needs to preserve these details because missing information cannot be reliably reconstructed later.

- Imaging usability and timing. Required imaging was obtained within its window, with the correct views and technical quality, and was repeated when it did not meet those requirements.

- Follow-up execution. Each visit produced the outputs required for surveillance and endpoint assessment.

- Endpoint data agreement. For endpoint-critical fields first recorded outside the CRF, a sample of CRF entries agrees with the source, and discrepancies are corrected and explained.

- Timeliness and closure. Notes, data entry, queries, and corrective actions are completed within the set times. The EMA reflection paper identifies delays in data entry and SAE reporting as possible triggers for targeted monitoring. It does not establish that a backlog predicts broader failure, so the framework treats delay as a signal to investigate.

- External context. The site reviews literature and registry findings, together with safety alerts and field notices, for the same implant class or procedure.

- Complication monitoring. Complication reviews took place at the planned intervals, and their signals were followed up. The analysis of the pattern itself remains part of complication monitoring.

Before enrollment begins, the site defines the frequency and sample for each check and assigns a reviewer, together with the trigger for an earlier review. The reviewer should be someone other than the person who performed the work. Where staffing permits, a person separate from daily execution undertakes the review. A small team can share this work with a trained peer or conduct it under documented PI oversight. Each review covers a defined period or responds to a trigger and can include all ten checks or a selected subset.

For each check, the reviewer records whether the planned control was carried out and whether the record confirms it, then assesses whether the control worked. A negative or uncertain finding leads to an action or a linked record, followed by a check of the correction's effectiveness. Because this review follows the site's own schedule, it also provides the periodic self-inspection required by DR5. Findings that need correction enter the corrective and preventive action process under Performance Oversight. The Internal Monitoring Plan (IMP) defines the scope and schedule (Appendix M), and the Internal Monitoring Checklist (IMC) records each review with its findings and actions (Appendix N).

4.4.2 Imaging standardization

Images support decisions throughout a shoulder investigation, from confirming anatomical suitability before surgery to assessing component position at follow-up. The classifications applied to them already have limited reliability, so variation in acquisition adds error that the database cannot distinguish from a real clinical difference. This component controls the variation introduced by the site and answers DR7 for CtQ domain 9, imaging acquisition, interpretation and endpoint control.

Trial-imaging guidance has developed mainly outside orthopedics, where core-imaging and central-reading work specifies acquisition methods, equipment, and site qualification in advance, along with the division between local and central reading [56, 61, 80]. Shoulder studies show how sensitive measurements can be to acquisition. In images reconstructed from cadaver scapulae, views only 5 degrees from true anteversion changed the critical shoulder angle by more than 2 degrees [81]. Another cadaveric study found stable measurements only within about 9 degrees of malrotation [82], while only 22% of anteroposterior films in routine practice met the standard projection [83]. Simulated changes in scapular position produced ranges of 14 and 17 degrees in arthroplasty inclination measures [85]. With standardized outlet views, the interobserver intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for acromiohumeral distance reached 0.91 [84].

The site applies the sponsor's imaging charter through four elements, beginning with a readiness assessment before activation. It maps the charter's requirements, including views and acquisition windows, onto a workflow its radiology service can follow. Any gaps are recorded, and the assessment is submitted to the sponsor, which retains the activation decision.

The people who acquire and assess images must be qualified and trained for those requirements, with release documented through the training records under Governance. Where a measurement depends on the assessor, agreement between assessors is tested before the first participant. ISO 14155:2026 also makes the PI responsible for maintenance and calibration of equipment used in the investigation, and its fourth edition added calibration to the content of the CIP.

Before the first image is acquired, the site defines who reviews images locally, the criteria used, and how nonconforming images are handled, including when a second reviewer or clinical escalation is needed. Each required image set is logged at acquisition with its timing, technical quality, and retrieval reference. A set that falls short leads to repeat acquisition or a second review while these can still resolve the problem. Central review, when used by the sponsor, requires coordination across sites and remains outside the site's control. The site can therefore claim consistency only for its own review.

The site also schedules imaging within the CIP window at a time when a reproducible position can be obtained. The studies above show that positioning affects measurement and that inadequate positioning is common, but we found no study measuring how often pain, dressings, or a sling prevent reproducible postoperative imaging, so the need to account for these conditions remains an operational inference. If a scheduled time proves unsuitable, the site documents the problem and raises it with the sponsor, which can define an acceptable window. The Site Imaging Readiness Record (SIRR) documents the mapping of the charter and the readiness assessment (Appendix O), and the Imaging Quality Log (IQL) records each image set (Appendix P).

4.4.3 Eligibility and follow-up records

The decision to enroll and each follow-up visit depend on records made for an individual participant. These records protect CtQ domains 2 and 7, participant selection and follow-up retention. The eligibility record also answers the part of DR7 that calls for structured verification of eligibility. In a shoulder investigation, this decision often depends on anatomy interpreted from images and on the planned procedure. An error at inclusion can become irreversible once the implant is in place.

The site documents each eligibility decision criterion by criterion against the current CIP, identifying the source of the evidence and the date it became available. Consent precedes any assessment that requires it, and the decision is recorded before enrollment so that the record shows what evidence was available at the time. Criteria that depend on imaging interpretation or information from several sources receive a second, independent review. Any unresolved disagreement goes to the PI, and enrollment waits until it is resolved. The investigator then signs the decision, leaving a record that can be reconstructed without relying on undocumented knowledge.

Every person considered for the investigation also enters a screening log under a code, with the screening outcome and the reason for any non-inclusion or withdrawal. The key linking the code to the person's identity is kept separately. ISO 14155:2026 requires each site to log all enrolled subjects under a code linked to their identity and permits the log to include everyone prescreened. At each review, the site reconciles this log with the eligibility records to account for every candidate. The recorded reasons for non-inclusion feed the enrollment quality indicator under Performance Oversight.

The record of each follow-up visit states whether it occurred within its window and which components required by the CIP were completed. It records the result of each component, any exception, and the action taken. A visit counts as usable only when the CtQ assessments for that time point were completed by the specified method and within the window, since attendance alone does not show that these requirements were met. If a visit is late or incomplete, the site records the reason and the effect on the data. Unexpected findings and concerns raised by the participant go to clinical review and, where applicable, the safety route. Performance Oversight then reviews follow-up across participants and review periods.

The Participant Screening and Eligibility Assessment Record (PSEAR) documents each eligibility decision (Appendix Q), while the Subject Identification and Failure Screening Log (SIFSL) records everyone screened (Appendix R). Each follow-up visit is documented in the Participant Follow-up Visit Record (PFVR) (Appendix S).

4.4.4 Source documentation and parallel records

The framework uses the site's existing documentary system for source records. ISO 14155:2026 requires the site team to create and maintain traceable source documents with their type and location documented. ICH E6(R3) also asks the investigator to define, before the trial, what counts as a source record, where it is kept, and how data are captured, while avoiding unnecessary transcription. Agreeing on the source in advance prevents uncertainty about which record is authoritative [86]. The site therefore defines the source of each critical datum, whether a clinical record or, where the CIP allows it, the CRF itself. It adapts its existing system to capture and link the records needed across the CtQ domains, from device traceability and operative documentation to imaging and follow-up. The instruments refer to these sources, supporting CtQ domain 11 without introducing a separate documentation system.

The operating room is the one setting in which the framework adds a source record, because the data generated there cannot be reliably reconstructed afterward. During each procedure, the site completes an intraoperative checklist and device log. It captures the identifiers of implanted components and the checks of critical steps as they occur, together with deviations or substitutions and the immediate review after surgery. The log is the source for data it captures first. For other information, it refers to the operative note and device accountability record, supporting both without replacing them. The log answers the part of DR6 concerned with intraoperative traceability and deviation capture.

The number of instruments in the framework creates a risk of recording the same information in more than one place, where versions may diverge. Some instruments assemble information that is not brought together elsewhere, such as a surgeon's case sequence or the analysis of a complication signal. Others document how the site works, including delegation and oversight. In either case, information that already has a defined source remains authoritative there. An instrument that needs participant-level information refers to that source and cites it whenever a value is used for a count or comparison. If the two records differ, the source takes precedence.

For example, the learning curve log records that a component substitution occurred and refers to the operative note. The complication log records the classification and reporting action without creating a second clinical narrative. If an event is documented differently in two places, the discrepancy becomes a finding for internal verification.

Using the site's existing records follows DR11 because a parallel documentation system would add work and cost that the target center could have difficulty sustaining. The instruments therefore add local controls within the existing system and use identifiers to link records to the decisions based on them. The Intraoperative Checklist and Device Log (ICDL) holds the record of each procedure (Appendix T).

4.5 Pillar 4: Performance Oversight

Performance Oversight uses a small set of indicators to identify a loss of control during the investigation and guide the site's response. The indicators answer DR8, and the corrective and preventive action cycle responds to the absence of a proactive improvement cycle at site level, identified in Gap 5 and expressed in DR5. We based this pillar on the measurement and improvement clauses of the quality management standards used to develop the framework.

We included an indicator when it could help the site recognize a problem while correction was still possible. The six selected indicators monitor processes whose failure can affect participant protection or the validity of the investigation. Enrollment quality, follow-up compliance, safety reporting timeliness, and critical data completeness cover domains 2, 7, 10, and 11, from which Table 3.4 derives DR8. The PD rate covers domain 12 and identifies recurring deviations for the correction cycle of DR5. Surgeon procedural consistency covers domains 5 and 6 by summarizing, for each review period, the learning curve that DR3 asks the site to follow.

The site defines these indicators for its own processes and escalates a trend when it may affect a critical factor of the trial. Their scope differs from that of the quality tolerance limits the sponsor sets for the trial as a whole. Before the first participant is enrolled, the site fixes each indicator's rule and the values that distinguish a process under control from one requiring closer review or action. A rule cannot be changed once its result is known. We found no published site-level threshold for most of these indicators, so the proposed values remain provisional and the site calibrates them as it gains experience across investigations. Because a single site has few cases, each result is reported as a count with its denominator. A percentage is added only when the denominator is large enough to keep it stable.

All six indicators use records already kept within the framework, from eligibility and visit records to safety and learning curve records, so the pillar requires no additional data collection. When an indicator or a finding from internal verification calls for correction, the site investigates the cause and checks whether the corrective action worked. At close-out, the experience from these actions informs changes to the site's standing procedures. The Performance Indicator Record (PIR) records each indicator's rule and result, together with the decision made in each review period (Appendix U).

4.5.1 Enrollment quality

Enrollment quality measures whether each inclusion was correct and supported by evidence available before the decision. An error at this point can affect participant safety and the comparability of the population, which places the indicator in CtQ domain 2. It uses the eligibility records kept under Operational Controls to assess whether the eligibility checks worked. Recruitment volume is recorded separately in the screening log, which counts candidates screened and enrolled.

The denominator includes every inclusion decision made during the review period because, with few participants enrolled at a single site, sampling would leave most decisions unreviewed. The site counts inclusions for which every critical criterion was evidenced before the decision, along with eligibility errors or clarifications found after enrollment and important eligibility deviations. The review also flags criteria that were ambiguous across several decisions and is conducted more frequently during the first cases and after a major protocol amendment.

The process is under control when every critical criterion was evidenced before every decision. Evidence completed after the decision but before surgery requires closer review. Any eligibility error found after enrollment or any important eligibility deviation requires action. When uncertainty recurs or concentrates in one criterion or assessor, the site responds with clarification and training, involving the sponsor if the criterion itself is unclear. A failure that recurs after this response enters corrective and preventive action.

4.5.2 Protocol deviation rate

The PD rate addresses CtQ domain 12 by showing where deviations occur, their importance, and whether they recur or concentrate in a particular area. A single percentage could conceal a few events in consent or safety reporting that matter more than many minor deviations, so the site reports the indicator by area, each with its own denominator. The PI applies the sponsor's criteria for classifying deviations as important, which ICH E6(R3) describes as deviations that may significantly affect the reliability of trial data or a participant's rights, safety, or well-being.

The indicator uses the deviation record kept under the CIP's policy, whether the site or the sponsor holds it. Each deviation is entered when it is found, including during surgery. Procedural deviations are counted per procedure and visit-window deviations per expected visit, with a corresponding denominator for each other area. Recurrence can indicate a failing process, while concentration helps locate it in the work of a particular surgeon or assessor, a type of visit, or a phase of the investigation.

Important deviations receive prompt case review according to their urgency, and the site reviews trends in minor deviations at the monthly oversight meeting. The process is under control when no important deviation has occurred and minor deviations remain below the number set in advance for each area. Several minor deviations in one area, or a minor type recurring from the previous period, require closer review, and any important deviation requires action. If an important deviation recurs or deviations form a cluster within one area, according to the definition set in advance, the site investigates the underlying process. A correction requiring more than local retraining or closer oversight enters corrective and preventive action or goes to the sponsor.

4.5.3 Follow-up compliance

Follow-up compliance measures whether the schedule produces usable data through visits that take place on time and include the required assessments. It covers CtQ domain 7 and the operational demands of repeated ROM assessments, imaging, and PROM completion over several years. The MDR requires the CIP to set procedures for participants lost to follow-up and the sponsor to complete participant follow-up. This indicator assesses the site's contribution using the visit records and their definition of a usable visit.

For each review period, the site reports four counts, each with its own denominator:

- Visits completed within their window, out of the visits expected.
- Visits containing every component required by the CIP, out of the visits completed.
- Visits missing a component needed for an endpoint, out of the visits that required that component.
- Participants whose follow-up could not be obtained despite documented efforts, out of the participants due for follow-up.

The reason recorded at each visit allows the reviewer to attribute the failure to its main cause, whether it concerns the participant, the site's process, or the protocol design. This distinction helps the site choose an appropriate response and avoids automatically treating retention problems as failures of site execution. The reviewer also checks how the components were obtained when that check was planned in advance or when omissions recur. Requiring a second person to check every visit would be difficult to sustain at the target center.

The process is under control when the share of visits completed within window remains at or above the value set in advance and no component needed for an endpoint is missing. Closer review is required when this share falls between that value and a second, lower predefined value, or when one visit lacks a component needed for an endpoint. Action is required if the share falls below the lower value, two or more visits lack such a component, or a participant is lost despite documented efforts. Repeated omission of a component that no endpoint depends on also requires closer review.

The site reviews trends early enough for reminders, changes in scheduling or visit preparation, and retraining to help protect later visits. A cause related to protocol design goes to the sponsor, and a failure that recurs after the initial response enters corrective and preventive action. The Follow-up Compliance Review (FUCR) records each period's review and the causes assigned to the failures (Appendix V).

4.5.4 Safety reporting timeliness

Safety reporting timeliness measures whether each reportable event reaches the sponsor within the interval set by the CIP, with a complete first report and subsequent follow-up information. It covers CtQ domain 10 by checking whether the reporting process worked for each event. Complication monitoring examines what the same events show when reviewed together (Section 4.3.3).

The indicator covers SAEs and DDs that could have led to a SADE. The MDR asks the sponsor to have an agreement under which the investigator reports these events in a timely manner, without specifying an interval. ISO 14155:2026 leaves the reporting details to the CIP, which may also reduce AE recording or reporting when the reduction is justified and approved in advance by the ethics committee. The indicator therefore uses the CIP's reporting window and scope. The sponsor's reports to the authorities remain outside its scope.

For each reportable event, the site uses the SART to check whether the initial notification reached the sponsor within the CIP interval, measured from when the site became aware of the event, and whether the first report was complete. It also checks whether follow-up information reached the sponsor on time and records cases still open at the end of the period. Each count uses the reportable events of that period as its denominator. If a report is late or incomplete, the reviewer identifies where the delay arose, from event recognition and internal escalation through the investigator's assessment, access to documentation, and transmission. The site directs the correction at that stage, so repeated delays in recognizing an event, for example, lead to retraining in safety reporting.

The CIP interval determines the threshold for action, so the site does not set a separate value. The process is under control when every initial notification was timely and every first report was complete. A first report requiring correction or late follow-up information requires closer review. Any late initial notification or case remaining open beyond the interval allowed by the CIP requires action and referral to the PI. The site reviews a late initial notification as soon as it is found and enters it in the deviation record, applying the sponsor's criteria to determine whether it is important. Trends are reviewed at the complication review, at least once a month. Delays recurring at one stage lead to a change in that stage, and recurrence after the change enters corrective and preventive action.

4.5.5 Surgeon procedural consistency

For each surgeon and review period, this indicator assesses whether delivery of the investigational procedure is stable and aligned with the CIP. It covers operator dependence in CtQ domains 5 and 6 and gives Performance Oversight a periodic summary of the learning curve required by DR3. The summary draws on the learning curve log, the intraoperative records, and the complication review. It includes unplanned component substitutions and procedural deviations, operative duration in comparable cases, and the senior support or proctoring used. It also records whether implant configuration and position were documented and includes the early technical complications already counted by surgeon in the complication review.

A substitution may reflect planning or implant availability, case complexity, or the way the procedure was performed. Its interpretation therefore depends on the other records of the case. Operative duration is assessed against cases of comparable complexity, and senior support is expected early in a surgeon's experience with the device. Similarly, a link between an early complication and procedural delivery requires clinical and technical review. The site considers these signals together against the surgeon's own earlier cases, without treating any one signal as proof of poor performance.

The indicator does not rank surgeons, since a comparison would require adjustment for case mix, including previous shoulder surgery, glenoid retroversion, younger age, rheumatoid arthritis, and implant size [87]. It would also have to account for clustering among participants treated by the same surgeon [67, 68, 69], which the small number of cases at a single site seldom allows. These signals therefore prompt a structured clinical review and do not constitute a statistical test.

Reviews are more frequent during a surgeon's first cases and after the introduction of a new device, technique, or indication. The process is under control when support followed the plan and any substitution or deviation was isolated and had a non-technical cause. Closer review is required for a signal with an unclear cause, an operation outside the expected duration for its complexity, or a gap in the documentation of implant position. Action is required if the same signal occurs twice in one period, if the device or technique is a suspected cause of an early complication, or if operative duration falls outside the expected range at two reviews. The site then assesses the surgeon's case sequence in the learning curve log to decide the response. If authorization may need to change, the matter returns to the credentialing decision.

4.5.6 Critical data completeness

Critical data completeness addresses CtQ domain 11 by measuring whether the data needed for the endpoints and safety analysis are available and current during conduct, and whether they agree with their source. Section 4.4.4 described the conditions that ISO 14155:2026 sets for the site's records. We found no clause requiring the site to measure how far its records have departed from those conditions, so this indicator provides that assessment.

Three measures are calculated monthly from existing records: the share of endpoint-critical fields completed within the interval set for each visit type, the number of open queries with their median age, and the share of operative notes finalized on time. A fourth measure is calculated during each cycle of internal verification. Among endpoint-critical fields first recorded outside the CRF, it reports the share of sampled CRF entries that agree with their source.

Reviewing these measures during conduct gives the site time to correct problems. In an analysis of sites flagged by central statistical monitoring and then followed up, data quality improved at 83%, compared with 56% of sites in historical studies without that monitoring [44]. This evidence comes from sponsor-level monitoring and supports the decision to act early on a data signal, and the present indicator applies that approach at site level.

The process is under control when all three monthly measures remain within their predefined values and every sampled entry agrees with its source. One measure outside its value, such as a late operative note, requires closer review. Action is required for a shortfall beyond a second, lower value, repeated late notes, or an unresolved discrepancy between source and CRF. It is also required if two monthly measures deteriorate from the previous month in each of two consecutive months. A failure that recurs after the initial response enters corrective and preventive action.

4.5.7 Closing the loop through corrective and preventive action

An indicator leads to corrective and preventive action when the initial response does not resolve the failure. The same process receives findings from internal verification and from the sponsor's monitoring, audits, or inspections, together with signals from complication monitoring and the learning curve. It answers Gap 5 through DR5 by investigating the causes of the failures detected by the controls and indicators and acting to prevent their recurrence. ISO 14155:2026 already requires the PI to determine the cause of significant non-compliance and put corrective and preventive action in place, with the monitor checking implementation and effectiveness.

The framework extends this work to signals detected before a failure becomes significant or produces an external finding. Thresholds are set before the first participant is enrolled, and oversight meetings and internal verification provide a site-led schedule for reviewing the signals, independent of sponsor visits. Where the center already conducts morbidity and mortality reviews, complications are also discussed there so that the clinical assessment of an event informs the review of the underlying process, and vice versa.

We adapted the corrective action steps in ISO 13485:2016 for use at the site. The site reviews the problem and determines its cause before deciding whether action is needed to prevent recurrence. Where action is needed, the site plans and documents it, checks that it does not compromise CIP compliance or participant safety, and reviews its effectiveness. When an indicator prompted the action, effectiveness is assessed by whether that indicator returns under control in subsequent reviews. An action closes only when the review shows that it was effective or when a documented limitation explains why its effect cannot yet be judged. Preventive action follows the same steps for a failure that has not yet occurred, such as a weakness identified in one process that may also exist in a similar one.

If the investigation of a failure reveals a weakness in a process, the site records the resulting risk in the risk register with a control and a review trigger. An indicator or review then follows that trigger, and a further signal can initiate another corrective action. Findings from correction therefore inform the next cycle of risk management. ICH E6(R3) requires the sponsor to run this cycle for the trial, including root cause analysis and corrective and preventive action for significant non-compliance, and the framework applies it within the site. The Corrective and Preventive Action Log (CAPA) records each action and its cause, with the effectiveness review and closure (Appendix W).

4.6 The quality lifecycle

Current guidance asks that quality be planned before a study begins and managed throughout its lifecycle [3, 4]. We applied this principle to the site's work by assigning each instrument to the phase in which it can have most effect, as DR9 requires. The timing is especially important in a surgical investigation. Once an implant is fixed, an error made during surgery cannot be corrected as a transcription error can, so the controls on the surgical pathway must be in place before the first procedure. Similarly, the learning curve log must begin with the surgeon's first case. A log started later may capture stable performance while missing the period of learning that the site needed to observe. Data generated at the point of care, such as the identifiers of an implanted component, must also be recorded when they arise to prevent the loss addressed by DR6.

The lifecycle sets out when each instrument is prepared and when it is used. It complements the milestone reviews, which identify points for decisions, and the review schedules, which specify how often each control runs. The site's work is organized into seven phases, each covering the CtQ domains relevant to that phase, with a safety stream across the phases of conduct.

4.6.1 Phases, gates, and the safety stream

Preparation includes feasibility and start-up, conduct includes enrollment, surgery, and follow-up, and closure includes close-out and continuous improvement (Figure 4.1). During feasibility, the site assesses whether it can conduct the investigation. This assessment informs the sponsor's selection and covers CtQ domain 1. Start-up establishes the four pillars before participant data are collected, including training and credentialing under domain 6 and arrangements for receiving and storing the device. Enrollment covers participant selection and consent, domains 2 and 3, while surgery covers device management and procedural delivery, domains 4 and 5. Follow-up covers domains 7, 8, and 9, from retention to outcome assessment and imaging. Data capture and deviation management, domains 11 and 12, continue through every phase.

Preparation and closure proceed at site level, with the PI confirming each phase's exit condition at a milestone review before the next phase begins. During conduct, participants progress at different times: one may be in screening while another is already in follow-up. Each conduct phase therefore opens for the site with its first case and closes with its last. At the end of start-up, the site confirms its readiness to enroll and records the implementation level it will use (Section 4.7).

Safety surveillance covers the period from the first enrolled participant to the last follow-up assessment and protects CtQ domain 10. It operates within the applicable external reporting intervals and connects events across phases, since a complication observed during follow-up may arise from a decision made during surgery. The site reviews safety cases at least once a month and keeps the record of each implanted unit current throughout the participant's follow-up.

At closure, the site can assess what the investigation has taught it about its own conduct. Corrective and preventive action has already been running since the first case, and preventive action can correct a weakness in other ongoing investigations as soon as it is identified. The closing phase brings these findings and the risk register together to revise the site's standing procedures. Recording the changes allows later teams to use the lessons even when the people involved have left, as DR10 requires. The Post-Investigation Debrief and Lessons Record (PIDLR) documents the debrief and its lessons (Appendix X), and the revised procedures provide the starting point for the next investigation.

4.6.2 Where each instrument acts

Fifteen of the twenty-four instruments act during start-up (Figure 4.2). Although this phase produces almost no participant data, it establishes the conditions under which the subsequent data will be collected. Training and credentialing decisions are completed, the risk register is opened, and imaging readiness is assessed. The site also fixes the rules for most other instruments, including criticality grades and indicator thresholds, before enrolling the first participant. Participant records open in the phase where their data arise, while indicators and complication reviews become more informative as cases accumulate. Milestone reviews extend across every phase, and the debrief is used only in the final phase, continuous improvement.

[Figure 4.2. The twenty-four instruments placed in the phases of the lifecycle, with an open marker where an instrument is prepared and its rules are fixed and a filled marker where it is in use.]

Within start-up, preparation follows the dependencies between instruments. Training requirements and criticality grades must be defined before anyone is released for a task, and release on device training must precede the credentialing decision that uses it. The site also sets the plan for internal verification before the first review.

The framework specifies four timing requirements that the instruments compared in Chapter 3 do not impose. For Gap 2, the risk register opens before the first participant, while the site can still assess local risks before exposure occurs. For Gap 3, the learning curve log has a defined basis of comparison before the first case and begins with each surgeon's first procedure, including surgeons who join during conduct. Internal verification has its scope and schedule set before conduct, either in its plan or on its checklist, so the site can review its work independently of sponsor visits (Gap 4). Finally, the rule for each indicator in use is fixed before the first participant, allowing a signal to initiate corrective action before an external finding does (Gap 5).

The evidence provides some support for attending to particular phases. Surgical complications may cluster early in a surgeon's learning curve [22], and loss to follow-up accumulates over later years [24]. These findings do not establish that sites using controls organized by phase perform better than sites responding to sponsor monitoring. The phase structure therefore remains a design hypothesis, consistent with the direction in ICH E8(R1) to focus quality resources on critical activities and risks. Effective controls during conduct might also reduce the corrections needed at close-out, but this thesis has not measured that benefit.

4.7 The scalability model

We designed the framework for a nonhospital organization, whose conditions define the minimum implementation level. However, hospitals hosted 239 of the 347 site records in the registry sample (68.9%), so the framework also needs to accommodate the settings in which most of these investigations take place. The scalability model answers DR12 by adapting implementation to the center's resources and the risk of the investigation while maintaining a complete and consistent documentation trail. Proportionality to risk follows ICH E6(R3), while adapting to resources is a choice of this design, acceptable only while the required evidence remains available at every level.

The number of instruments in use varies between levels, while each instrument retains the same form. The three levels follow the organizational environments in Table 3.3 and the support the framework can reasonably assume in each. Where the site does not use a separate instrument, an existing record takes on its control function. Table 4.2 identifies this proxy for each instrument omitted from a minimum set. The recommended minimum is twelve instruments for a nonhospital organization, eighteen for a nonacademic hospital, and all twenty-four for an academic organization. A site with capacity to use more should do so, and investigation-specific risks may require additions to its minimum set.

At the minimum level, the clinical staff also perform the research work, without the support of a hospital system or university. The twelve instruments cover the site's existing duties and provide at least one response to each gap. The POT supports oversight, the RACI records assignments, and the TRE and SCR document training release and surgeon authorization. The site opens its risk register before the first participant and follows each safety case in the SART. It also retains the eligibility decision, the intraoperative record, and the log of each image set. Internal verification uses the IMC, follow-up is reviewed in the FUCR, and the PIDLR records lessons at closure.

In these small teams, one person may hold several of the six functions, but each function has a named substitute so that no CtQ function depends on one individual. Where the separation required by DR10 cannot be fully achieved, the operational constraint in Section 3.5 takes precedence. Even so, the person reviewing work must be different from the person who performed it, and reviews must meet the minimum frequency set by the pillars, including the monthly review of safety cases.

A nonacademic hospital provides services such as radiology and controlled storage, with more people available to share review duties. Its recommended minimum rises to eighteen instruments. The six additions support work that requires time for analysis and planning: the learning curve log, the complication log and its separate signal review, the internal monitoring plan, the performance indicator record, and the corrective action log. With these additions, each gap in Table 3.1 has the full response specified by the framework.

In an academic organization, the hospital also has university support and may already have a research unit and an institutional quality system. All twenty-four instruments are recommended at this level. The six additions give larger teams a more formal structure through the graded delegation matrix, milestone review log, training requirements matrix, and records of imaging readiness, screening, and visits. Where the institution already keeps training records or a corrective action system, the instruments refer to it, as DR11 requires. They add the information needed to follow the named surgeon, device, and investigation where the institutional system does not already provide it. The services available from clinical trial units nevertheless vary [89, 90].

[Table 4.2. The scalability model, with the recommended minimum set of instruments at each of the three implementation levels, which follow the organizational environments of Table 3.3, and the proxy for each instrument left out.]

Organizational category alone does not establish research capacity. In one survey, stakeholders from higher-level hospitals performed better on average, but substantial room for improvement remained [88]. Hospitals and academic centers also report shortages of staff and infrastructure (Section 3.4.4). The levels therefore provide an initial assumption about support that the site must check against its actual conditions. A site lacking the support assumed for its category uses the set of the level below, while a nonhospital organization with its own research capacity may use a higher level. The PI records the choice at the review that closes start-up and revisits it when capacity changes, for example after the departure of the coordinator. Repeated institutional self-assessment has been associated with improved capability to conduct research, although that association was not shown to be causal [30].

Within each level, the site adds instruments for domains rated as high risk in the risk register. Strong operator dependence, such as that associated with a new implant design or technique, calls for the learning curve log. Limited classification reliability calls for the imaging and training controls of the higher levels, while prolonged follow-up calls for closer review of retention. A nonhospital site investigating a new implant design would therefore add the learning curve log to its twelve instruments. If the required controls exceed its capacity, the site must obtain support from the sponsor, such as proctoring, or decline the investigation at feasibility.

Every implementation level must retain the evidence needed to show that the investigation was conducted as required. The MDR requires investigation information to be recorded, handled, and stored so that it can be accurately reported, interpreted, and verified, and requires Member States to inspect investigation sites at an appropriate level. Records must therefore remain complete, consistent, current, and retrievable even at the minimum level. The records required by ISO 14155:2026 and the MDR remain necessary across implementation levels and degrees of risk. They include consent and source documents, delegation and training, device accountability, deviations, and adverse events. The framework specifies where these records are kept, and the instruments in use retain their operating rules: the risk register covers all twelve domains, and the intraoperative record is completed during the procedure.

4.8 The operational instrument set

The regulatory texts examined do not require the site to operate a quality system (Gap 1). Section 3.5 therefore established the need to justify each part of the framework through its origin, and Sections 4.2 to 4.5 have done so for the individual instruments (Table 4.3). Adoption also requires a reason to use the instruments together. A site already keeps many records, yet a recurring problem can remain unnoticed within them. For example, an unplanned component substitution may appear in both the operative note and the device accountability record and recur in later cases before anyone reviews the records together. The framework must therefore connect the records to decisions, with a control for each domain and a stated reason for including each instrument.

The instruments work at three levels (Figure 4.3). At the event or person level, the ICDL records one procedure and the TRE records one person's training and release. At the next level, the site reviews several records together, using instruments such as the learning curve log for a surgeon or the follow-up review for a period. These reviews inform decisions about site conduct, documented at the third level in instruments such as the delegation matrix and risk register. A resulting decision then sets a condition against which subsequent work is checked.

The component substitution illustrates how these levels connect in a site using the full set. The event is recorded in the ICDL during surgery, then reviewed alongside the surgeon's other cases in the learning curve log and reported in the procedural consistency indicator for that period. If the review confirms a pattern, the PI records a decision in the POT. That decision may change the surgeon's authorization to require proctoring, which is documented in the credentialing record and checked in the ICDL for subsequent procedures. The sequence begins under Operational Controls, is reviewed under Risk Management and Performance Oversight, and leads to a decision under Governance. We designed the instruments after defining the functions of all four pillars so that these exchanges could be built into the set.

Five instruments require recording at the clinical encounter: the PSEAR, ICDL, IQL, PFVR, and SART. They cover the points where Chapter 3 identified data that cannot be recovered once lost. The other nineteen use existing records, either these five or the site's clinical records, which follows DR11 and the rule on source documentation. At a lower implementation level, the oversight meeting documented in the POT takes on reviews that a dedicated instrument would otherwise support, such as the learning curve review or the indicators in the PIR. Four of the five clinical records remain as instruments at every level, while an existing record serves as the proxy for the visit record, so the underlying evidence remains when the site uses fewer dedicated instruments for synthesis. The time required for the periodic work of the other nineteen instruments has not been measured in a small team.

Coverage has to be checked against both the CtQ domains and the design requirements (Figure 4.4). Every domain has at least one instrument that carries its control. Some of these instruments do not answer a design requirement on their own, but are needed to perform the underlying work, as the training and credentialing records do for domain 6 and the visit record does for follow-up. Checking the set only against the requirements would miss these control records.

The requirements identify a further need to assess whether those controls work. The PIR and FUCR meet DR8 by providing indicators of drift, using information recorded by other instruments. A check based only on domain coverage would miss this measurement function. Across the full set, each requirement that can be met by an individual instrument has a direct response, and each instrument either carries a domain's control or directly answers a requirement. DR9, DR11, and DR12 concern the set as a whole. DR9 is met by assigning instruments to phases (Figure 4.2), while DR11 and DR12 concern use within the site's documentary system and the retention of required records at each implementation level (Table 4.2).

Domains 5, 6, 8, and 9 depend wholly or partly on requirements that the regulatory texts examined do not assign to the site. Their controls are provided through site instruments, including the credentialing record, learning curve log, ICDL, and imaging records. Most of these instruments directly meet requirements derived from the four domains in Section 3.5 and provide controls beyond the duties assigned to the site in those texts. In the other domains, the framework mainly organizes existing duties, such as delegation and adverse event reporting. Additional controls in these domains also follow a requirement derived in Chapter 3, as the review of complication patterns follows DR8.

Within this mapping, consent is covered by the eligibility record because the regulations already specify the consent process and Chapter 3 identified no additional design requirement beyond the risk register. Endpoint assessment has more limited direct coverage. The visit record documents the assessment and is reviewed through the follow-up instrument, while assessor qualification and consistency are covered in the training records. When endpoints depend on readings that vary between observers, the site therefore adds the DTRM according to the risk register, as described in Section 4.7.

The design establishes how records are intended to connect and why each instrument is included. Whether a site reviews those records in time to act depends on the people doing the work and the time available to them, which the mapping cannot establish.

Draft templates for all twenty-four instruments are provided in Appendices A to X. We developed them for shoulder device investigations, with that context reflected mainly in complication categories, imaging requirements, and the case complexity levels used in the learning curve log. We kept their structure, including fields and escalation rules, as general as the context allowed. Adapting them to another surgical specialty would therefore be expected to involve mainly changes to classification lists, although no such adaptation has been tested. The open, editable format allows a site to match the templates to its own documents and adjust fields while retaining the control function of each instrument.
