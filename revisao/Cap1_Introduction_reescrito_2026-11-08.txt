# 1. Introduction
## 1.1 Historical Overview and Recent Updates
Over the past decade, quality management in clinical research has changed^{1}. In the older model, sites and sponsors found most problems during monitoring visits and audits and then corrected them case by case. These checks ran throughout the trial, but their purpose was detection and verification, so the quality of a trial depended largely on what the checks found after the work had been done.
Current guidance asks that quality be planned before a study begins and managed across its whole lifecycle^{2,3}. The change can be followed through the guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH). ICH E6, the guideline for Good Clinical Practice (GCP) adopted in 1996, set out the responsibilities of sponsors, investigators and ethics committees through detailed procedural requirements. Its second revision, ICH E6(R2), was adopted in 2016. It added a quality management section for the sponsor and introduced risk-based monitoring (RBM), because the same level of checking cannot usefully be applied to every activity of a trial^{4}. The same revision made the investigator explicitly responsible for supervising any person or party to whom trial activities are delegated^{4}.
ICH E8(R1), adopted in 2021, moved the central question from how a study should be monitored to how it should be designed^{2}. With it, Quality by Design (QbD) entered the guidance for clinical research. Under QbD, the people who design a study work out in advance which of its attributes matter most for the protection of participants and for the reliability of the results. They then put effort and resources where the risks to those attributes are highest. ICH E8(R1) calls these attributes critical-to-quality (CtQ) factors. It defines them as attributes of a study “whose integrity is fundamental to the protection of study participants, the reliability and interpretability of the study results, and the decisions made based on the study results”^{2}. The eligibility criteria and the collection of endpoint and safety data are among the considerations the guideline lists for identifying them. Because the factors depend on the design and endpoints of each study, they have to be identified again for every protocol.
ICH E6(R3), adopted on 6 January 2025, continued in the same direction^{3}. QbD and proportionality now appear among the principles that govern both the design and the conduct of a trial, and quality management is expected to cover the whole trial lifecycle. For the investigator, the revision ties oversight to risk, since the level of oversight of delegated activities should depend on the importance of the data collected and on the risks to participant safety and data reliability^{3}. Trial-level quality management, including the identification of CtQ factors and the assessment of risks to them, remains a duty of the sponsor^{3}. Commentators have described the revision as a move from a prescriptive model toward a governance model^{5}. In the first, quality is shown by following set procedures. In the second, the responsible parties design a system proportionate to the risks of the trial and keep improving it.
The ICH guidelines were written mainly for trials of medicinal products, and their vocabulary reflects that origin. Clinical investigations of medical devices are governed by ISO 14155, the GCP standard of the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) for that field^{6}. The standard was first published in 1996, and its latest edition, ISO 14155:2026, appeared on 23 March 2026, after the research for this thesis had started^{6}. For the investigational site, the most relevant change of this edition concerns risk. The new edition separates the residual risks of the investigational device from the risks of clinical procedures that the clinical investigation plan (CIP) requires outside routine clinical practice^{6}. Device risks continue to follow ISO 14971, the standard that manufacturers apply to manage the risks of a medical device throughout its life^{47}, while the procedural risks are assessed as part of the investigation itself. Site staff usually carry out these additional procedures, such as extra imaging or functional assessments, so the new distinction brings part of the risk of an investigation closer to the work done at the site. The edition also adds provisions on the sponsor's side, such as a clinical events committee for the central review of events^{6}.
In the European Union, the legal requirements for device investigations come from Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices (MDR), which governs clinical investigations from their authorization to the reporting of their results^{8}. Under the MDR, a device that conforms to a harmonized standard whose reference is published in the Official Journal of the European Union is presumed to meet the requirements that the standard covers^{8}. The edition of ISO 14155 listed for this purpose is still EN ISO 14155:2020+A11:2024, according to Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/193^{7}. In Europe, the 2020 edition therefore carries the presumption of conformity, while the 2026 edition describes the current state of the art. In the United States, device investigations follow a separate framework under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act^{9} and Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations^{10}.
Adoption of these principles is still uneven, even among sponsors. In a global survey of 119 pharmaceutical and biotechnology sponsors on 32 risk-based quality management (RBQM) practices, the identification of CtQ factors was among the five least used practices^{11}. Sites were not part of the survey, so it says nothing directly about how sites apply CtQ principles. It does show that the first step of the QbD approach is still difficult for sponsors, who developed the concept and usually have more resources available for it than sites.
## 1.2 Surgical Device Investigations as a Distinct Methodological Context
Most of the methods and regulatory requirements of clinical research were developed for medicinal products, where the intervention is a compound given at a defined dose and schedule. Implantable devices, however, raise design and evaluation problems that this model does not cover^{12,13}. In a surgical investigation the intervention is a device placed by a surgeon during an operation, and the evidence about it is collected over years of follow-up. This setting has five conditions that change what quality management has to do at the site, because each of them affects how data are produced in the operating room or during follow-up.
A prosthesis fixed to bone cannot be stopped or withdrawn the way a medicinal product can, so an error during implantation may have lasting consequences for the participant^{12,14}. This irreversibility raises the cost of any quality failure at surgery, such as an incorrect implant size or a technical deviation that nobody recorded.
Outcomes may also depend substantially on the surgeon. Results vary between surgeons and, for one surgeon, change as experience grows along the learning curve^{15–19}.
Each implanted unit has to remain traceable to the participant who received it. Under the MDR, documentation and record-retention duties support the traceability of the individual investigational device, and ISO 14155:2026 sets requirements for device accountability^{6,8}. A joint replacement, such as a shoulder prosthesis, may include several components, each with its own size and identifier, and the record has to show which of them were implanted in each participant. For quality purposes it should also allow the surgical decision behind that configuration to be reconstructed.
Much of the evidence is produced after surgery. Range-of-motion (ROM) assessments and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) often require the participant to attend over several years, and radiographic evaluations add the same demand, with the same procedures applied at each visit. Orthopedic studies show that loss to follow-up is common enough to threaten interpretation when it is not defined and managed consistently^{20,21}.
Finally, many of the assessments behind eligibility and outcomes have limited reliability in themselves. Radiological and clinical classifications used in shoulder surgery show limited agreement between observers^{22,23}, and PROM scores depend on how participants interpret each item. These are limits of the measurement methods. Local execution adds a further source of variation, for example when the clinician who measures ROM changes between visits or when radiographs are taken with the patient in a different position. This added variation is the part that a site can control.
## 1.3 Quality Responsibility at the Investigational Site
Each of these conditions plays out at the investigational site, because the implant is placed in its operating room and the follow-up assessments that produce the endpoints are carried out by its staff. The guidance divides the responsibility for quality between two parties. The sponsor holds trial-level quality management, so it identifies the CtQ factors of the trial and assesses the risks to them^{3}. It then decides which controls prevent or detect errors and sets the limits beyond which a signal calls for action^{3}. The investigator is responsible for the conduct of the trial at the site, with qualified staff and adequate resources, and for overseeing delegated activities in proportion to their importance and risk^{3,6}.
Much of the work that protects or damages the CtQ factors of a trial happens inside the site, in steps that the protocol only partly specifies. The site decides, for example, how potentially eligible participants are found and checked against the criteria, and its staff deliver the intervention and record the outcome data and adverse events (AEs) on which the analysis depends. The sponsor sees most of this work afterward, through monitoring and data review, so the quality of these steps depends largely on how the site organizes its own processes.
The guidance does not require a site to run a quality system of its own. It asks the investigator to put adequate processes in place and to oversee delegated work, without describing how a site should turn these duties into daily practice. This thesis proposes that a site can apply to its own processes the reasoning that QbD asks of the sponsor, by identifying where its own conduct may fail and by controlling those points while the investigation runs. The proposal goes beyond what the texts require, so its usefulness has to rest on the problems it can address and on what existing instruments already offer.
## 1.4 Existing Quality Instruments and Their Users
Clinical research has built many quality instruments over the past two decades. To find out what already exists for the site, the literature reviewed for this thesis was searched for instruments that plan, measure or support quality in clinical trials. For each instrument the review noted its purpose and the party that applies it, together with the party it is applied to. Most of the instruments found were built by or for sponsors and are applied to the trial and its sites. A smaller group measures or supports the site, and a few are applied by sites to their own organization.
### 1.4.1 Instruments for trial planning and oversight
TransCelerate BioPharma, a nonprofit organization founded in 2012 by biopharmaceutical companies, produced a conceptual framework for a clinical quality management system together with a set of RBM frameworks^{28}. The Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative (CTTI), a public-private partnership founded in 2007 by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and Duke University, issued recommendations on QbD with tools to support them^{29,49}. These recommendations ask that CtQ factors be identified prospectively and reviewed periodically while the trial runs. They also call for clinical investigators and research site staff, together with trial participants, to take part in the discussion of design and feasibility^{29}. The site therefore has a voice in how the quality of a trial is planned. The tools that put the recommendations into practice are addressed to sponsors, however, and the recommendations do not describe how a site should organize the quality of its own work^{29}. The Medical Device Innovation Consortium (MDIC), founded in 2012, extended the CTTI approach to devices in a supplement on CtQ factors, which recognizes that device investigations can involve learning curves in product use^{30}.
For surgical trials, a further line of work deals with how the intervention itself is delivered. It is also written from the position of the trial team, but it reaches into execution during the trial as well as design. A systematic review of oncology trials grouped the reported challenges to surgical quality assurance (SQA) under credentialing and standardization, with monitoring as a third group, and found the implementation and reporting of effective measures inadequate^{31}. The Randomised Oesophagectomy: Minimally Invasive or Open (ROMIO) study then developed concrete methods, including entry criteria for surgeons based on the assessment of operative videos and the standardization of operative technique, with structured monitoring during the trial^{32}. A Delphi process with 71 stakeholders later produced 59 strategies to overcome the challenges to SQA in oncology trials^{33}. A Delphi checklist for gastrointestinal surgery trials added proctoring and performance monitoring during the trial^{34}. In all of these proposals the trial team specifies the measures and scores the results.
Studies of how these measures were applied in practice report weak implementation. In 2024, a multinational panel reviewed randomized trials of arteriovenous grafts for hemodialysis, a surgically implanted device. Quality assurance (QA) was highly variable and often poorly described, with no improvement across the three decades of trials examined. Few trials defined the experience a surgeon needed before taking part, and none had a monitoring program defined before the trial or assessed the technical performance of its surgeons^{35}. In surgical oncology, 2 of 12 randomized trials of quality improvement interventions showed an effect on the primary outcome^{36}.
These studies show that measures proposed for surgical trials were often not carried into conduct, and that the observation of surgeons during the trial was the step most often missing^{31,35}, but they do not show why. The reasons were not studied, and several could contribute, from the workload and funding of the trial to incomplete reporting in the publications themselves. One further explanation, which this thesis explores without claiming to have established it, concerns where the measures are held. The trial team defines SQA frameworks centrally so that one standard applies across sites, while the observation they require takes place in each operating room during conduct. A site with no structure of its own for recording and reviewing the work of its surgeons depends on the trial team to carry out that step.
### 1.4.2 Instruments that measure or support the site
Experts convened through a forum of the United States National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine developed a framework for clinical trial site readiness from the site qualification documents of 14 sponsors. It describes 40 practices in six domains, one of which is quality oversight^{25}. Its authors present it as a common set of expectations that can simplify site selection and that experienced sites can use to assess their current practices. The practices are generic and describe readiness before a trial, without the protocol-specific requirements of any single study^{25}.
The Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure measures the operational performance of individual trials. It was developed in six Italian research centers, including university and public hospitals. Of 126 candidate indicators, 18 were retained in four domains: participant retention and consent, data completeness and timeliness, AE reporting, and protocol compliance^{37}. The trial team at the site completes the measure as a self-assessment of each trial, and a four-item short form is proposed for routine monitoring. Its authors present it as a tool for benchmarking and resource allocation and for identifying underperforming sites across networks^{37}. A site can therefore use it to observe its own performance. The items are the same for every trial, however, and the measure was validated in a cross-sectional study. It therefore shows how a trial is performing without linking the result to the CtQ factors of that trial or to the controls that protect them.
Sponsors also measure sites through central monitoring. In an analysis of 1,111 sites in 159 trials, data quality improved at 83% of the sites flagged by central statistical monitoring, against 56% in two historical studies without it^{24}. In trials of implantable cardiac devices covering 133 sites and 3,442 participants, the sponsor scored each site on weighted risk factors, including follow-up compliance and missing critical data. It used the ranking to decide where to send on-site monitoring visits. After one year the worst-performing sites had improved in most of these factors, although the evaluation had no comparison group^{42}. In both cases the indicators described processes that the site runs, and the improvement followed a signal that the sponsor produced and sent to the site. A review run by the site itself would have to complement this kind of sponsor review and show that it adds information or allows earlier action.
A few instruments are applied by sites to their own organization. The Clinical Trial Assessment of Infrastructure Matrix (CTAIM) was developed in a community cancer centers program of the United States National Cancer Institute. It supports an annual self-assessment of a research program against 11 infrastructure attributes, each with three progressive levels^{26}. Clinical trial centers in Italy have implemented ISO 9001:2015, the general standard for quality management systems, and reported organizational workload and insufficient training as the main barriers to certification^{27}. Academic institutions add research management systems^{86} and clinical trial units whose senior staff rank quality management among their essential services^{87}. Guidance written for sites describes what a new private site has to assemble before its first trial, from standard operating procedures to trained staff^{77}, and how sites can adapt their practice to ICH E6(R3)^{91}. These examples show that local quality management is already an established area of practice. The instruments reviewed organize the research capacity of a site as a whole, but none of them is built around the CtQ factors of a specific protocol, and none deals with the conditions of surgical device research.
Studies of site practice describe what happens where no such structure exists. Several report site-level quality management as reactive and ad hoc and connect it to persistent problems with data quality^{24,38,39}. In one mature research network, only about half of the participating sites used a standardized method for identifying AEs^{40}. In a survey of 46 Portuguese clinical trial sites, 9 (19.6%) described a standard tool or document that captured risk analysis at site level^{78}. Sites value RBM but often experience it as an external requirement over which they have little control^{41}. These findings support attention to local processes, but they do not show a uniform deficit across research organizations, and what a site needs depends on what it already runs.
### 1.4.3 What remains to be integrated
A site can already take part in planning the quality of a trial with the sponsor^{29}, and it can assess its own infrastructure and readiness with tools built for that purpose^{25,26}, as well as score the performance of each trial^{37}. It can also certify a general quality management system^{27}. None of the instruments reviewed, however, links the CtQ factors of a specific investigation to the risks that the site's own conduct creates. Nor do they connect those risks to the controls and indicators that the site reviews while the investigation runs, with the resulting corrections kept in records the site holds. Surgical device investigations add two needs that no instrument in the material reviewed assigns to the site. One is following the learning of each surgeon with the device during conduct. The other is capturing, as they arise, the intraoperative data that later records cannot reconstruct, such as the identifiers of the implanted components and the reasons for an unplanned substitution.
This situation is consistent with the way the texts and the tools distribute responsibility. Trial-level quality management is assigned to the sponsor^{3,6}, and most of the instruments that put it into practice were built for the sponsor. As a result, the quality of the work done at the site is organized mainly by controls that reach it from outside. A site that holds no structure of its own is likely to respond to deviations and findings one case at a time, which agrees with the reactive practice described in the studies above^{38,39}. Those studies do not test this explanation, but it defines the function that a site-level framework would have to perform.
## 1.5 Aim, Objectives, and Scope
This thesis aims to develop an operational quality management framework for investigational sites that conduct clinical investigations of shoulder medical devices. The framework applies the QbD and risk-proportionate oversight principles of ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) to the processes that the site itself runs. It is meant to sit alongside the obligations set by the MDR and ISO 14155:2026. It complements the quality system of the sponsor by giving the site a way to connect the CtQ factors of each investigation to the risks and controls of its own processes, with the corrections recorded in documents the site holds. To reach this aim, the work pursued four objectives:
- To compare ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and MDR Annex XV clause by clause, identifying where the instruments agree, where they differ, and where none creates a structured obligation for the investigational site.
- To identify the site-level CtQ domains that organize the areas of local conduct affecting the critical factors of each study, as distinct from the trial-level CtQ factors defined by the sponsor.
- To derive an explicit and traceable set of design requirements for a site-level framework from the regulatory analysis, from the gaps reported in the literature and from the operating conditions of surgical device investigations.
- To develop the framework and its operational instruments, showing how each component answers a stated requirement and how it relates to existing approaches.
The regulatory analysis uses the European framework, in which ISO 14155 is the GCP standard harmonized under the MDR, so that the device standard and the legal requirements could be read together. The corresponding requirements of the United States, set out in Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations, were not analyzed in the same detail. ICH E6(R3) and ISO 14155 apply internationally, however, and the framework is built around processes common to investigational sites, so it is intended for use beyond Europe once its regulatory references are mapped to local requirements.
The work focuses on orthopedic investigations and, within them, on the shoulder. A framework is usable only when it reaches operational detail, and that level of detail cannot be achieved across several surgical disciplines at once, so a single field had to be chosen. Orthopedics was chosen for two reasons. The MDR classifies total and partial joint replacements as class III devices and, for implantable and class III devices, generally requires clinical investigations before the device reaches the market^{8}, so new joint replacements depend on investigations conducted at surgical sites. The co-supervisor of this work, Dr. Nuno Moura, works in orthopedics, which allowed specific feedback on the operational detail of the framework and its instruments. Within orthopedics, the shoulder shows the five conditions described in this chapter in a well-documented form, and most of them are shared with other surgical device investigations. Learning periods of about 12 to 86 cases have been reported for shoulder arthroplasty^{17,79}, and one shoulder arthroplasty cohort lost 34.3% of its participants to follow-up by the seventh year^{21}. Measurement is also a known problem in this field, because the classifications used for eligibility show limited agreement between observers^{22,23} and registered shoulder arthroplasty trials vary so widely in their outcome domains and instruments that core outcome sets were developed for shoulder disorders^{57,58}.
This is a design study. Its result is an artifact built from documented evidence and from requirements derived in the work itself. The framework has not yet been implemented or evaluated at a site, so any claim about its effects remains an expectation.
