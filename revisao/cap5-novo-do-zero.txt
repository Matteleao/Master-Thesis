5. Discussion

5.1 What the thesis set out to do and what it delivered

Section 1.5 asked what structure a site can use to carry its part of the responsibility for quality during conduct, and it set four objectives to answer that question. The first was a clause-by-clause comparison of ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and MDR Annex XV, and Chapter 3 reports it as five site-level gaps (Table 3.1). The second was to identify the CtQ domains that apply at site level, and Section 3.3 derived twelve of them, kept apart from the trial-level CtQ factors that the sponsor defines. The third objective, a traceable set of requirements, produced DR1 to DR12 (Table 3.4). The fourth asked for the framework itself and for a link between each component and a stated requirement. Chapter 4 delivers four pillars with twenty-four instruments, a lifecycle, and a scalability model, and no domain is left without a control and no instrument without an origin (Figure 4.4).

All four objectives were met at the level of design. None was met at the level of use, because no site has yet operated the framework, and Section 5.6 returns to that limit. The distinction matters for how the rest of this chapter should be read. What the thesis shows is that a site-level framework can be derived from the texts that govern device investigations, and that each of its parts answers a gap, a domain, or an operating condition. What it was designed to enable is a site that holds the record of its own control. What it only expects, and has not measured, is that such a site detects problems earlier and loses less information than one without the framework.

The design-science literature offers a way to state what kind of contribution this is. Gregor and Hevner classify a design contribution by how mature the problem is and how mature the solution is, and they call the application of a known solution to a new problem an exaptation [ref: Gregor and Hevner 2013]. Most of the framework fits that description. A documented quality system is a known solution, which the manufacturer runs under ISO 13485:2016 and ISO 14971:2019 and the medical laboratory runs under ISO 15189:2022 [52, 53, 54]. The problem is new, since the revised guidelines give the site a larger share of the responsibility for quality without giving it a structure to carry that share. The four pillars therefore transpose a solution that already works for two other organizations in the evidence chain, and most of what they contain organizes duties that ICH E6(R3) and ISO 14155:2026 already assign to the investigator.

The monitoring of the learning curve during conduct is the part that does not fit this pattern. ISO 14155:2026 lets the sponsor plan a roll-in phase in the CIP [8], and the surgical trials literature controls the operator at entry [35, 36, 37, 38]. None of the texts we compared gives the site a role in observing a surgeon's proficiency once the investigation has started (Gap 3). The SLCT, the surgeon procedural consistency indicator, and the link from both to the credentialing decision answer that gap. In the same classification this is an improvement, a new solution to a problem the field already knows. It is also the component with the weakest derivation, because no clause asks for it and its justification rests on the literature reviewed in Section 3.4.2.

5.2 Position in the literature

Four bodies of work deal with quality in clinical investigations, and each one places the site in a different position. Table 5.1 names the main works in each group, what they produce, and who operates them.

[Table 5.1. Prior work on quality in clinical investigations, with what each body of work produces, the party that operates it, and how the framework of this thesis differs.]

| Body of work | Main works | What it produces | Who operates it | Difference from this framework |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sponsor-level quality infrastructure | TransCelerate clinical quality management system [32, 33]; CTTI quality by design recommendations and MDIC device supplement [34]; EMA reflection paper [1]; risk-based monitoring [91] | Methods to identify CtQ factors and to plan risk-based oversight of a trial | Sponsor | The site receives controls designed elsewhere and holds no layer of its own |
| Surgical quality assurance in trials | Review of challenges in oncology [35]; ROMIO methods [36]; Delphi strategies [37]; SQA checklist [38]; arteriovenous graft review [39] | Entry criteria and standardization of the surgical intervention | Trial team and sponsor, mostly at entry | Proficiency is checked before the trial; nobody follows each surgeon during conduct |
| Site performance and readiness measures | Site readiness framework [29]; CTAIM [30]; Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure [41] | Indicators and checklists to assess a site | Sponsor, network, or the site in self-assessment | The site is assessed for another party; the framework gives the site indicators to manage its own processes |
| Empirical studies of site practice | Portuguese site survey [77]; data quality and monitoring studies [42, 43, 44]; adverse event reporting [45]; perceptions of risk-based monitoring [46, 47]; ISO 9001 in Italian centers [31] | Evidence that site quality management is mostly reactive and partly undocumented | Not applicable | The framework is designed as an answer to the state these studies describe |

The sponsor-level infrastructure is mature and well documented, and it is written for the party that designs and oversees the trial. The MDIC supplement does list considerations that depend on the site, such as its referral base and device accountability [34], so the site appears in this literature, but only as a contributor to the design of a trial. None of these works treats the site as an organization that runs controls of its own. The framework of this thesis starts where that literature stops, at the processes of one site.

The surgical quality assurance literature is the closest in subject. Its methods, from the assessment of operative videos at entry [36] to the 13-item checklist for gastrointestinal surgery [38], control the operator before the trial starts. A survey of 44 United Kingdom Clinical Trials Units found that 89% managed learning by setting a minimum level of expertise [71]. A review of arteriovenous graft trials found that no included trial had a defined pre-trial monitoring program and none assessed technical performance [39]. The framework takes over the concern of this literature and moves the observation of the operator into the conduct phase, at the site.

The site performance literature offers the most direct comparison, because the Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure reduced 126 candidate indicators to 18, organized in four validated domains [41]. All four domains have a counterpart among the six indicators of Section 4.5. Enrollment quality and follow-up compliance correspond to the first domain, critical data completeness to the second, safety reporting timeliness to the third, and the protocol deviation rate to the fourth. Surgeon procedural consistency has no counterpart, since a measure built for sites in general has no reason to contain it. The comparison also runs against this work. The four domains survived a reduction and a validation study, while our six indicators rest on the domain analysis and on argument, so a reader who asks which set is better evidenced should prefer the validated one. The two sets also serve different users, because the validated measure assesses a site for another party, and the framework's indicators tell the site whether one of its own processes has started to drift.

Two works come closer to a quality system run by the site. The CTAIM gives research programs a matrix to assess their own infrastructure [30], and an exploratory study implemented ISO 9001:2015 in Italian clinical trial centers [31]. Both show that sites can adopt a quality structure of their own. Neither is built around the CtQ domains of a surgical device investigation, and neither links the record of each procedure to the decisions about the people who perform it.

The empirical studies describe the situation the framework responds to. Site quality management is reported as reactive and ad hoc [42, 43, 44], and about half of the sites in one research network used a standard method to identify adverse events [45]. Sites also tend to experience risk-based monitoring as a requirement that reaches them through the sponsor [46, 47]. In the Portuguese survey, only 19.6% of the sites described a standard tool that captured risk analysis at site level [77]. Diani and colleagues reported that a risk-based monitoring approach improved data quality in trials of implantable cardiac devices [48]. In that study the sponsor ran the approach and the sites were the units it ranked, so it supports the plausibility of risk-based control for implants without giving the site a role in it. Cardiac rhythm devices also produce automated data and depend far less on operator technique than a shoulder arthroplasty does.

Our search returned no published direct comparison of ICH E6(R3) with ISO 14155. Because the review was narrative, this describes what we found, and a systematic search could still find such a comparison.

5.3 How the framework is expected to work

The framework rests on a chain that Section 4.8 described. A record made at the clinical moment is read together with other records, the synthesis supports a decision, and the decision returns as the condition that the next record is checked against. The value of the framework depends on that chain closing in time, and the design can only make it possible. The expected effects are therefore propositions, each with the measurement that would test it.

The first proposition is that the SLCT and the procedural consistency indicator detect procedural drift before it appears as a complication or a protocol deviation. Testing it requires the time between the first signal in the log and the first event in the safety or deviation records, compared between sites with and without the log. The second is that a site that runs the risk register and internal verification during conduct reaches close-out with fewer unresolved items, which can be measured as open queries at database lock and staff hours spent on close-out. The third is that monitors spend less time reconstructing site conduct when the site hands over its own records, which requires measuring monitoring time with and without those records. None of these effects has been measured, and none of the six indicators has been validated against the outcome it is meant to anticipate.

Three of the indicators read a process while it runs: enrollment quality, critical data completeness, and surgeon procedural consistency. The other three count events that have already happened, although each can still give an early signal of a deterioration that has not yet reached the participant or the endpoint. DR8 asks for indicators that expose drift, and the set meets it only in part, so the indicators should be read as the site's first warning system, whose predictive value is still unknown.

A record does not improve conduct by existing. The narrower claim we can defend is that controls written for a specific activity leave fewer failures unnoticed, so the framework acts on the probability that a failure goes undetected. Failures can come from lapses, competing demands on the same person, systems that do not fit the work, or unclear requirements, and the framework addresses those that nobody noticed in time. It does not address deliberate misconduct, because a person who intends to misrepresent conduct can misrepresent the record as well. Even if the framework changed nothing in conduct, the site would still hold a complete and traceable record of what it did, which improves reconstruction and auditability. Whether that record protects a participant depends on someone reviewing it and acting on it in time.

Observation may also change behavior. Staff who know that their work is reviewed may attend more closely to it, and the review teaches which elements matter. The same effect can push people to improve the record and leave the conduct as it was. Two features of the design reduce that risk. The instruments record decisions, identifiers, and dates, so a device identifier is either captured or missing, and most instruments are read by someone other than the person who completed them.

The effect of the framework also depends on how a site starts it. The lifecycle sets the order (Section 4.6). Governance and the risk register are prepared in start-up, the rules of internal verification and of each indicator are fixed before the first participant, and the participant records open in the phase where their data arise. The scalability model sets how much the site runs (Section 4.7). A nonhospital organization starts with twelve instruments, and the risk register adds others where a domain is rated high. An implementation should therefore begin with the minimum set of the site's level and grow from the risk register, and a first study should test that sequence.

5.4 Scaling and transfer

The framework was designed for the nonhospital organization, where it can assume the least support, and it scales by the number of instruments in use, with each instrument keeping the same form. This choice rests on one premise that we could not source. Inspection assesses compliance through the completeness and consistency of the records, and the minimum sets keep every record that ISO 14155:2026 and the MDR require, so a site at the minimum level should carry no additional regulatory exposure. We found no study that compares inspection outcomes across quality systems of different scope, and a reader who rejects the premise should also reject the scalability argument.

The minimum level has a cost of its own. The oversight meeting takes on syntheses that dedicated instruments would otherwise carry, such as the learning curve review or the indicators, so a signal may be noticed later than at a higher level. One person may hold several functions, and although each function has a named substitute, the separation between execution and oversight that DR10 asks for is weaker. The framework accepts this because Section 3.5 made the operational constraint binding, and because a control the site cannot run protects nobody.

ICH E6(R3) asks for proportionate quality management and warns against unnecessary complexity and data collection [6], which raises an objection to any framework that adds records. The framework answers it in three ways that Chapter 4 already contains. A site that keeps a record performing a function uses that record, a new record is justified only where a critical process would otherwise not be controlled or evidenced, and the minimum sets limit what a smaller site runs. Whether the twelve instruments of the minimum set are still too many for a team of one investigator and one coordinator is a question the design cannot settle, because nobody has measured the periodic work they require.

The framework was built for the shoulder, and Section 4.8 separated what is specific to that context from what is general. The complication categories, the imaging requirements, and the complexity levels of the learning curve log carry anatomical content and would need replacement for another specialty. The fields, review triggers, and escalation logic carry none. Neither the MDR nor ISO 14155:2026 distinguishes a shoulder device from a knee or hip device, so the obligations behind the instruments apply across implant surgery, which makes transfer plausible without showing it. The claim holds for implant-based surgical investigations, where the operator delivers the intervention, each device is traceable as a unit, and outcomes depend on imaging and functional measurement. It does not hold for device investigations without surgery, where operator dependence takes another form, or for surgical investigations without an implant.

5.5 Implications

5.5.1 For the investigational site

Conducting an investigation well and showing that the site controlled its conduct are separate achievements. ICH E6(R3) expects the investigator to oversee delegated activities in proportion to their importance, and ISO 14155:2026 gives the principal investigator duties that run for the whole investigation [8]. Neither text says what the investigator should produce to show that these duties were met. A careful site can therefore conduct an investigation well and still have little to show a governance review. The framework gives the site that account, and its value is clearest when the people change. A coordinator may leave, a monitor may be replaced, or a question a previous monitor had settled may come back, and a site with a documented framework carries its history through each of these events.

The account has a price in staff time. Research compensation in this field is calculated per procedure and per participant who completes the study, and sponsors negotiate it against Fair Market Value schedules [ref needed], so no line of the budget funds an internal quality system. In the United States, payment above Fair Market Value also creates compliance exposure under anti-inducement rules [ref needed]. We did not examine the equivalent rules in the European Union. An avoidable error therefore becomes a direct loss for the site, and whether a control that prevents such an error returns more than it costs is a hypothesis this thesis did not test. Testing it requires the staff time per instrument on one side and the cost of the events avoided on the other.

5.5.2 For sponsors, contract research organizations, and monitors

The framework meets the sponsor's system at four points. At site qualification, ISO 14155:2026 Clause 6.8 asks the sponsor to judge the qualifications of the principal investigator and the adequacy of the site. A site that arrives with its own risk register and oversight template gives documentary evidence on both. At a monitoring visit, a site that hands over its risk register, delegation matrix, corrective action log, and indicator results lets the monitor review a site that has already described its own risks. We found no study that measures how much monitoring time this saves [91]. When a monitoring finding needs correction, a site with a working corrective and preventive action process can show that it removed the cause as well as the instance. The fourth point concerns signals. The sponsor's central monitoring uses key risk indicators and quality tolerance limits at trial level, and the site's indicators read the same processes at one center. A sponsor could therefore receive the site's indicator results as an input to central monitoring, provided both parties agree that the source of each datum stays where Section 4.4.4 placed it.

These interfaces carry a disincentive. A site that records a weakness creates a record that a monitor can read, and a finding the site raised against itself is still a finding. The framework asks the site to accept that exposure, because the alternative leaves the same weakness in place and unrecorded. How monitors treat findings that a site declared itself is not known, and it will decide whether sites are willing to keep these records.

The framework does not require the site to build everything alone. A sponsor already supplies the protocol, the Investigator's Brochure, and training, and it already runs a clinical quality management system under Clause 9.1 of ISO 14155:2026. It could also supply the templates and help set up the framework across its sites. What should not move is the operation of the framework and the ownership of its records, which stay with the party present when the data are generated. One sponsor could pilot this arrangement in several of its sites within one investigation.

5.5.3 For standards and regulators

Reading ISO 14155:2026 from the position of the site shows some points that a future revision could consider. Clause 6.2 requires the sponsor to manage risk in three streams: the use of the device, the clinical procedures required by the CIP beyond routine practice, and the conduct of the investigation. All three belong to the sponsor, and no text describes how a site handles the part of those risks that its own execution creates. Clause 9.2.4.5 p) asks monitors to verify that equipment relevant to assessment is maintained and calibrated, and Annex A.6.4 adds calibration to the content of the CIP, but no clause describes the record through which the site shows this work. Clause 6.12 introduces the clinical events committee and creates no local counterpart. These observations come from one position, and the standard has a wider purpose, so a revision might reasonably leave them open.

The observations also differ in size. A clause asking a site to keep a risk register would ask for one document and one review cycle, while a requirement for a site-level quality system would ask for much more. The version with a presumption of conformity under the MDR is still EN ISO 14155:2020+A11:2024, while the 2026 edition represents the current state of the art. A site in the European Union works to one text while the field moves to another. Annex XV of the MDR governs the conduct of clinical investigations and says nothing about how a site organizes itself to conduct them, and the harmonization of the fourth edition would be a natural moment to examine that question. The laboratory offers a precedent, since ISO 15189:2022 gives the medical laboratory a quality system that can be accredited [54]. Whether investigational sites should one day have an equivalent is beyond what this thesis can answer, and we raise it as a question for the field.

Within the present texts, no clause requires a site to keep the records this framework produces, so a competent authority that inspects a site under the MDR [10] would not find their absence to be a non-compliance. Their value at inspection is different. They let the site show how it controlled its conduct, which is what a governance-oriented inspection looks for and what a site without them can only reconstruct from primary documents.

5.5.4 Ethical implications

Participants in a device investigation accept risk in the service of knowledge, and the quality of conduct at the site decides how well that acceptance is honored. A delayed safety report postpones the identification of a device-related signal at every center. An incomplete traceability record can prevent a complication from being linked to the lot that produced it, and an eligibility error exposes a participant to a procedure that cannot be undone. The participant carries these risks and sees none of the quality system. The sponsor judges the site at qualification and the ethics committee at approval, and the framework supports the third judgment, the one the site makes in every case.

The framework also observes the people who do the work. The SLCT and the procedural consistency indicator hold performance by surgeon and can lead to a restriction of the cases a surgeon takes, so they are in substance an assessment of professional performance and have to be governed as such. The design codes the surgeon, keeps the key with the principal investigator, fixes before the first case the outcomes that a review can reach, and requires the reasoning behind any restriction to be documented. These records are also personal data of the professional, and under the European data protection rules the site has to define who can access them and for what purpose. We did not analyze those rules in detail.

The weakest point arises when the site belongs to the surgeon it observes. In a surgeon-owned practice, the principal investigator who decides on a restriction may be the surgeon being assessed, or a close colleague who shares the commercial consequence. The role structure of Section 4.2.2 already gives every function a named substitute. The framework can therefore require that, when the principal investigator is the surgeon under review, the decision passes to that substitute or to a party outside the site, such as the sponsor's medical monitor or the ethics committee. This route has to be fixed before the first case.

5.6 Evaluation, limitations, and further research

5.6.1 The evaluation this thesis performed

The Design Science Research Methodology has six activities, and this thesis reports four of them, problem identification, the objectives of a solution, design and development, and communication [49]. Demonstration and evaluation are not reported. The thesis did perform one kind of evaluation. The design-science literature distinguishes evaluation before an artifact is used, which validates design decisions against existing knowledge, from evaluation after it is used [ref: Sonnenberg and vom Brocke 2012]. It also classifies evaluations as formative or summative and as artificial or naturalistic [ref: Venable et al. 2016]. The traceability from each component to a requirement, and from each requirement to a clause, a domain, or an operating condition, is an evaluation of the first kind, formative and artificial. It shows that the framework is coherent with the knowledge it was built from. It cannot show that the framework works in a site, which only a naturalistic and summative evaluation can show.

5.6.2 Limitations

The absence of any implementation weakens every claim about effect in this chapter. The propositions in Section 5.3 and the cost argument in Section 5.5.1 remain expectations, and the volume of work that the instruments require is unknown. A feasibility study in one site would reduce this limitation, and the design already reduces its consequence by keeping the regulatory analysis and the derivation independent of any expected effect.

The literature review was narrative. This weakens any statement that a body of work is absent from the field, so the position in Section 5.2 describes the works we found and read. A systematic search would test that position, and we reduced the risk by searching the bodies of work most likely to contain a site-level framework and by reporting the closest works we found.

The registry analysis classified organizations from their public self-description, and one researcher made each classification with a second internal pass and no independent assessor. This weakens the assignment of organizations to environments and the reproducibility of the counts in Table 3.3. A second coder would test it.

The registry is weighted toward the United States, with 155 of the 238 organizations and 63 of the 68 nonhospital organizations located there, while the framework was designed under the MDR and ISO 14155:2026. The regulatory analysis does not depend on the registry, which serves only to describe the organizational profile of the sites. The inference that weakens is the one from this profile to European sites, and a registry analysis based on European data, which Eudamed did not provide, would test it.

Nobody outside the research team reviewed the design. No site, monitor, or surgeon assessed the instruments, which weakens the claim that they can be completed as intended. A content review by experts and a usability test of the templates are the first steps of the validation sequence below.

Some choices of this thesis are delimitations, set on purpose: the focus on the shoulder, the European regulatory framework, and the nonhospital organization as the design case. They set the scope of the claims and are discussed in Section 5.4.

5.6.3 Further research

The framework has completed the development phase of a complex intervention, and the next phases are feasibility, evaluation, and implementation, which do not have to follow one another in strict order [ref: Skivington et al. 2021]. A content review by sites, monitors, and surgeons, with a usability test of the templates, comes first, because an instrument that nobody can complete correctly cannot be evaluated for anything else. A feasibility study in one nonhospital organization and one hospital would then run the minimum set through a complete investigation. It would measure staff time per instrument, the completeness of the records against a defined checklist, and the share of deviations that the site detected before the monitor did.

The volume of a single site does not allow the indicators to be validated, because an investigation spread across several centers leaves each center with few cases over several years. Indicator validation can instead use existing records. A retrospective study could pair historical values of the indicators with the events that followed them, and pooling the records of several centers of one sponsor would provide the volume that one site cannot. Query backlog and reporting timeliness can be recovered from most trial databases, while operative time by complexity level can be recovered only where the site recorded the level.

Two further studies need no new investigation. Applying the instrument set to the documentation of a completed hip or knee device investigation, and recording which fields had to change, would show how much of the structure transfers between specialties. A pilot in which one sponsor supplies the framework to several of its sites would test the arrangement described in Section 5.5.2 and whether the instruments carry into an electronic system without structural change.
