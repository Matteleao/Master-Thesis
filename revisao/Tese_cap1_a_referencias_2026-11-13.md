# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Historical Overview and Recent Updates

Over the past decade, quality management in clinical research has changed<sup>1</sup>. In the older model, sites and sponsors found most problems during monitoring visits and audits and then corrected them case by case. These checks ran throughout the trial, but their purpose was detection and verification, so the quality of a trial depended largely on what the checks found after the work had been done.

Current guidance asks that quality be planned before a study begins and managed across its whole lifecycle<sup>2,3</sup>. The change can be followed through the guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH). ICH E6, the guideline for Good Clinical Practice (GCP) adopted in 1996, set out the responsibilities of sponsors, investigators and ethics committees through detailed procedural requirements. Its second revision, ICH E6(R2), was adopted in 2016. It added a quality management section for the sponsor and introduced risk-based monitoring (RBM), because the same level of checking cannot usefully be applied to every activity of a trial<sup>4</sup>. The same revision made the investigator explicitly responsible for supervising any person or party to whom trial activities are delegated<sup>4</sup>.

ICH E8(R1), adopted in 2021, moved the central question from how a study should be monitored to how it should be designed<sup>2</sup>. With it, Quality by Design (QbD) entered the guidance for clinical research. Under QbD, the people who design a study work out in advance which of its attributes matter most for the protection of participants and for the reliability of the results. They then put effort and resources where the risks to those attributes are highest. ICH E8(R1) calls these attributes critical-to-quality (CtQ) factors. It defines them as attributes of a study “whose integrity is fundamental to the protection of study participants, the reliability and interpretability of the study results, and the decisions made based on the study results”<sup>2</sup>. The eligibility criteria and the collection of endpoint and safety data are among the considerations the guideline lists for identifying them. Because the factors depend on the design and endpoints of each study, they have to be identified again for every protocol.

ICH E6(R3), adopted on 6 January 2025, continued in the same direction<sup>3</sup>. QbD and proportionality now appear among the principles that govern both the design and the conduct of a trial, and quality management is expected to cover the whole trial lifecycle. For the investigator, the revision ties oversight to risk, since the level of oversight of delegated activities should depend on the importance of the data collected and on the risks to participant safety and data reliability<sup>3</sup>. Trial-level quality management, including the identification of CtQ factors and the assessment of risks to them, remains a duty of the sponsor<sup>3</sup>. Commentators have described the revision as a move from a prescriptive model toward a governance model<sup>5</sup>. In the first, quality is shown by following set procedures. In the second, the responsible parties design a system proportionate to the risks of the trial and keep improving it.

The ICH guidelines were written mainly for trials of medicinal products, and their vocabulary reflects that origin. Clinical investigations of medical devices are governed by the GCP standard of the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) for that field, ISO 14155<sup>6</sup>. The standard was first published in 1996, and its latest edition, ISO 14155:2026, appeared on 23 March 2026, after the research for this thesis had started<sup>6</sup>. For the investigational site, the most relevant change of this edition concerns risk. The new edition separates the residual risks of the investigational device from the risks of clinical procedures that the clinical investigation plan (CIP) requires outside routine clinical practice<sup>6</sup>. Device risks continue to follow ISO 14971, the standard that manufacturers apply to manage the risks of a medical device throughout its life<sup>7</sup>, while the procedural risks are assessed as part of the investigation itself. Site staff usually carry out these additional procedures, such as extra imaging or functional assessments, so the new distinction brings part of the risk of an investigation closer to the work done at the site. The edition also adds provisions on the sponsor's side, such as a clinical events committee for the central review of events<sup>6</sup>.

In the European Union, the legal requirements for device investigations come from Regulation (EU) 2017/745 on medical devices (MDR), which governs clinical investigations from their authorization to the reporting of their results<sup>8</sup>. Under the MDR, a device that conforms to a harmonized standard whose reference is published in the Official Journal of the European Union is presumed to meet the requirements that the standard covers<sup>8</sup>. The edition of ISO 14155 listed for this purpose is still EN ISO 14155:2020+A11:2024, according to Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/193<sup>9</sup>. In Europe, the 2020 edition therefore carries the presumption of conformity, while the 2026 edition describes the current state of the art. In the United States, device investigations follow a separate framework under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act<sup>10</sup> and Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR)<sup>11</sup>.

Adoption of these principles is still uneven, even among sponsors. In a global survey of 119 pharmaceutical and biotechnology sponsors on 32 risk-based quality management (RBQM) practices, the identification of CtQ factors was among the five least used practices<sup>12</sup>. Sites were not part of the survey, so it says nothing directly about how sites apply CtQ principles. It does show that the first step of the QbD approach is still difficult for sponsors, who developed the concept and usually have more resources available for it than sites.

## 1.2 Surgical Device Investigations as a Distinct Methodological Context

Most of the methods and regulatory requirements of clinical research were developed for medicinal products, where the intervention is a compound given at a defined dose and schedule. Implantable devices, however, raise design and evaluation problems that this model does not cover<sup>13,14</sup>. In a surgical investigation the intervention is a device placed by a surgeon during an operation, and the evidence about it is collected over years of follow-up. This setting has five conditions that change what quality management has to do at the site, because each of them affects how data are produced in the operating room or during follow-up.

A prosthesis fixed to bone cannot be stopped or withdrawn the way a medicinal product can, so an error during implantation may have lasting consequences for the participant<sup>13,15</sup>. This irreversibility raises the cost of any quality failure at surgery, such as an incorrect implant size or a technical deviation that nobody recorded.

Outcomes may also depend substantially on the surgeon. Results vary between surgeons and, for one surgeon, change as experience grows along the learning curve<sup>16–20</sup>.

Each implanted unit has to remain traceable to the participant who received it. Under the MDR, documentation and record-retention duties support the traceability of the individual investigational device, and ISO 14155:2026 sets requirements for device accountability<sup>6,8</sup>. A joint replacement, such as a shoulder prosthesis, may include several components, each with its own size and identifier, and the record has to show which of them were implanted in each participant. For quality purposes it should also allow the surgical decision behind that configuration to be reconstructed.

Much of the evidence is produced after surgery. Range-of-motion (ROM) assessments and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) often require the participant to attend over several years, and radiographic evaluations add the same demand, with the same procedures applied at each visit. Orthopedic studies show that loss to follow-up is common enough to threaten interpretation when it is not defined and managed consistently<sup>21,22</sup>.

Finally, many of the assessments behind eligibility and outcomes have limited reliability in themselves. Radiological and clinical classifications used in shoulder surgery show limited agreement between observers<sup>23,24</sup>, and PROM scores depend on how participants interpret each item. These are limits of the measurement methods. Local execution adds a further source of variation, for example when the clinician who measures ROM changes between visits or when radiographs are taken with the patient in a different position. This added variation is the part that a site can control.

## 1.3 Quality Responsibility at the Investigational Site

Each of these conditions plays out at the investigational site, because the implant is placed in its operating room and the follow-up assessments that produce the endpoints are carried out by its staff. The guidance divides the responsibility for quality between two parties. The sponsor holds trial-level quality management, so it identifies the CtQ factors of the trial and assesses the risks to them<sup>3</sup>. It then decides which controls prevent or detect errors and sets the limits beyond which a signal calls for action<sup>3</sup>. The investigator is responsible for the conduct of the trial at the site, with qualified staff and adequate resources, and for overseeing delegated activities in proportion to their importance and risk<sup>3,6</sup>.

Much of the work that protects or damages the CtQ factors of a trial happens inside the site, in steps that the protocol only partly specifies. The site decides, for example, how potentially eligible participants are found and checked against the criteria, and its staff deliver the intervention and record the outcome data and adverse events (AEs) on which the analysis depends. The sponsor sees most of this work afterward, through monitoring and data review, so the quality of these steps depends largely on how the site organizes its own processes.

The guidance does not require a site to run a quality system of its own. It asks the investigator to put adequate processes in place and to oversee delegated work, without describing how a site should turn these duties into daily practice. This thesis proposes that a site can apply to its own processes the reasoning that QbD asks of the sponsor, by identifying where its own conduct may fail and by controlling those points while the investigation runs. The proposal goes beyond what the texts require, so its usefulness has to rest on the problems it can address and on what existing instruments already offer.

## 1.4 Existing Quality Instruments and Their Users

Clinical research has built many quality instruments over the past two decades. To find out what already exists for the site, the literature reviewed for this thesis was searched for instruments that plan, measure or support quality in clinical trials. For each instrument the review noted its purpose and the party that applies it, together with the party it is applied to. Most of the instruments found were built by or for sponsors and are applied to the trial and its sites. A smaller group measures or supports the site, and a few are applied by sites to their own organization.

### 1.4.1 Instruments for trial planning and oversight

TransCelerate BioPharma, a nonprofit organization founded in 2012 by biopharmaceutical companies, produced a conceptual framework for a clinical quality management system together with a set of RBM frameworks<sup>25</sup>. The Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative (CTTI), a public-private partnership founded in 2007 by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and Duke University, issued recommendations on QbD with tools to support them<sup>26,27</sup>. These recommendations ask that CtQ factors be identified prospectively and reviewed periodically while the trial runs. They also call for clinical investigators and research site staff, together with trial participants, to take part in the discussion of design and feasibility<sup>26</sup>. The site therefore has a voice in how the quality of a trial is planned. The tools that put the recommendations into practice are addressed to sponsors, however, and the recommendations do not describe how a site should organize the quality of its own work<sup>26</sup>. The Medical Device Innovation Consortium (MDIC), founded in 2012, extended the CTTI approach to devices in a supplement on CtQ factors, which recognizes that device investigations can involve learning curves in product use<sup>28</sup>.

For surgical trials, a further line of work deals with how the intervention itself is delivered. It is also written from the position of the trial team, but it reaches into execution during the trial as well as design. A systematic review of oncology trials grouped the reported challenges to surgical quality assurance (SQA) under credentialing and standardization, with monitoring as a third group, and found the implementation and reporting of effective measures inadequate<sup>29</sup>. The Randomised Oesophagectomy: Minimally Invasive or Open (ROMIO) study then developed concrete methods, including entry criteria for surgeons based on the assessment of operative videos and the standardization of operative technique, with structured monitoring during the trial<sup>30</sup>. A Delphi process with 71 stakeholders later produced 59 strategies to overcome the challenges to SQA in oncology trials<sup>31</sup>. A Delphi checklist for gastrointestinal surgery trials added proctoring and performance monitoring during the trial<sup>32</sup>. In all of these proposals the trial team specifies the measures and scores the results.

Studies of how these measures were applied in practice report weak implementation. In 2024, a multinational panel reviewed randomized trials of arteriovenous grafts for hemodialysis, a surgically implanted device. Quality assurance (QA) was highly variable and often poorly described, with no improvement across the three decades of trials examined. Few trials defined the experience a surgeon needed before taking part, and none had a monitoring program defined before the trial or assessed the technical performance of its surgeons<sup>33</sup>. In surgical oncology, 2 of 12 randomized trials of quality improvement interventions showed an effect on the primary outcome<sup>34</sup>.

These studies show that measures proposed for surgical trials were often not carried into conduct, and that the observation of surgeons during the trial was the step most often missing<sup>29,33</sup>, but they do not show why. The reasons were not studied, and several could contribute, from the workload and funding of the trial to incomplete reporting in the publications themselves. One further explanation, which this thesis explores without claiming to have established it, concerns where the measures are held. The trial team defines SQA frameworks centrally so that one standard applies across sites, while the observation they require takes place in each operating room during conduct. A site with no structure of its own for recording and reviewing the work of its surgeons depends on the trial team to carry out that step.

### 1.4.2 Instruments that measure or support the site

Experts convened through a forum of the United States National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine developed a framework for clinical trial site readiness from the site qualification documents of 14 sponsors. It describes 40 practices in six domains, one of which is quality oversight<sup>35</sup>. Its authors present it as a common set of expectations that can simplify site selection and that experienced sites can use to assess their current practices. The practices are generic and describe readiness before a trial, without the protocol-specific requirements of any single study<sup>35</sup>.

The Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure measures the operational performance of individual trials. It was developed in six Italian research centers, including university and public hospitals. Of 126 candidate indicators, 18 were retained in four domains: participant retention and consent, data completeness and timeliness, AE reporting, and protocol compliance<sup>36</sup>. The trial team at the site completes the measure as a self-assessment of each trial, and a four-item short form is proposed for routine monitoring. Its authors present it as a tool for benchmarking and resource allocation and for identifying underperforming sites across networks<sup>36</sup>. A site can therefore use it to observe its own performance. The items are the same for every trial, however, and the measure was validated in a cross-sectional study. It therefore shows how a trial is performing without linking the result to the CtQ factors of that trial or to the controls that protect them.

Sponsors also measure sites through central monitoring. In an analysis of 1,111 sites in 159 trials, data quality improved at 83% of the sites flagged by central statistical monitoring, against 56% in two historical studies without it<sup>37</sup>. In trials of implantable cardiac devices covering 133 sites and 3,442 participants, the sponsor scored each site on weighted risk factors, including follow-up compliance and missing critical data. It used the ranking to decide where to send on-site monitoring visits. After one year the worst-performing sites had improved in most of these factors, although the evaluation had no comparison group<sup>38</sup>. In both cases the indicators described processes that the site runs, and the improvement followed a signal that the sponsor produced and sent to the site. A review run by the site itself would have to complement this kind of sponsor review and show that it adds information or allows earlier action.

A few instruments are applied by sites to their own organization. The Clinical Trial Assessment of Infrastructure Matrix (CTAIM) was developed in a community cancer centers program of the United States National Cancer Institute. It supports an annual self-assessment of a research program against 11 infrastructure attributes, each with three progressive levels<sup>39</sup>. Clinical trial centers in Italy have implemented ISO 9001:2015, the general standard for quality management systems, and reported organizational workload and insufficient training as the main barriers to certification<sup>40</sup>. Academic institutions add research management systems<sup>41</sup> and clinical trial units whose senior staff rank quality management among their essential services<sup>42</sup>. Guidance written for sites describes what a new private site has to assemble before its first trial, from standard operating procedures to trained staff<sup>43</sup>, and how sites can adapt their practice to ICH E6(R3)<sup>44</sup>. These examples show that local quality management is already an established area of practice. The instruments reviewed organize the research capacity of a site as a whole, but none of them is built around the CtQ factors of a specific protocol, and none deals with the conditions of surgical device research.

Studies of site practice describe what happens where no such structure exists. Several report site-level quality management as reactive and ad hoc and connect it to persistent problems with data quality<sup>37,45,46</sup>. In one mature research network, only about half of the participating sites used a standardized method for identifying AEs<sup>47</sup>. In a survey of 46 Portuguese clinical trial sites, 9 (19.6%) described a standard tool or document that captured risk analysis at site level<sup>48</sup>. Sites value RBM but often experience it as an external requirement over which they have little control<sup>49</sup>. These findings support attention to local processes, but they do not show a uniform deficit across research organizations, and what a site needs depends on what it already runs.

### 1.4.3 What remains to be integrated

A site can already take part in planning the quality of a trial with the sponsor<sup>26</sup>, and it can assess its own infrastructure and readiness with tools built for that purpose<sup>35,39</sup>, as well as score the performance of each trial<sup>36</sup>. It can also certify a general quality management system<sup>40</sup>. None of the instruments reviewed, however, links the CtQ factors of a specific investigation to the risks that the site's own conduct creates. Nor do they connect those risks to the controls and indicators that the site reviews while the investigation runs, with the resulting corrections kept in records the site holds. Surgical device investigations add two needs that no instrument in the material reviewed assigns to the site. One is following the learning of each surgeon with the device during conduct. The other is capturing, as they arise, the intraoperative data that later records cannot reconstruct, such as the identifiers of the implanted components and the reasons for an unplanned substitution.

This situation is consistent with the way the texts and the tools distribute responsibility. Trial-level quality management is assigned to the sponsor<sup>3,6</sup>, and most of the instruments that put it into practice were built for the sponsor. As a result, the quality of the work done at the site is organized mainly by controls that reach it from outside. A site that holds no structure of its own is likely to respond to deviations and findings one case at a time, which agrees with the reactive practice described in the studies above<sup>45,46</sup>. Those studies do not test this explanation, but it defines the function that a site-level framework would have to perform.

## 1.5 Aim, Objectives, and Scope

This thesis aims to develop an operational quality management framework for investigational sites that conduct clinical investigations of shoulder medical devices. The framework applies the QbD and risk-proportionate oversight principles of ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) to the processes that the site itself runs. It is meant to sit alongside the obligations set by the MDR and ISO 14155:2026. It complements the quality system of the sponsor by giving the site a way to connect the CtQ factors of each investigation to the risks and controls of its own processes, with the corrections recorded in documents the site holds. To reach this aim, the work pursued four objectives:

- To compare ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and MDR Annex XV clause by clause, identifying where the instruments agree, where they differ, and where none creates a structured obligation for the investigational site.

- To identify the site-level CtQ domains that organize the areas of local conduct affecting the critical factors of each study, as distinct from the trial-level CtQ factors defined by the sponsor.

- To derive an explicit and traceable set of design requirements for a site-level framework from the regulatory analysis, from the gaps reported in the literature and from the operating conditions of surgical device investigations.

- To develop the framework and its operational instruments, showing how each component answers a stated requirement and how it relates to existing approaches.

The regulatory analysis uses the European framework, in which ISO 14155 is the GCP standard harmonized under the MDR, so that the device standard and the legal requirements could be read together. The corresponding requirements of the United States, set out in Title 21 of the CFR, were not analyzed in the same detail. ICH E6(R3) and ISO 14155 apply internationally, however, and the framework is built around processes common to investigational sites, so it is intended for use beyond Europe once its regulatory references are mapped to local requirements.

The work focuses on orthopedic investigations and, within them, on the shoulder. A framework is usable only when it reaches operational detail, and that level of detail cannot be achieved across several surgical disciplines at once, so a single field had to be chosen. Orthopedics was chosen for two reasons. The MDR classifies total and partial joint replacements as class III devices and, for implantable and class III devices, generally requires clinical investigations before the device reaches the market<sup>8</sup>, so new joint replacements depend on investigations conducted at surgical sites. The co-supervisor of this work, Dr. Nuno Moura, works in orthopedics, which allowed specific feedback on the operational detail of the framework and its instruments. Within orthopedics, the shoulder shows the five conditions of surgical device investigations in a well-documented form, and most of them are shared with other surgical device investigations. Learning periods of about 12 to 86 cases have been reported for shoulder arthroplasty<sup>18,50</sup>, and one shoulder arthroplasty cohort lost 34.3% of its participants to follow-up by the seventh year<sup>22</sup>. Measurement is also a known problem in this field, because the classifications used for eligibility show limited agreement between observers<sup>23,24</sup> and registered shoulder arthroplasty trials vary so widely in their outcome domains and instruments that core outcome sets were developed for shoulder disorders<sup>51,52</sup>.

This is a design study. Its result is an artifact built from documented evidence and from requirements derived in the work itself. The framework has not yet been implemented or evaluated at a site, so any claim about its effects remains an expectation.

# 2. Methodology

## 2.1 Design research

### 2.1.1 Design science research methodology

As already stated, the objective of this thesis is to build a framework of quality that supports the principles required by the guidelines. To achieve this, our work used an adapted Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM), based on Peffers et al.<sup>53</sup>. Design science research (DSR) creates and evaluates artifacts that solve identified organizational problems. DSRM organizes that work into six activities: problem identification and motivation, definition of the objectives for a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication.

Four of those activities are reported here. Chapter 1 and Sections 3.2 and 3.4 carry the problem identification and motivation. Sections 3.3 and 3.5 state the objectives for a solution, as site-level CtQ domains and as design requirements DR1 to DR12. Chapter 4 reports the design and development, and this thesis is the communication. The remaining two activities, demonstration and evaluation, were not undertaken.

Peffers et al. also note that a DSRM study can enter the sequence at different activities, depending on what triggers the research, and that a study triggered by a problem, which they call problem-centered initiation, starts at the first activity. This thesis is problem-centered. The difficulty of managing quality at the site is already recognized in the literature, which describes data-quality management at sites as often reactive and standardized site practices as uncommon<sup>45,47</sup>, together with measures for surgical trials that are rarely carried through conduct<sup>33</sup>. The recent guidelines ask more of the investigator and the site team in the day-to-day quality of conduct, but they do not describe how a site should organize itself to do so. Since the problem is the absence of a structured, site-level quality management framework for surgical device investigations, the artifact is a framework together with the methods that put it to work.

DSRM also asks that the link between problem and solution stay visible. We kept it visible by tracing each major component of the framework to the design requirement it answers, and each requirement to the regulatory gap, CtQ domain, or operating condition that motivated it. Table 3.4 traces each design requirement to its origin, while Figure 4.4 maps the requirements onto the instruments that answer them.

### 2.1.2 Overview of the research process

To support the problem identification and motivation step we used three sources of evidence to feed the design. The first is an analysis of the normative instruments that govern device investigations. The second is a review of the literature. The third is a characterization of the setting where these investigations are conducted.

The regulatory analysis came first and provided the basis on which the design was going to be built. The instruments showed what is required, the literature showed what the field has observed and proposed, and the registry analysis showed in which organizational environments the work is done.

For the definition of the objectives, we derived the site-level CtQ domains from the requirements recorded in the first activity and then formulated the design requirements that the framework must meet. For design and development, we built the pillars and the operational instruments of the framework.

## 2.2 Problem identification

### 2.2.1 Normative instruments

We used ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) as conceptual and design references since they are the main source for the definition of QbD, CtQ, and risk-proportionate quality principles. Both were written for medicinal products, so we took the device-specific obligations from two other instruments. ISO 14155:2026 is the GCP standard for these investigations and our main source of investigator-level requirements. The MDR supplies the European legal framework, including the traceability and safety reporting requirements specific to devices.

We consulted the latest version of three further standards: ISO 13485:2016, ISO 14971:2019 with its guidance document ISO/TR 24971:2020, and ISO 15189:2022. None of them creates an obligation for the investigational site. We use them as models of a documented quality system elsewhere in the same regulatory and supply chain.

### 2.2.2 Reading and comparison of the normative instruments

We read ICH E8(R1), ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and the MDR to identify the responsibilities that the instruments declare for the site or the sponsor, and the quality factors that the site’s own work is known to influence. The matrix was built from ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and the MDR. ICH E8(R1) did not enter the matrix, and we used it as a reference to check the entries and the questions raised during the comparison. In each of the three texts we recorded every requirement that concerns participant protection, reliability of the results or procedural consistency. Each entry was associated with the clause, section or chapter it comes from, with the explicit text of that clause. We also recorded the obligation that the clause creates and the party responsible for it.

Some entries describe what has to be protected, such as informed consent or the accountability of the device, and others describe how quality is controlled, such as risk review or monitoring. Both kinds entered the matrix.

We merged the three sets of entries into a single matrix and compared the requirements across the instruments. Each one was sorted into one of three categories. A requirement is strongly harmonized when all three instruments create an equivalent obligation on the same party. A requirement is partially harmonized when the instruments agree on the principle but bind different parties or use different terms. It is unaddressed at site level when none of the instruments gives the site an obligation that matches the requirement, either because the obligation falls on the sponsor or because no instrument assigns it to any party in operational form.

Requirements found in only some of the instruments were classified case by case. A requirement that is absent from ICH E6(R3) only because it is specific to medical devices, and that both device instruments address, was classified as strongly harmonized. A requirement found in only one of the device instruments was classified by the party it binds, as partially harmonized when that instrument binds the site and as unaddressed at site level when it binds only the sponsor.

### 2.2.3 Literature review

The literature relevant to this work is spread across five fields: quality management in clinical research, SQA in trials, the methodology of surgical trials, the clinical literature on the shoulder, and the organization and performance of investigational sites. We reviewed it narratively so that the review would not drift toward other questions. The review mapped the problem already described in these fields, showed gaps reported by other authors that our reading of the instruments had missed, and allowed us to test our first findings against published work.

We used literature search engines such as Scopus, PubMed, OpenAlex and Google Scholar to find papers related to those fields and tracked citations from the articles we retrieved. We gave priority to recent work, because the regulatory environment has changed substantially in the last few years, although we kept methodological work whose content does not date, which for example applies to the surgical trials literature on learning and clustering effects. We extended the material beyond peer-reviewed publications to regulatory and industry guidance and to standards. The main interpretive sources were the CTTI QbD recommendations with the QbD Documentation Tool, and the 2016 MDIC device-specific supplement on CtQ factors<sup>26,28</sup>.

The search was not confined to an initial phase. It continued throughout the work, because each stage raised questions that the first reading had not covered. When the comparison of the instruments, the derivation of the domains or the design of the instruments depended on a point that the material already gathered did not settle, we ran targeted searches with the same engines and criteria, either to support the point, to understand it better, or to establish its relevance. One of these searches concerned the conditions of clinical research outside academic centers, and its findings led to the registry analysis.

### 2.2.4 Analysis of existing frameworks

The review had a second function beyond mapping the problem. It had to establish what the field has already built, so that the framework could generate something new or improve what already exists.

For every framework, consensus statement, and measurement instrument we identified, we recorded four components. These are the level at which it is defined, the party that operates it, the party it is applied to, and the clinical field it was developed in.

We then compared each instrument with the framework under construction, component by component. For each pairing we recorded one of three outcomes: a component with a direct counterpart, a component with a partial counterpart operating at a different level, and a component with no counterpart.

## 2.3 Registry analysis of investigational site characteristics

The framework’s relevance depends on the conditions under which sites work. Studies identified during the literature review report recurring staffing and infrastructure constraints in clinical research outside academic centers, but we found no published source describing in which organizational environments implantable shoulder device investigations are conducted. We therefore produced this characterization ourselves from public registry data.

### 2.3.1 Data source and search

We first searched the European database on medical devices (Eudamed), set up under the MDR. Applications for clinical investigations are submitted through its electronic system on clinical investigations (Articles 33 and 73). In the application, the sponsor must identify each investigational site and the principal investigator at each one (Annex XV, Chapter II, Section 3.1.3)<sup>8</sup>. Eudamed returned few of these investigations and little site-level data, so we retrieved study records from ClinicalTrials.gov through its public application programming interface (API) v2, with records accessed on 2 May 2026. ClinicalTrials.gov provides structured, publicly accessible records, including the facility fields that sponsors enter for participating locations. Those fields are not always complete or consistent, as earlier assessments of ClinicalTrials.gov data quality have shown<sup>54,55</sup>.

We searched for interventional studies involving shoulder conditions, device interventions, and an industry lead sponsor, with study start dates between 2000 and 2026. We then retained investigations involving implantable shoulder devices. The site record was the unit of analysis, so an organization was counted once for each investigation in which it took part, and the distribution reflects how often each environment hosts investigational activity. The registry data and the institutional sources used for classification were publicly available, and the analysis used no participant-level personal data.

### 2.3.2 Classification of sites

ClinicalTrials.gov records each study location under the facility name entered by the sponsor. Facility names alone provide an unreliable basis for classification because naming conventions vary across countries and organizations. We therefore normalized spelling variants, former names, and multiple entries referring to the same organization.

We classified each organization from public sources, giving priority to its official website and then to public licensing records and recognized institutional directories, with academic publications as the last source. The classification considered inpatient capacity and ownership, together with any declared university or teaching affiliation, and assigned each organization to one of three categories:

- Academic. University or teaching hospitals and university departments, together with the ambulatory facilities these institutions own.

- Nonacademic hospital. Hospitals with inpatient capacity and no declared university affiliation, together with ambulatory facilities belonging to a nonacademic hospital system.

- Nonhospital. Independent orthopedic practices and ambulatory centers outside hospital or university ownership. Exclusively orthopedic inpatient facilities owned by the surgeons who operate in them were also assigned here.

Entries that could not be resolved to a clinical organization, such as sponsor addresses, unnamed facilities, and research foundations, were excluded.

## 2.4 Definition of the objectives of a solution

### 2.4.1 Selection criteria for site-level CtQ domains

ICH E8(R1), in Section 3.2, describes CtQ factors as “attributes of a study whose integrity is fundamental to the protection of study participants, the reliability and interpretability of the study results, and the decisions made based on the study results”<sup>2</sup>. A factor therefore belongs to one study. It depends on the design and endpoints of that study and on the product it tests, and ICH E6(R3) places its identification within the quality management of the sponsor (Section 3.10)<sup>3</sup>. No list written before an investigation exists can state its factors, so this thesis does not define CtQ factors. It defines site-level CtQ domains, the areas of site operation where the factors of an investigation fall and where the work of the site decides whether they are protected. When an investigation starts, the site identifies within each domain the factors that its own conduct can affect, using the protocol and what the sponsor communicates about critical factors and risks.

The test that selected the domains came from ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3), the only instruments examined that define CtQ factors and describe how to identify them. ICH E8(R1) Section 7 lists 18 considerations for identifying them, written from the position of the sponsor as the party that designs the study. Because our object was the site, we used four criteria that apply to any requirement, independent of the party responsible for it. Each of them is a principle already stated in the ICH guidelines, and ICH E6(R3) states three of them among its principles (Principles 4, 7, and 10). We selected them as a joint test for site-level domains and gave them short labels for reference.

1.Proportionality. Study procedures and controls are scaled to the risks of the study and to the importance of the data they produce (ICH E8(R1), Section 3.2; ICH E6(R3), Principle 7).

2.Parsimony. The set of critical factors stays focused. Data and activities that serve neither participant protection nor the primary objectives are left out (ICH E8(R1), Sections 3.2 and 3.3.2).

3.Assigned responsibility. The identification and protection of critical factors are allocated to defined parties, with qualified staff and sufficient resources. At the site, the investigator confirms that each person given a delegated activity is qualified for it (ICH E6(R3), Principle 10 and Sections 2.3.2 and 3.2 to 3.4).

4.Reliance on existing knowledge. What is known about the product and the condition, and about the population for which the product is intended, determines which factors are treated as critical and which controls apply, and both are revised as knowledge grows during the study (ICH E8(R1), Section 3.2; ICH E6(R3), Principle 4 and Section 3.10.1.5).

### 2.4.2 Derivation of the site-level CtQ domains

We built the domains from the comparison matrix in three steps. The first step removed the requirements that a site-level framework does not need to cover, using three filters:

- Responsibility. We removed requirements that only the sponsor controls. We kept a requirement when the duty formally belongs to the sponsor but the work done at the site decides whether it is met.

- Scope. We removed requirements that fall outside the limits of this thesis, and requirements that would add more complexity than value.

- Relevance. We removed requirements that do not apply to a surgical device investigation.

Each requirement that passed the filters was then labeled by what it describes. A requirement states something the site has to protect, such as consent or the record of an implanted device, while a control states how quality is kept, such as risk review or internal verification. Requirements went on to form the domains, and the controls that no instrument assigns to the site became the gaps.

In the second step, we grouped the requirements by the area of the site where the work takes place and by the phase of the investigation in which it happens, and each group became a candidate domain. A requirement could feed more than one group. Requirements on the storage and handling of the investigational device, for example, were grouped with requirements on unit-level accountability, because both concern the device and both are met inside the site.

In the third step, we tested each candidate domain against the four selection criteria, applied together as a single judgment expressed in three questions. Proportionality and parsimony entered the first, assigned responsibility the second, and reliance on existing knowledge the third. For each domain we assessed whether a failure would affect participant protection or the reliability of the results to a meaningful degree, weighing its likelihood against its consequences. We also assessed whether control of the domain demands an assigned responsibility, and to which party it should be assigned. Finally, we checked whether the issue had already been identified, in the literature, together with the risks and difficulties associated with it and any response or solution already available.

### 2.4.3 Formulation of the design requirements

We formulated the design requirements from the three outputs of the previous steps: the gaps of the clause comparison, the site-level CtQ domains, and the operating conditions identified in the literature and the registry analysis. For each gap we stated what the framework must provide where no instrument assigns the site an obligation. For each domain we stated what the site must have in place for the domain to be under control, and for each operating condition we stated the constraint it places on how the framework can work. We kept regulatory obligations apart from design requirements throughout. An obligation defines what is expected of trial conduct, while a design requirement specifies what the framework should provide to help the site meet that expectation. Each requirement was written as a statement that a component can later be checked against, independent of any particular solution, and Table 3.4 traces each one to its origin.

## 2.5 Design and development

### 2.5.1 Derivation of the pillars

We derived the pillars from the clause structure of three standards that govern other organizations in the device conformity chain: ISO 13485:2016<sup>56</sup>, ISO 14971:2019<sup>7</sup>, and ISO 15189:2022, which sets requirements for medical laboratories<sup>57</sup>. None of them applies to the investigational site. We read the main clauses of each standard, grouped them by the function they serve in the organization, and synthesized four pillars from these groups and from the design requirements: Governance, Risk Management, Operational Controls, and Performance Oversight. ISO 13485 applies risk management across its other processes (Clauses 4.1.2 and 7.1), whereas ISO 14971 sets it out as a documented cycle of its own, so we made risk management a separate pillar. We then assigned each design requirement to the pillar whose function it serves. Figure 4.4 maps each requirement to the instruments that answer it.

### 2.5.2 Development of the operational instruments

Each pillar needed instruments that a site can actually operate, so we derived them from three inputs in a fixed order.

We started from the mechanisms used by the ICH guidelines to operationalize CtQ factors. ICH E8(R1) frames risk in terms of probability, detectability, and impact, while ICH E6(R3) uses likelihood, detectability, and impact for risk evaluation and requires pre-specified acceptable ranges, including trial-level quality tolerance limits where appropriate. We operationalized that logic into site-owned records; the guidelines do not prescribe the named site instruments developed here. The adaptation was informed by the CTTI QbD Documentation Tool and MDIC’s 2016 device case material. We then considered the operational conditions of surgical device investigations and what each demanded of a document. Finally, we adapted familiar clinical-research formats, including risk registers, delegation logs, monitoring plans, and corrective-action logs, changing their content and ownership to fit the site-level purpose.

Each instrument carries the requirement it answers. Appendices A through X present each template completed with simulated data. The working files of the design, including the clause comparison matrix, the derivation of the domains and design requirements, and blank versions of the instrument templates, are available in an open repository where each step can be checked (Appendix Y).

# 3. Identified Gaps and Framework Design Requirements

## 3.1 Scope and analytical basis of this chapter

The first two DSRM activities, the identification of the problem and the definition of the objectives of a solution, produced four results. The comparison of the normative instruments identified the gaps, and the same comparison gave the site-level CtQ domains, the operational areas of a surgical center where a local failure would most directly affect participant protection or the reliability of the results. The conditions under which sites conduct these investigations determine both what the framework must control and the form it can take. The design requirements follow from these results, and Table 3.4 traces each one to its origin.

## 3.2 The regulatory and operational gap

We compared ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and MDR Annex XV clause by clause. The unit of comparison is the individual requirement, because that is the level at which a clause creates an obligation. For each requirement we recorded the obligation each instrument creates, the party it binds, and the clause it derives from. The comparison sorted the requirements into three categories: strongly harmonized, partially harmonized, and unaddressed at site level.

The comparison recorded 251 entries, 100 from ICH E6(R3), 90 from ISO 14155:2026, and 61 from the MDR, which we grouped into 43 themes. ICH E6(R3) gives the investigator or the site a duty in 55% of its entries and ISO 14155:2026 in 59%, while only 25% of the MDR entries reach the site. Of the 43 themes, 8 are strongly harmonized, 21 are partially harmonized, and 14 carry no obligation for the site.

The first result is agreement. At requirement level, a requirement is strongly harmonized when the three instruments in the clause comparison create an equivalent obligation on the same party. Eight themes fall here: informed consent, participant information, participant rights, investigator qualification, protocol compliance, medical care, source records, and confidentiality. In each of them the three instruments treat the requirement in broadly equivalent terms, both in principle and in the obligation they create.

The second result is partial agreement. A requirement is partially harmonized when the instruments agree on the principle but bind a different party or use different terms. Twenty-one themes fall here, among them protocol deviation (PD) documentation, device accountability, follow-up, and safety reporting. Safety reporting shows how far apart the instruments can be while they agree on the principle. They agree on expedited reporting, but they differ in two ways that matter in practice. ISO 14155 sets no fixed reporting deadline for the investigator. It requires reporting to the sponsor without unjustified delay and leaves the operative timeline to the CIP. ICH E6(R3) asks the investigator to report serious adverse events to the sponsor immediately, while the MDR applies its reporting interval to the sponsor, who reports to the Member States, and asks only that investigators report to the sponsor in a timely manner<sup>3,8</sup>.

The instruments also differ in how they classify an event. Since the 2026 update, ISO 14155:2026 separates events related to the investigational device from events related to procedures required by the CIP that fall outside routine clinical practice, and the two are assessed separately<sup>6</sup>. It also establishes central review and classification methods for safety or effectiveness endpoints, including serious adverse device effects (SADEs) and device deficiencies (DDs), when a Clinical Events Committee (CEC) is used<sup>6</sup>. The note to Clause 6.2.3 expressly recognizes that national regulations do not necessarily require categorization of AEs related to these procedures. ICH E6(R3) has no equivalent category because it was written for medicinal products, where the intervention and the procedure are one thing. These divergences affect how a site-level instrument has to be built, in particular its safety indicator.

The third category holds the 14 themes that carry no obligation for the site. Six of them concern the design of the investigation or functions the sponsor holds centrally, such as statistics, insurance, and oversight committees, and the responsibility filter removed them. Three are requirements that no instrument assigns to the site, which are surgical technique, endpoint assessment, and the learning curve, and they entered the CtQ domains. The other five are control functions. They give four of the five gaps listed in Table 3.1, since self-inspection belongs to the improvement cycle of Gap 5, and the learning curve gives the fifth. Most of these functions are required at trial level, with responsibility assigned to the sponsor. The site participates by supporting monitoring, following the CIP, reporting observations, and implementing corrective actions assigned to it<sup>3,6,8</sup>. For these functions, the gap concerns the absence of an explicit requirement for arrangements that the site owns and operates.

Under the responsibility filter, sponsor-assigned requirements were retained when site activities determined whether they were fulfilled. Events relevant to these functions arise in the operating room, during follow-up visits, and in site records. The site therefore contributes directly to detecting and controlling these events, while the instruments generally assign the overarching systems to the sponsor.

ICH E6(R3) and ISO 14155:2026 already give the investigator several quality duties, such as supervising delegated activities and making sure that staff are qualified and resources adequate. ICH E6(R3) also asks the investigator, together with the sponsor, to implement proportionate processes for the data of the trial (ICH E6(R3), Section 4). These duties appear in separate clauses, and meeting them consistently depends on a system that organizes them, but no clause in the three instruments requires that system. Gap 1 is therefore the absence of a structured site-level quality management system (QMS), which the manufacturer and the medical laboratory in the same chain hold under ISO 13485:2016 and ISO 15189:2022.

The instruments require the risks of an investigation to be assessed and reviewed throughout its conduct, and they assign this cycle to the sponsor<sup>3,6</sup>. Many of these risks come from the way the site executes the protocol, and the site sees them first. For the cycle to work at that level, the site needs a register of its own risks (Gap 2), fed by an internal observation of what it actually does (Gap 4). Those observations then have to lead to correction and prevention through an improvement loop (Gap 5). The duty formally belongs to the sponsor, but the work done at the site decides whether the cycle works. The instruments, however, only ask the site to support sponsor monitoring and to apply the corrective actions it receives.

On surgical competence the three instruments agree, and sites can meet them, because all three treat competence as a qualification confirmed before the investigation starts. The surgical trials literature goes further and recommends that the surgeon’s performance also be followed during conduct, since it changes along the learning curve, and it reports that trials rarely do so<sup>29–31,33</sup>. The comparison found no site obligation for the learning curve, and this literature shows why the absence matters. Gap 3 is the distance between the practice it recommends and what the instruments require.

The sponsor’s quality system cannot fill this space on its own, because it is built for the trial and not for any one site. In a multicenter investigation, the sponsor’s controls are designed to apply one standard across every participating site, which is a deliberate choice when the aim is comparability. The same choice leaves out what is specific to each site, such as its daily workflow, its staffing, and the technical limits that follow from its size and structure. The literature describes the consequence from the site’s side. Site-level quality management is often reactive and handled case by case<sup>37,45,46</sup>, standardized approaches remain uncommon<sup>47</sup>, and sites experience risk-based monitoring as an external requirement over which they have little control<sup>49</sup>. Part of the data that matter most is also generated at the point of care, where external oversight cannot see it in time.

### 3.2.1 Site conduct and the clinical evidence of the device

Under the MDR, every device subject to conformity assessment needs a clinical evaluation, with clinical evidence appropriate to the device and its intended purpose. Article 61 and Annex XIV set out how the evaluation is planned and the data appraised, and the results go into a clinical evaluation report (CER) within the technical documentation. Where the available data do not show conformity with the general safety and performance requirements, the manufacturer has to generate them through clinical investigations, governed by Article 62(1), Articles 63 to 80, and Annex XV. The data produced at the site, in the operating room and in follow-up, therefore become the evidence behind the CER that the notified body assesses, and each link in that chain is only as reliable as the one before it.

The manufacturer works under a documented quality system, set out in ISO 13485:2016 with corrective and preventive action, and ISO 14971:2019 adds a documented cycle for managing device risk<sup>7,56</sup>. A medical laboratory sits outside this chain but also produces results on which others base decisions, and ISO 15189:2022 gives it an equivalent system<sup>57</sup>. ISO 14155:2026 gives the investigational site nothing similar, since its Clause 9.1 places clinical quality management on the sponsor and Clause 10, on the responsibilities of the principal investigator (PI), covers qualification and conduct, including the correction of significant noncompliance, without asking for an ongoing local system. The fourth edition enlarged the sponsor’s side further, with risks arising from investigation processes and a provision on the CEC<sup>6</sup>. Of the two organizations that produce the evidence behind the CER, only the site has no requirement to hold a quality system of its own.

These five gaps do not necessarily indicate defects in the regulatory framework. Addressing them may support compliance with existing duties, even where the instruments do not explicitly require the proposed site-level arrangements. They mark where the site can act on the quality of the investigation and on the safety of its own conduct. The framework adds an operational layer to support the implementation of ICH E6(R3) principles in daily site practice.

## 3.3 Site-level Critical-to-Quality domains

ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) ask the sponsor to identify the CtQ factors of each study when it designs the protocol<sup>2</sup>. These factors depend on the design and endpoints of the study, so they change from one investigation to the next. A center, however, runs every investigation through the same processes, from consent and surgery to imaging and follow-up, and a failure in any of them can affect the factors of whatever study is running. A site-level analysis should therefore ask in which areas of its own operation a center can compromise those factors in the cases it contributes. We therefore decided to derive domains, areas of operation that require special attention, and left the factors to be identified by the site for each investigation, within each domain, from the protocol and from what the sponsor communicates as critical and risky.

The derivation started from the 43 themes of the comparison. The responsibility filter initially removed nine that the sponsor alone decides or holds, such as statistics and the Investigator’s Brochure. The scope filter defined for this framework removed another five that fall outside the framework, such as record retention, or whose content another theme already covers. The relevance filter removed randomization and blinding, because in a surgical device investigation the surgeon knows which device is implanted and the allocation is run centrally, so the site follows a procedure set elsewhere, and a failure to follow it is a protocol deviation, which is already covered. Of the 28 remaining themes, 22 are requirements, stating what the site has to protect, and 6 are controls, stating how quality is kept. Only the requirements were grouped, since a control applies across every area of the site and would have produced overlapping domains. The learning curve is the only theme that serves twice, as a requirement within domain 6 and as the basis of Gap 3.

Grouping the 22 requirements by the area of the site where the work happens and by the phase of the investigation produced 12 candidate domains. Each of them met the four selection criteria, which gave the twelve domains in Table 3.2. Eight of the twelve domains rest entirely on requirements that at least one instrument already assigns to the investigator, and for these the framework only organizes and facilitates duties the site already holds. Domains 6 and 9 rest partly, and domains 5 and 8 wholly, on requirements that no instrument assigns to the site. Surgical technique is described by the sponsor in the CIP, endpoints are defined by the sponsor and may be read centrally, and the learning curve appears only in ISO 14155:2026, as a roll-in that the sponsor plans<sup>6</sup>. These requirements entered because the work they describe takes place at the site and because the literature reviewed shows that it influences the results<sup>27,28</sup>.

Device management takes a form specific to device investigations, with accountability from receipt through implantation in a named participant to return or explantation, and ISO 14155:2026 adds, where appropriate, an implant card, prepared by the sponsor as a template, that the investigator gives the participant after implantation<sup>6</sup>. Procedural consistency and learning-curve control turn surgeon-dependent variability, which the methodological literature treats as a design problem, into something the site controls and monitors. Imaging can decide eligibility and provide an endpoint, and it also supplies safety data, so variability in how images are acquired produces systematic error that cannot be told apart from clinical variance. The imaging literature is general or based on knee trials, so we used it with caution<sup>58,59</sup>.

The device supplement of the MDIC to the CTTI principles offers an external check on four of the domains. Among the considerations it adds for device trials are site feasibility, including the referral base and the equipment for imaging, together with device accountability, product training with proctoring, and the learning curve<sup>28</sup>. These correspond to domains 1, 4, 6, and 9.

These twelve domains govern the framework in two ways. Every component is introduced to control the risks associated with one or more domains, and every performance indicator is anchored in at least one domain.

## 3.4 Operational conditions constraining the design

The gaps establish what is missing, and the domains establish what has to be controlled. Neither yet establishes what form a site-level framework can take. That is settled by the operational conditions under which the framework would have to work, and these conditions constrain the design as tightly as the regulatory analysis does.

Surgical device investigations are conducted under conditions that a site-level framework has to take into consideration. Six main conditions apply in total. Five are structural and common to surgical trials, and two of those, operator dependence and traceability, are restrictive enough to be examined separately. The sixth, the organizational setting of the sites, is not structural but heavily influences the design, and it is also one of the findings of this thesis.

### 3.4.1 Design consequences of the five structural conditions

1. Irreversibility. A drug can be stopped, reduced, or replaced if it fails or causes harm. But once a prosthesis is fixed to bone, the procedure has produced a permanent change in the patient's anatomy. This raises the operational cost of every quality failure that happens at surgery, because an incorrect implant size, an undocumented technical deviation, or a missing traceability record cannot be corrected as a transcription error can. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) has described poor risk identification and mitigation as producing a reactive approach based mainly on corrective action<sup>1</sup>. ICH E8(R1) warns that resources are needed to identify and prevent or control errors that matter, and the CTTI CtQ principles also place attention on prevention before conduct. A site-level framework must therefore identify in advance which steps of the surgical pathway are irreversible, and therefore critical, and place controls on them so that errors are prevented and information is not lost.

2. Operator dependence. In device investigations, results depend on the device and just as much on the person and technique used to implant it<sup>17</sup>. Requirements for surgeon qualification and adequate training with the specific device are already established by the instruments, but other sources of variation also require control, including learning-curve effects, differences among surgeons in positioning and soft-tissue management, and intraoperative judgment. The study by Blencowe et al.<sup>60</sup> provides us one method to evaluate this effect by setting standards for surgical intervention delivery before the trial and monitor it during conduct through forms that capture deviations. Quality management therefore has to characterize this variation without trying to remove it, because removing it would mean removing surgical judgment itself. The consequence for the design is that the framework needs the operator as a unit of analysis, alongside the participant and the device.

3. Device traceability. Pharmaceutical and device trials both require records linking batch or serial numbers to participants, but the requirements differ in how long the link must be kept<sup>3,6</sup>. A medicinal product is used or returned so once the trial ends there is nothing left in the participant to trace. Its accountability record is kept with the essential records, but no one has to maintain an active link between the product and the participant. An implanted device remains in the participant for years, so the MDR requires post-investigation follow-up that covers traceability, and the records must also cover any return or explantation. Traceability here also extends to reconstructing the surgical decisions that produced that configuration in that participant, which creates a documentary burden of a different order. The framework therefore needs a record of each implanted unit that the site keeps current for as long as the participant is followed.

4. The operational weight of follow-up. Long follow-up is not unique to surgical trials, but the measurements involved like ROM and radiographic assessment of implant position require the participant to be physically present, while patient-reported outcomes (PROs) require consistent collection at the specified time points. Assessments vary less when the same procedure is used at every visit and, where the protocol provides for it, when an independent reviewer reads the results centrally<sup>61</sup>. None of the three instruments asks for the same assessor at every visit, so keeping assessment consistent over the years of follow-up falls to the site, and images need the same acquisition procedure and quality control at every time point for the same reason<sup>58,62</sup>. This raises the cost of every visit and increases the risk of attrition over the years between surgery and the late assessments. Orthopedic studies with at least three years of follow-up reported 14.8% loss to follow-up, compared with 9.8% in studies with shorter follow-up<sup>21</sup>, and one shoulder arthroplasty cohort reported 34.3% loss by the seventh year<sup>22</sup>.

5. Limited classification reliability. Shoulder investigations rely on radiological and clinical classifications that in many cases show only fair-to-moderate interobserver reliability<sup>63</sup>. Widely used classifications for proximal humeral fractures have been reported with interobserver kappa coefficients in the fair range, even when three-dimensional models reconstructed from computed tomography (CT) data are used<sup>64</sup>. Two assessors looking at the same patient can therefore reach different conclusions about eligibility or about an outcome, and the database cannot distinguish that variance from genuine clinical variance. Outcome measurement makes this worse. Shoulder arthroplasty trials vary widely in outcome domains, instruments, and assessment time points. That inconsistency was substantial enough to motivate core outcome sets for shoulder disorders<sup>51,52</sup>. The framework therefore has to control the sources of this variance that lie at the site, such as assessor qualification and the acquisition procedure.

### 3.4.2 Operator-dependent variability as a design constraint

Operator dependence produces clustering<sup>65</sup>. Participants operated on by the same surgeon share that surgeon’s technique and intraoperative decision thresholds, as well as the perioperative management that follows, so for the purposes of quality they are not independent of one another, and a problem in the surgeon’s execution reaches every case that surgeon contributes. Clustering also matters for the design and analysis of a trial. In simulations, ignoring non-negligible clustering produced type I error rates above 20%<sup>66</sup>, yet only 21.5% of eligible orthopedic randomized trials with several centers or treatment providers used an analysis that accounted for it<sup>67</sup>.

The methodological literature on surgical trials counts clustering among the central challenges of randomized trials involving surgery, together with the learning curve that interacts with it<sup>65,68</sup>, and practice still varies. In a survey of 44 United Kingdom Clinical Trials Units, clustering was handled mostly at design, through stratification that balances each surgeon’s cases across the arms of the trial, and at analysis, through adjustment. Learning was managed mainly through a minimum level of expertise for the treatment provider, set by 89% of units, while only one unit reported analyzing learning effects as part of the main analysis<sup>69</sup>.

Operator learning is therefore controlled mainly at entry but is rarely observed during conduct. In a review of 247 published surgical trials, 39% addressed expertise through predefined surgeon credentials, while, again, only one trial analyzed learning during conduct; a related analysis of funder guidance identified the same general concern<sup>70</sup>. The clause comparison reaches the same boundary, since the governing instruments address qualification and training and do not assign ongoing observation of operator learning to the site. The interval between entry qualification and performance during conduct is exactly what a site-level instrument could cover, and it is the interval that Gap 3 in Table 3.1 identifies. A protocol can define a procedure in general terms, but it cannot standardize the many small decisions through which a particular surgeon implants a particular device in a particular anatomy. The site therefore needs an instrument that operates during conduct.

### 3.4.3 Traceability as a point-of-care obligation

Unit-level traceability is distinctive in how detailed it is, and it is distinctive again in the circumstances under which it has to be met. It is executed in the operating room, under time pressure, by staff whose attention is on the surgical procedure. ISO 14155:2026 requires records of receipt, identification, quantity, dates of use, participant identification, and return or explantation, together with written procedures for the full accountability process. The sponsor maintains the record of physical location through delivery to the site, and the investigator's records continue the link through use in the participant and any return or explantation. The MDR also requires access control, return procedures, and follow-up in the CIP, immediate identification and recall of devices in an emergency, and post-investigation traceability. In surgery, the required dates of use and participant identification make capture at the point of care the most reliable operational response. A break in this chain can compromise regulatory compliance and the attribution of an AE to a specific device or lot. The cited clauses do not state that such a break automatically invalidates the affected case.

Traceability is the clearest example of a broader pattern that runs through the domains in Table 3.2. A class of critical data is generated at the point of care, external oversight cannot see it while it is happening, and it cannot be recovered once it is lost. Four examples share these properties: the intraoperative decision to substitute a component, the technical deviation prompted by unexpected anatomy, the immediate postoperative image that may or may not be adequate, and the early complication whose relationship to the device is still undetermined. A framework that deals with this class of data has to operate when the data are generated, because sponsor verification takes place afterward.

### 3.4.4 Where these investigations are conducted

During the literature review, we found evidence that clinical research is concentrated in the United States and that, within the country, much of it is done away from academic centers. Between 2006 and 2012, the United States ran 540 trial site-years per million inhabitants, more than twice the rate of the other high-income OECD countries<sup>71</sup>. Open Payments data show that a large majority of US investigators in commercially sponsored trials conduct them in their private practice<sup>72</sup>. In FDA records from 2008 to 2015, one third of principal investigators each year were filing for the first time, and 40% of those active in a given year did not file again the following year<sup>73</sup>. Among the principal investigators of FDA-regulated drug trials, 63.8% found it burdensome to fit the trial around their other work<sup>74</sup>.

The burden does not rest on investigators alone. Studies of the organizations that host trials report shortages of research staff and infrastructure, and they report them in hospitals as well as in community settings. In a national survey of 58 community cancer centers, 52% named limited staffing as a challenge to conducting research<sup>75</sup>. Stakeholders in Ontario described community hospitals as willing to join trials but mostly without research infrastructure<sup>76</sup>, and authors from a Swiss private hospital network report early difficulties with infrastructure, regulatory compliance and quality assurance in setting up a trial unit<sup>77</sup>. Device research strains even academic centers. Leaders of children’s hospitals linked to the FDA pediatric device consortia described device trial infrastructure in academic medical centers as suboptimal, and found that drug trial infrastructure, though often well developed, did not necessarily carry over to device trials<sup>78</sup>.

The constraints weigh more where organizational capacity is lower. Among community cancer centers running therapeutic trials, 2 of 8 rural practices offered phase 1 trials against 22 of 33 urban ones, and the centers running none cited limited infrastructure, funding and staffing<sup>75</sup>. Respondents with experience of nontraditional research sites named deficient operational infrastructure as their main challenge and quality oversight as a main support need<sup>79</sup>.

All of these studies concern drug trials, and investigations of implantable shoulder devices add the five structural conditions, which place further demands on the site. This raised the question of where shoulder device investigations mostly take place: in hospital settings, which report these constraints but have some organizational support behind the site, or to a large extent in organizations with less capacity to absorb them. We looked for published data on how these investigations are distributed across organizational environments and found none, so we built that distribution ourselves from public registry records.

The registry search produced 46 eligible industry-sponsored investigations of implantable shoulder devices with 355 site records. Eight records could not be resolved to a clinical organization and were excluded. One investigation lost its only listed site with this exclusion, which left 347 site records from 45 investigations and 238 organizations. We grouped the organizations into the three categories of Table 3.3, because academic status and ownership could be identified consistently from public institutional descriptions.

### 3.4.5 The operating conditions of the target center

In Table 3.3, nonhospital organizations held 108 of the 347 site records (31.1%), too large a share to treat as an exception, although most of them were in the United States. Within this group, the framework was designed for a small independent clinic with few research staff and little research infrastructure, the environment for which it can assume the least, because no hospital system or university stands behind the site. The category is broader than this case, since it also includes surgeon-owned orthopedic facilities with inpatient beds, and the registry records neither staffing nor infrastructure. The registry therefore supports the relevance of designing for sites outside hospital systems, and the choice of the small clinic is a design decision.

The evidence on research staffing and infrastructure comes from drug trials, and part of it concerns drug administration. Most of what it reports, however, concerns staff, clinician time, infrastructure and oversight, which a site needs whatever the product under investigation, and the conditions of an implantable device investigation add to these demands. The few studies on device and orthopedic research point the same way. In a pilot within a UK hip preservation registry, centers given additional administrative support achieved 79% follow-up compliance at 30 days against 55% in centers without it, and 69.4% against 47% at 90 days<sup>80</sup>. Experts on pediatric device trials rated a device training plan among the most important infrastructure components for implanting and using the device<sup>81</sup>. A small clinic conducting a shoulder device investigation is therefore unlikely to face lighter constraints than those reported for drug trials, although the present work did not test this inference.

The registry did not record staffing, budgets or the priority each organization gives to research, so these conditions entered the design as assumptions. The framework does not assume a dedicated research office or quality function, and it allows research duties to be shared among clinical staff. The framework had to fit within existing site roles without depending on a new physical infrastructure or dedicated quality department. Its instruments had to work on paper or within an electronic system already available at the site. Key quality decisions also had to remain in records held by the organization when individual responsibilities changed.

These conditions define the minimum implementation level of the scalability model. Organizations with established research departments can place the same functions within their existing systems and use deeper controls.

## 3.5 Design requirements

A CtQ domain defines where the site has to protect the critical factors of an investigation, but it does not say what the site must have in place for that domain to be under control. The design requirements state this. They complete the second DSRM activity, the definition of the objectives of a solution, because they turn these findings into statements of what an acceptable framework has to deliver, written so that each of its components can later be checked against them.

Table 3.4 lists the twelve requirements and traces each one back to its origin. DR1 to DR5 come mainly from the regulatory gaps, and DR6 to DR9 from the CtQ domains and the structural conditions of surgical investigation. DR10 to DR12 come from the operating conditions of the target center.

Requirements and domains do not correspond one to one. DR2 covers all twelve domains, because each of them needs its execution risks identified at the site. The four domains that rest wholly or partly on requirements no instrument assigns to the site (domains 5, 6, 8 and 9) feed the requirements on operator proficiency, point-of-care capture and measurement variance (DR3, DR6 and DR7). The other domains enter the set through how the site organizes their control, for example through indicators (DR8) and correction (DR5). Informed consent (domain 3) is the only domain with no requirement beyond DR2, since the instruments regulate consent in detail.

Meeting one requirement can also make another harder to meet. DR11 rules out capital investment and asks the site to reuse its existing documentary system, so it limits how DR1 to DR9 can be met. A site could satisfy every functional requirement with an information system built for the investigation, and that solution would pass the regulatory analysis while failing the operational one. When two requirements point to different solutions, we treat the operational constraint as binding, because a control the site cannot afford to run will not be run.

No instrument examined here requires a site to operate a quality system (Gap 1), so the framework cannot be justified by pointing to a rule that demands it. Its justification comes from tracing each part back to its origin. Each component answers a requirement, and each requirement comes from a regulatory gap, a CtQ domain or an operating condition (Table 3.4). The gaps and domains trace back to the clause comparison, with Gap 3 checked against the surgical trials literature, and the operating conditions to the literature and registry evidence. Where the framework adds a control that no instrument requires, that control answers a gap the comparison documented. Everywhere else, the framework organizes duties the instruments already assign, so that a site can carry them out under the conditions in which it works.

# 4. Results: The Quality Framework

## 4.1 Overview and design rationale

The third DSRM activity, design and development, produced a framework that meets DR1 to DR12. Each component is described in enough detail for a site to implement and operate it.

The Site-Centered Operational Quality Management Framework consists of four pillars, each with a set of instruments. A lifecycle assigns the instruments to the phases in which they are prepared and used (DR9), while a scalability model determines which instruments a site runs according to its organizational resources and the risk of the investigation (DR12). The role structure within Governance assigns each quality function to a defined person and separates authority from responsibility for individual tasks (DR10).

Governance, Risk Management, Operational Controls, and Performance Oversight together answer DR1 to DR8. Of the two organizations producing the evidence behind the clinical evaluation report, only the site has no requirement to hold a quality system of its own, so the pillars bring to the site the functions of the systems that ISO 13485:2016 and ISO 14971:2019 give the manufacturer and ISO 15189:2022 gives the medical laboratory<sup>7,56,57</sup>. None of the three standards uses this exact division.

Governance draws on the management clauses of the three standards (ISO 13485 Clause 5, ISO 14971 Clause 4, and ISO 15189 Clause 5). Risk Management draws on ISO 13485 Clauses 4.1.2 and 7.1, ISO 14971 Clauses 5 to 7, and ISO 15189 Clauses 5.6 and 8.5. Operational Controls draws on ISO 13485 Clause 7, including Clause 7.5, and ISO 15189 Clause 7. ISO 14971 does not contribute to this pillar because it does not address the control of routine processes. Performance Oversight draws on the measurement and review clauses of all three standards (ISO 13485 Clauses 8.2.4, 8.4, and 8.5, ISO 14971 Clauses 9 and 10, and ISO 15189 Clauses 8.7 to 8.9).

The instruments form one set that can work on paper or within an electronic system the site already uses (DR11). This operational constraint takes precedence wherever it conflicts with another requirement, so no component depends on new software or new staff. We designed the instruments after defining the functions of all four pillars. Each instrument therefore belongs to one pillar but can use records produced under another.

The conditions of the target center, a small independent clinic, shaped the instruments and set the minimum implementation level. Because the components are described as functions, centers with different staffing structures can assign their own posts to those functions.

Each instrument describes a control function, so a site that already keeps a record performing that function uses it without opening a new one. The instruments are not regulatory requirements and do not replace sponsor procedures or the controls defined in the CIP. Records already required by the regulations remain in place. A new record is justified only where a critical process would otherwise lack a control or a record of its execution.

## 4.2 Pillar 1: Governance

None of the regulatory instruments examined requires a structure that brings together the quality duties they assign to the investigator (Gap 1). Governance provides that structure for DR1 and DR10, in CtQ domains 1 and 6, while leaving each duty with the party that already holds it. The oversight model defines how the PI exercises authority for quality, the delegation structure identifies who may perform each task, and device training documentation sets the preparation each task requires, with a separate qualification and authorization decision for surgeons. Once a surgeon starts operating, Risk Management tracks the learning curve and provides evidence for later authorization decisions.

### 4.2.1 The oversight model

During design, we compared three ways of allocating oversight under different conditions of staffing and capacity. The descriptions of trial-site operations we reviewed confirm that these functions need to be covered<sup>43,82</sup>, but they do not classify them into these arrangements. We therefore use the three arrangements as design options.

In the first arrangement, the PI intervenes directly in most critical activities and personally reviews most documentary and operational workflows. This becomes difficult to sustain as activity grows. Site PIs already carry scientific and regulatory duties alongside recruitment, supervision, and administration, and their contribution is often under-recognized<sup>82</sup>. Routine review then takes time from the decisions that need the PI's clinical judgment, and the arrangement scales poorly because the PI would have to follow every area of the investigation.

In the second arrangement, the study coordinator manages daily work while the PI retains final authority. This is a workable allocation: the clinical research coordinator is a core site function<sup>43</sup>, and ICH E6(R3) permits delegation while the investigator retains ultimate responsibility and proportionate oversight<sup>3</sup>. Oversight becomes nominal, however, if the site leaves undefined the decisions that require the PI, such as borderline eligibility, an unplanned component substitution, or the interpretation of a revision, and sets neither a review schedule nor a route for escalation.

We adopted a third arrangement, a tiered model of PI oversight adjusted to risk. It retains delegation and adds the review structure, schedule, and escalation route missing from the second arrangement, so the PI can concentrate on decisions requiring clinical judgment. The PI's involvement in each task follows the risk it poses to the CtQ factors identified when the investigation starts, which applies the CtQ logic of ICH E8(R1) to the allocation of the investigator's attention<sup>2</sup>. The site therefore sets thresholds and a review schedule before enrolling the first participant and assigns someone to keep the meetings running.

Five mechanisms make this oversight a documented and scheduled practice, as DR1 requires. The criticality grade assigned to each delegated task provides the basis for the other mechanisms.

- Risk-tiered involvement. For each type of activity, the site states in advance which decisions require the PI's own judgment and which activities the PI can review afterward. This distinction depends on the device and procedure and may change as the team gains experience, so the site revisits it at each oversight cycle. Requiring direct involvement too often recreates the workload of the first arrangement, while requiring it too rarely can leave the PI without meaningful oversight. The Criticality-Coded Delegation Matrix (CCDM) records the supervision level for each task (Appendix A).

- Criticality grading of delegated activity. Each task receives a grade that determines its level of supervision and its escalation trigger. Even routine work can be critical, since a scheduling decision can place a follow-up outside the protocol window. The grades therefore come from the CtQ factors of the investigation itself, because a scheme copied from another site may overlook risks specific to its procedures or team. The CCDM records these grades.

- Milestone review. The PI formally reviews the investigation at four points where the most consequential threats concentrate: procedural implementation, the immediate postoperative period, the approach to endpoint assessment, and close-out. A review must take place after the events whose problems it needs to identify, but early enough to correct a pattern before it affects further cases. These points reflect the risk profile of a shoulder implant investigation and would need revision for another device or procedure. The Milestone Review Log (MRL) records each decision (Appendix B).

- Event-triggered escalation. Events that immediately threaten participant safety or causal interpretation require prompt notification to the PI, whatever the scheduled review date. The CCDM defines the trigger for each task, and the PI Oversight Template (POT) records the PI's decision and the resulting action (Appendix C).

- Oversight meetings. The site team meets regularly to conduct the review, giving oversight a structure beyond ad hoc contact. We adapted the consensus-based review of events that ISO 14155:2026 assigns to a sponsor's clinical events committee (CEC)<sup>6</sup> and the periodic risk review described in the EMA reflection paper<sup>1</sup> to this site-level meeting. We found no study showing that such forums improve oversight at site level. Maintaining them is particularly difficult in the nonhospital organizations targeted here, because the people attending are also responsible for the work being reviewed. Each meeting closes one cycle of the POT.

### 4.2.2 Delegation, roles, and functions

The PI can adjust supervision to a task's criticality only if the site knows who performs it, whether that person is authorized and trained, and who must act when an escalation trigger is met. DR10 asks for a defined holder of each quality function, so that no CtQ function stops when one person is unavailable.

A conventional delegation log records who performs each trial-related activity. ICH E6(R3) asks for this record in proportion to the significance of the activity and accepts that work performed as part of clinical practice may need no delegation record (ICH E6(R3), Section 2.3.3). ISO 14155:2026 requires the names, signatures, functions, and authorizations of new team members to be documented. Neither requires a criticality grade or evidence of competence in the same record. The framework needs both to connect the supervision assigned to each task with the training or qualification that permits a person to perform it. The investigator remains accountable for delegated work. The record therefore has three properties.

- Task-based and graded. Each entry identifies a specific task and its criticality grade, which sets how closely the PI reviews it. Authorization for one task does not imply authorization for another. A research nurse may be well placed to coordinate visits and keep screening logs but may not be qualified to resolve a clinically meaningful discrepancy in operative documentation.

- Competency-gated. An entry becomes active only after the person has been released for the task following training or, for a surgeon, qualified and authorized for the procedure.

- Version-controlled. Each change of person, task, or grade is dated. The record can therefore show who was authorized at any point in the investigation, including after the people named in it have left, as DR10 requires.

The role structure comprises six functions covering the work of an orthopedic implant investigation, each with a defined limit of authority (Table 4.1). Under ISO 14155:2026, delegation transfers execution while the PI retains responsibility. The limits of authority distinguish responsibility for individual tasks from the authority to make decisions. Clinical judgments remain with the PI or surgeon, and endpoint adjudication belongs to the central reader where one is used.

### 4.2.3 Device training documentation

Before assigning a task, the site uses the training records to check that the person has received the preparation it requires. Monitors and inspectors use the same records as evidence that critical activities were performed by staff who were both delegated and prepared for the investigation.

Under the MDR and ISO 14155:2026, the sponsor defines the training content through the CIP and the Investigator’s Brochure and verifies that training is documented, while the PI trains the site team before each member starts an assignment<sup>6,8</sup>. ICH E6(R3) limits trial-related training to what delegated activities require beyond a person's usual training, and ICH E8(R1) asks that training match each role. None of these texts specifies the training needed for each site role and task, how readiness for independent work is decided, or when training needs updating, and this component organizes the site's existing duty around those decisions.

Medicinal-product GCP also requires personnel familiar with the investigational product. A device investigation adds the functions linked to the device and its use, which the framework groups into five training areas, each protecting one or more CtQ domains.

- Protocol and procedural training: the study objectives, the key eligibility restrictions, the visit windows that matter for endpoints, PDs, and the decision points specific to the study.

- Device training: the investigational system, its instrumentation and implant components, preparation and handling, and any constraint the study places on the workflow.

- Operative and perioperative documentation: intraoperative records, component reconciliation, capture of implant lot and Unique Device Identification (UDI), documentation of substitutions and deviations, and chain of custody.

- Assessment training: radiographic acquisition standards and imaging timing, ROM measurement, and PROM administration where these bear on an endpoint.

- Safety reporting: recognition and documentation of AEs and DDs, and the escalation pathway for clinically important findings.

Each training record identifies the person and training area, the date and trainer, and the version of the controlled document used. After a revision of the instructions for use (IFU) or an amended CIP, the site can therefore identify everyone trained on the earlier version.

The site records completion of training separately from release to perform a task. Attendance at a session shows that training took place, but does not establish that the person can perform the task independently. For tasks graded as critical in the delegation record, particularly those involving the device, imaging, or outcome assessment, the site records an explicit release once training is complete. This release activates the competency gate in the delegation record and, for surgeons, provides one input to the authorization decision. A person performing only routine clinical care receives no device-specific training unless the CIP requires it.

The site retrains staff when the work changes through a protocol amendment, revised IFU, new device iteration, or altered workflow. Deviation records and monitoring findings may also indicate a need for retraining, for example after a safety signal or repeated documentation errors. Responding to these findings follows ICH E8(R1), which recommends retraining when issues related to CtQ factors arise during the study. These records support DR10 by linking each function to a defined training requirement and a recorded release. The Device and Study Training Requirements Matrix (DTRM) specifies what each role or task requires, and the Training Record Evaluation and Release (TRE) records each person's training and release (Appendices E and F).

### 4.2.4 Surgeon qualification and continued authorization

Under the MDR and ISO 14155:2026, the sponsor sets the selection criteria and verifies qualification before the investigation starts, while the PI documents the team's qualifications, keeps the team adequate throughout, and ensures that only authorized users handle the device. The sponsor therefore sets the entry threshold through the CIP and its training requirements, and the site documents that each surgeon assigned a study procedure meets it.

A specialty credential establishes a surgeon's general professional standing, while competence in the specific procedure and readiness to use the investigational device require separate consideration. ISO 14155:2026 already distinguishes experience in the field of application from training in the investigational device for the PI (Clause 10.2(b)). A surgeon experienced in a comparable procedure may still be unfamiliar with the technique required for this device. Before the first case, the site therefore assesses how far the sponsor's entry criteria and the surgeon's release on device training establish readiness, and whether anything remains open. The resulting decision places the surgeon in one of four categories: credentialed, conditional, restricted, or not credentialed. Its link to the delegation record ensures that assigned surgical tasks match the current authorization.

Where the risk assessment or expected learning curve calls for closer supervision, the site may add temporary controls proportionate to the surgeon's experience with the procedure and device. These may include proctoring, co-surgery, or an initial assignment to less complex cases, within the requirements of the investigation. The surgeon holds a conditional authorization until the conditions recorded in the decision have been met.

The site reviews qualification at least once a year while the surgeon operates on study participants, and after a protocol amendment, safety signal, cluster of complications, or evidence of drift in performance. Learning curve tracking and complication monitoring under Risk Management supply the evidence from the surgeon's own cases. Each reassessment uses the same four categories, so authorization can be restricted or withdrawn and later restored. Linking observed performance to continued authorization gives the monitoring required by DR3 a route to action (Gap 3). The Surgical Credentialing Record (SCR) records each decision and its evidence, and the RACI carries the resulting authorization into the delegation record (Appendix G).

## 4.3 Pillar 2: Risk Management

The site needs to identify where its own conduct may fail and control those risks before they affect participants or the data. Risk Management provides the site risk record missing in Gap 2 and the monitoring of operator learning called for by Gap 3. It answers DR2 and DR3, together with parts of DR6 and DR8. Because execution can affect any of the twelve CtQ domains, the pillar covers all of them, but includes only risks that threaten a CtQ factor.

The texts examined address risk at device and trial levels. The manufacturer manages device risk under ISO 14971, and ICH E8(R1) and ICH E6(R3) ask the sponsor to manage risks to critical factors through a cycle from identification to review. ISO 14155:2026 adds two sources beyond the use of the device, the clinical procedures required by the CIP beyond routine practice and the conduct of the investigation itself, and assigns both to the sponsor.

The third level concerns the risks created by the way an individual site carries out the protocol, and none of these texts assigns an owner to a separate risk cycle at this level. The sponsor's cycle has to work across centers with different staff and working practices, so it cannot be built around the weaknesses of one center. Most of the structural conditions of surgical device investigations act at the site, and the first signs of device-related complications also appear there, which puts the site in a position to identify and control these local risks.

### 4.3.1 The site risk register

A 2023 survey of 46 Portuguese clinical trial sites found that only nine (19.6%) described a standard tool or document that captured risk analysis at site level<sup>48</sup>. Although limited to one national survey, this was the closest measurement of Gap 2 that we found.

The register records risks arising from the site's own execution of the investigation. Around surgery, these may include incomplete capture of implant identifiers or late documentation of an intraoperative deviation. Later, they may arise from inconsistent image acquisition or follow-up visits outside their windows. The site opens the register before enrolling the first participant so that controls can be put in place before these failures occur. It uses the protocol and the sponsor's information on critical factors and risks to identify, within each CtQ domain, the factors that its own conduct can affect. Each local risk is then linked to the factor it threatens, with its cause and consequence recorded.

The site rates each risk by its impact on the CtQ factor and by how likely it is to occur and remain undetected, following the risk logic of ICH E8(R1). The rating helps keep the register focused enough for the PI to review at each oversight cycle. Each entry also names an owner from the role structure and records the control in place, the residual risk, and the trigger for reassessment. The named owner is responsible for acting on the entry, and the trigger lets a change in local conditions prompt a review before a failure occurs. A risk with possible effects beyond the site goes to the sponsor. The Site Risk Register (SRR) documents this process from identification to reassessment, as DR2 requires, and holds each risk with its CtQ factor and control plan (Appendix H).

### 4.3.2 Operator-dependent risk and the learning curve

In a surgical device investigation, the procedure forms part of the intervention, so the surgeon's technique and decision thresholds can affect protocol adherence, implant traceability, and endpoints across all the cases that surgeon performs. Because the instruments compared in this thesis control operator learning only at entry (Gap 3), the framework monitors it during conduct, specific to the surgeon, procedure, and device, as DR3 requires.

Experience is associated with shorter operative times, fewer complications, and better PROM scores, although studies define proficiency differently, and reported learning periods range from about 12 to 86 cases depending on the procedure and the surgeon<sup>18,50</sup>. The framework therefore sets no fixed case count.

The site tracks each surgeon's cases to assess whether variation is becoming a material risk to its CtQ factors. This follows ICH E8(R1), which calls for periodic review of CtQ factors as new issues arise during a study and risk controls may need adjustment. The learning curve is one such issue at a surgical site. Existing SQA frameworks deal with it mainly at selection<sup>31,33</sup>, while the Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure has no indicator for a surgeon's procedural consistency during a trial<sup>36</sup>.

Before the first case, the site defines the levels of case complexity relevant to the investigation, the operative duration expected for comparable cases, and the support a surgeon should receive early on, such as proctoring, and these definitions form the basis for comparison. The record of each case then includes its complexity and duration, with any unplanned event such as a component substitution or a need for senior support. A slow operation has a different meaning in a complex case and in a routine one, and its interpretation also depends on the surgeon's experience with the device. Each case is therefore compared with the same surgeon's earlier cases at the same level of complexity. This avoids most of the case-mix problem of comparisons between surgeons, although changes in complexity over time still need to be considered.

The site reviews the sequence after the first cases and at set points afterward. A pattern such as repeated substitutions or a cluster of early technical complications prompts an earlier assessment. Signals from complication monitoring are also checked against the case sequence before they are attributed to the device or the operator, so that normal variation is not taken for procedural drift.

The review may confirm stable execution or lead to closer observation, renewed device training, or temporary proctoring. A material or recurrent pattern requires formal escalation, and a material pattern also enters the risk register or corrective and preventive action. If the response would restrict the surgeon's scope, it returns to the credentialing decision under Governance because it changes the authorization to operate. Recording cases as they occur can reveal drift before it appears as an AE or a PD and preserves point-of-care data that cannot be reconstructed later, and so answers part of DR6. The Surgeon Learning Curve Tracking Log (SLCT) holds the case sequence, its basis of comparison, and each review with the resulting decision (Appendix I).

### 4.3.3 Device-specific complication monitoring

The site reviews clinical events for possible relationships with the investigational device, the procedure, or their interaction in use. Monitoring covers AEs and DDs, with particular attention to complications arising from the procedure or device. An early complication whose relationship to the device remains unclear is one example of the point-of-care data that cannot be recovered once lost. This component answers DR8 and part of DR6 within CtQ domain 10, safety surveillance and adverse device event management.

Each event already has a reporting route that begins at the site. ISO 14155:2026 asks the PI to record and categorize every AE and observed DD and to report the serious ones to the sponsor without unjustified delay, and an AE associated with a DD follows both pathways. Under the MDR, the sponsor then reports serious events with a possible causal relationship to the device or the investigation procedure, together with device deficiencies that might have led to one. These requirements deal with each event on its own, and none of the three instruments asks the site to review its events together for a pattern.

Several events with plausible individual explanations may share a cause, such as one implant component or one surgeon. Reviewing them together can reveal that relationship while the site can still act on it. The framework therefore requires the site to review its own events on a schedule it sets at start-up, independent of the sponsor's, and also whenever a serious event or a cluster of events calls for it. This review starts from events that have occurred, while the risk register works on weaknesses that may cause a future failure, such as an implant identifier that may not be captured before the operating room workflow ends.

The site logs each event when it occurs, whether it is first observed in the operating room or at a later clinical contact. The entry refers to the source records and reporting route, allowing the log to index events without repeating their clinical narrative. It also records a preliminary assessment of what the event may relate to, which the investigator's final classification later confirms or replaces. The entry also carries the complication grade under the Clavien-Dindo classification, which ranks a complication by the treatment it required, from grade I, managed without surgical, endoscopic or radiological intervention, to grade V, the death of the patient.<sup>83</sup>

At each review, the site counts events by category and examines their distribution by component and surgeon. Each count is read against the number of procedures performed. Counts of grade I to II and of grade IIIa to V complications are kept apart, so that a rise in complications that needed an intervention or intensive care is not hidden among minor ones. A rate above a threshold set in advance, or a sharp rise since the previous period, constitutes a signal. The review also flags new event categories and events concentrated in one surgeon. The site assesses each signal for its likely cause and directs it to the relevant process. A signal tied to a surgeon goes to the learning curve review, while one that points to the device goes to the sponsor through the deficiency route. A pattern in a local process enters the risk register or corrective and preventive action.

Under ISO 14155:2026, the sponsor considers establishing a CEC to review and classify events from all participating centers by consensus. The local review uses the site's own denominator and triggers. We found no evidence that either level detects a signal first, so the design gives the site a defined point for action without assuming that it detects signals sooner. When a local trigger is met, the site acts, and signals that may matter beyond the site go to the sponsor to inform the CEC's assessment. The Site Complication Monitoring Log (SCML) indexes events and their routes (Appendix J), while the Safety Assessment, Reporting and Tracking Template (SART) follows each safety case from opening to closure (Appendix K). The counts and signals from each periodic review are recorded in the Complication Signal Surveillance Template (CSST) (Appendix L).

## 4.4 Pillar 3: Operational Controls

Critical activities need consistent methods and records that allow the site to reconstruct what happened. Operational Controls define how these activities are carried out and checked, and the pillar answers DR4 and DR7, together with parts of DR6 and DR11. The MDR requires investigation information to be recorded and stored so that it can be accurately reported, interpreted, and verified, and ISO 14155:2026 specifies the requirements for source documents, data, and device accountability at the site.

Some differences between the protocol and its execution are difficult for an outside party to identify in time, because local conduct can drift between sponsor visits and variation in imaging may appear only when images from different time points are compared. The site therefore needs to check its own work during conduct and to control the records used for those checks.

### 4.4.1 Internal verification of site conduct

The site reviews its conduct and documentation at set intervals to identify and correct failures within its own responsibilities, as DR4 requires. The MDR, ISO 14155:2026, and ICH E6(R3) place monitoring with the sponsor, and none requires the site to check its own conduct between visits (Gap 4). Internal verification covers this interval alongside the sponsor's monitoring, which remains independent of the site.

The review focuses on the points where operating room practice meets implant logistics and documentation, and on later imaging and follow-up. Its effort follows the risk to participants and data, applying at the site the principle that risk-based monitoring uses for sponsor oversight, and twelve checks define its scope.

- Participant entry. Consent was signed on the current version before any study procedure, and the PI confirmed eligibility before surgery.

- Delegation and training. Everyone who performed a study task was delegated and trained for it on that date.

- Sterile release to use. Package integrity, expiry, and storage are confirmed before use, and any breach of sterile handling or intraoperative substitution is reconciled.

- Implant traceability and component reconciliation. Lot number, serial number, or UDI agree across the operative note, accountability record, implant labels, and case report form (CRF), and the implanted configuration matches the plan.

- Instrumentation and operating room readiness. The correct implant system and instrument set were available, and any incompatibility or malfunction is documented as a procedural issue or, where applicable, a DD.

- Operative documentation. The operative note contains enough detail to interpret the final configuration and intraoperative decisions, including substitutions, deviations, and complications. The record needs to preserve these details because missing information cannot be reliably reconstructed later.

- Imaging usability and timing. Required imaging was obtained within its window, with the correct views and technical quality, and was repeated when it did not meet those requirements.

- Follow-up execution. Each visit produced the outputs required for surveillance and endpoint assessment.

- Endpoint data agreement. For endpoint-critical fields first recorded outside the CRF, a sample of CRF entries agrees with the source, and discrepancies are corrected and explained.

- Timeliness and closure. Notes, data entry, queries, and corrective actions are completed within the set times. The EMA reflection paper identifies delays in data entry and in the reporting of serious adverse events (SAEs) as possible triggers for targeted monitoring. It does not establish that a backlog predicts broader failure, so the framework treats delay as a signal to investigate.

- External context. The site reviews the safety alerts and field safety notices it receives for the same implant class or procedure and routes those that affect the investigation to the risk register.

- Complication monitoring. Complication reviews took place at the planned intervals, and their signals were followed up. The analysis of the pattern itself remains part of complication monitoring.

Before enrollment begins, the site defines the frequency and sample for each check and assigns a reviewer, together with the trigger for an earlier review. The reviewer should be someone other than the person who performed the work. Where staffing permits, a person separate from daily execution undertakes the review. A small team can share this work with a trained peer or conduct it under documented PI oversight. Each review covers a defined period or responds to a trigger and can include all twelve checks or a selected subset.

For each check, the reviewer records whether the planned control was carried out and whether the record confirms it, then assesses whether the control worked. A negative or uncertain finding leads to an action or a linked record, followed by a check of the correction's effectiveness. Because this review follows the site's own schedule, it also provides the periodic self-inspection required by DR5. Findings that need correction enter the corrective and preventive action process under Performance Oversight. The Internal Monitoring Plan (IMP) defines the scope and schedule (Appendix M), and the Internal Monitoring Checklist (IMC) records each review with its findings and actions (Appendix N).

### 4.4.2 Imaging standardization

Images support decisions throughout a shoulder investigation, from confirming anatomical suitability before surgery to assessing component position at follow-up. The classifications applied to them already have limited reliability, so variation in acquisition adds error that the database cannot distinguish from a real clinical difference. This component controls the variation introduced by the site and answers DR7 for CtQ domain 9, imaging acquisition, interpretation and endpoint control.

Trial-imaging guidance has developed mainly outside orthopedics, where core-imaging and central-reading work specifies acquisition methods, equipment, and site qualification in advance, along with the division between local and central reading<sup>58,62</sup>. Shoulder studies show how sensitive measurements can be to acquisition. In images reconstructed from cadaver scapulae, views only 5 degrees from true anteversion changed the critical shoulder angle by more than 2 degrees<sup>84</sup>. Only 22% of anteroposterior films in routine practice met the standard projection<sup>85</sup>. Simulated changes in scapular position produced ranges of 14 and 17 degrees in arthroplasty inclination measures<sup>86</sup>. With standardized outlet views, the interobserver intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for acromiohumeral distance reached 0.91<sup>87</sup>.

The site applies the sponsor's imaging charter through four elements, beginning with a readiness assessment before activation. It maps the charter's requirements, including views and acquisition windows, onto a workflow its radiology service can follow. Any gaps are recorded, and the assessment is submitted to the sponsor, which retains the activation decision.

The people who acquire and assess images must be qualified and trained for those requirements, with release documented through the training records under Governance. Where a measurement depends on the assessor, agreement between assessors is tested before the first participant. ISO 14155:2026 also makes the PI responsible for maintenance and calibration of equipment used in the investigation, and its fourth edition added calibration to the content of the CIP.

Before the first image is acquired, the site defines who reviews images locally, the criteria used, and how nonconforming images are handled, including when a second reviewer or clinical escalation is needed. Each required image set is logged at acquisition with its timing, technical quality, and retrieval reference. A set that falls short leads to repeat acquisition or a second review while these can still resolve the problem. Central review, when used by the sponsor, requires coordination across sites and remains outside the site's control. The site can therefore claim consistency only for its own review.

The site also schedules imaging within the CIP window at a time when a reproducible position can be obtained, which pain, dressings, or a sling may prevent soon after surgery, although we found no study measuring how often this happens. If a scheduled time proves unsuitable, the site documents the problem and raises it with the sponsor, which can define an acceptable window. The Site Imaging Readiness Record (SIRR) documents the mapping of the charter and the readiness assessment (Appendix O), and the Imaging Quality Log (IQL) records each image set (Appendix P).

### 4.4.3 Eligibility and follow-up records

The decision to enroll and each follow-up visit depend on records made for an individual participant, which protect CtQ domains 2 and 7. The eligibility record also answers the part of DR7 that calls for structured verification of eligibility. In a shoulder investigation, this decision often depends on anatomy interpreted from images and on the planned procedure, and an error at inclusion can become irreversible once the implant is in place.

The site documents each eligibility decision criterion by criterion against the current CIP, identifying the source of the evidence and the date it became available. Consent precedes any assessment that requires it, and the decision is recorded before enrollment so that the record shows what evidence was available at the time. Criteria that depend on imaging interpretation or information from several sources receive a second, independent review. Any unresolved disagreement goes to the PI, and enrollment waits until it is resolved. The investigator then signs the decision, leaving a record that can be reconstructed without relying on undocumented knowledge.

Every person considered for the investigation also enters a screening log under a code, with the screening outcome and the reason for any non-inclusion or withdrawal. The key linking the code to the person's identity is kept separately. ISO 14155:2026 requires each site to log all enrolled subjects under a code linked to their identity and permits the log to include everyone prescreened. At each review, the site reconciles this log with the eligibility records to account for every candidate. The recorded reasons for non-inclusion feed the enrollment quality indicator under Performance Oversight.

The record of each follow-up visit states whether it occurred within its window and which components required by the CIP were completed. It records the result of each component, any exception, and the action taken. A visit counts as usable only when the CtQ assessments for that time point were completed by the specified method and within the window, since attendance alone does not show that these requirements were met. If a visit is late or incomplete, the site records the reason and the effect on the data. Unexpected findings and concerns raised by the participant go to clinical review and, where applicable, the safety route. Performance Oversight then reviews follow-up across participants and review periods.

The Participant Screening and Eligibility Assessment Record (PSEAR) documents each eligibility decision (Appendix Q), while the Subject Identification and Failure Screening Log (SIFSL) records everyone screened (Appendix R). Each follow-up visit is documented in the Participant Follow-up Visit Record (PFVR) (Appendix S).

### 4.4.4 Source documentation and parallel records

The framework uses the site's existing documentary system for source records. ISO 14155:2026 requires traceable source documents with their type and location documented, and ICH E6(R3) asks the investigator to define before the trial what counts as a source record and where it is kept. The site therefore defines the source of each critical datum, whether a clinical record or, where the CIP allows it, the CRF itself, and the instruments refer to these sources, supporting CtQ domain 11 without a separate documentation system.

The operating room is the one setting in which the framework adds a source record, because the data generated there cannot be reliably reconstructed afterward. During each procedure, the site completes an intraoperative checklist and device log. It captures the identifiers of implanted components and the checks of critical steps as they occur, together with deviations or substitutions and the immediate review after surgery. The log is the source for data it captures first. For other information, it refers to the operative note and device accountability record, supporting both without replacing them. The log answers the part of DR6 concerned with intraoperative traceability and deviation capture.

Some instruments assemble information that is not brought together elsewhere, such as a surgeon's case sequence or the analysis of a complication signal, and others document how the site works. Information that already has a defined source remains authoritative there. An instrument that needs participant-level information refers to that source and cites it whenever a value is used for a count or comparison, and if the two records differ, the source takes precedence.

For example, the learning curve log records that a component substitution occurred and refers to the operative note. The complication log records the classification and reporting action without creating a second clinical narrative. If an event is documented differently in two places, the discrepancy becomes a finding for internal verification.

Working within the existing records follows DR11, because a parallel documentation system would add work that the target center could have difficulty sustaining. The Intraoperative Checklist and Device Log (ICDL) holds the record of each procedure (Appendix T).

## 4.5 Pillar 4: Performance Oversight

Performance Oversight uses a small set of indicators to identify a loss of control during the investigation and guide the site's response. The indicators answer DR8, and the corrective and preventive action cycle responds to the absence of a proactive improvement cycle at site level, identified in Gap 5 and expressed in DR5. We based this pillar on the measurement and improvement clauses of the quality management standards used to develop the framework.

We included an indicator when it could help the site recognize a problem while correction was still possible. The six selected indicators monitor processes whose failure can affect participant protection or the validity of the investigation. Enrollment quality, follow-up compliance, safety reporting timeliness, and critical data completeness cover domains 2, 7, 10, and 11, from which Table 3.4 derives DR8. The PD rate covers domain 12 and identifies recurring deviations for the correction cycle of DR5. Surgeon procedural consistency covers domains 5 and 6 by summarizing, for each review period, the learning curve that DR3 asks the site to follow.

The site defines these indicators for its own processes and escalates a trend when it may affect a critical factor of the trial. Their scope differs from that of the quality tolerance limits the sponsor sets for the trial as a whole. Before the first participant is enrolled, the site fixes each indicator's rule and the values that distinguish a process under control from one requiring closer review or action. A rule cannot be changed once its result is known. We found no published site-level threshold for most of these indicators, so the proposed values remain provisional and the site calibrates them as it gains experience across investigations. Because a single site has few cases, each result is reported as a count with its denominator. A percentage is added only when the denominator is large enough to keep it stable.

All six indicators use records already kept within the framework, from eligibility and visit records to safety and learning curve records, so the pillar requires no additional data collection. When an indicator or a finding from internal verification calls for correction, the site investigates the cause and checks whether the corrective action worked. At close-out, the experience from these actions informs changes to the site's standing procedures. The Performance Indicator Record (PIR) records each indicator's rule and result, together with the decision made in each review period (Appendix U).

### 4.5.1 Enrollment quality

Enrollment quality measures whether each inclusion was correct and supported by evidence available before the decision, which places the indicator in CtQ domain 2. It uses the eligibility records kept under Operational Controls, while recruitment volume is counted separately in the screening log.

The denominator includes every inclusion decision made during the review period because, with few participants enrolled at a single site, sampling would leave most decisions unreviewed. The site counts inclusions for which every critical criterion was evidenced before the decision, along with eligibility errors or clarifications found after enrollment and important eligibility deviations. The review also flags criteria that were ambiguous across several decisions and is conducted more frequently during the first cases and after a major protocol amendment.

The process is under control when every critical criterion was evidenced before every decision. Evidence completed after the decision but before surgery requires closer review. Any eligibility error found after enrollment or any important eligibility deviation requires action. When uncertainty recurs or concentrates in one criterion or assessor, the site responds with clarification and training, involving the sponsor if the criterion itself is unclear.

### 4.5.2 Protocol deviation rate

The PD rate addresses CtQ domain 12 by showing where deviations occur, their importance, and whether they recur or concentrate in a particular area. A single percentage could conceal a few events in consent or safety reporting that matter more than many minor deviations, so the site reports the indicator by area, each with its own denominator. The PI applies the sponsor's criteria for classifying deviations as important, which ICH E6(R3) describes as deviations that may significantly affect the reliability of trial data or a participant's rights, safety, or well-being.

The indicator uses the deviation record kept under the CIP's policy, whether the site or the sponsor holds it. Each deviation is entered when it is found, including during surgery. Procedural deviations are counted per procedure and visit-window deviations per expected visit, with a corresponding denominator for each other area. Recurrence can indicate a failing process, while concentration helps locate it in the work of a particular surgeon or assessor, a type of visit, or a phase of the investigation.

Important deviations receive prompt case review according to their urgency, and the site reviews trends in minor deviations at its oversight meetings. The process is under control when no important deviation has occurred and minor deviations remain below the number set in advance for each area. Several minor deviations in one area, or a minor type recurring from the previous period, require closer review, and any important deviation requires action. If an important deviation recurs or deviations form a cluster within one area, according to the definition set in advance, the site investigates the underlying process. A correction requiring more than local retraining or closer oversight enters corrective and preventive action or goes to the sponsor.

### 4.5.3 Follow-up compliance

Follow-up compliance measures whether the schedule produces usable data through visits that take place on time and include the required assessments. It covers CtQ domain 7 and uses the visit records and their definition of a usable visit to assess the site's part in the follow-up that the MDR asks the sponsor to complete.

For each review period, the site reports four counts, each with its own denominator:

Visits completed within their window, out of the visits expected.

Visits containing every component required by the CIP, out of the visits completed.

Visits missing a component needed for an endpoint, out of the visits that required that component.

Participants whose follow-up could not be obtained despite documented efforts, out of the participants due for follow-up.

The reason recorded at each visit allows the reviewer to attribute the failure to its main cause, whether it concerns the participant, the site's process, or the protocol design. This distinction helps the site choose an appropriate response and avoids automatically treating retention problems as failures of site execution. The reviewer also checks how the components were obtained when that check was planned in advance or when omissions recur. Requiring a second person to check every visit would be difficult to sustain at the target center.

The process is under control when the share of visits completed within window remains at or above the value set in advance and no component needed for an endpoint is missing. Closer review is required when this share falls between that value and a second, lower predefined value, or when one visit lacks a component needed for an endpoint. Action is required if the share falls below the lower value, two or more visits lack such a component, or a participant is lost despite documented efforts. Repeated omission of a component that no endpoint depends on also requires closer review.

The site reviews trends early enough for reminders, changes in scheduling or visit preparation, and retraining to help protect later visits. A cause related to protocol design goes to the sponsor. The Follow-up Compliance Review (FUCR) records each period's review and the causes assigned to the failures (Appendix V).

### 4.5.4 Safety reporting timeliness

Safety reporting timeliness measures whether each reportable event reaches the sponsor within the interval set by the CIP, with a complete first report and subsequent follow-up information. It covers CtQ domain 10 by checking whether the reporting process worked for each event. Complication monitoring examines what the same events show when reviewed together.

The indicator covers SAEs and DDs that could have led to a SADE. The MDR asks the sponsor to have an agreement under which the investigator reports these events in a timely manner, without specifying an interval. ISO 14155:2026 leaves the reporting details to the CIP, which may also reduce AE recording or reporting when the reduction is justified and approved in advance by the ethics committee. The indicator therefore uses the CIP's reporting window and scope. The sponsor's reports to the authorities remain outside its scope.

For each reportable event, the site uses the SART to check whether the initial notification reached the sponsor within the CIP interval, measured from when the site became aware of the event, and whether the first report was complete. It also checks whether follow-up information reached the sponsor on time and records cases still open at the end of the period. Each count uses the reportable events of that period as its denominator. If a report is late or incomplete, the reviewer identifies where the delay arose, from event recognition and internal escalation through the investigator's assessment, access to documentation, and transmission. The site directs the correction at that stage, so repeated delays in recognizing an event, for example, lead to retraining in safety reporting.

The CIP interval determines the threshold for action, so the site does not set a separate value. The process is under control when every initial notification was timely and every first report was complete. A first report requiring correction or late follow-up information requires closer review. Any late initial notification or case remaining open beyond the interval allowed by the CIP requires action and referral to the PI. The site reviews a late initial notification as soon as it is found and enters it in the deviation record, applying the sponsor's criteria to determine whether it is important. Trends are reviewed at the complication review, at the frequency the site has set. Delays recurring at one stage lead to a change in that stage.

### 4.5.5 Surgeon procedural consistency

For each surgeon and review period, this indicator summarizes whether delivery of the investigational procedure is stable and aligned with the CIP. It covers operator dependence in CtQ domains 5 and 6 and gives Performance Oversight a periodic reading of the learning curve required by DR3, drawn from the learning curve log, the intraoperative records, and the complication review.

Each of these signals has more than one possible cause, so the site considers them together against the surgeon's own earlier cases and treats no single one as proof of poor performance.

The indicator does not rank surgeons, since a comparison would require adjustment for case mix, including previous shoulder surgery, glenoid retroversion, younger age, rheumatoid arthritis, and implant size<sup>88</sup>. It would also have to account for clustering among participants treated by the same surgeon<sup>65–67</sup>, which the small number of cases at a single site seldom allows. These signals therefore prompt a structured clinical review and do not constitute a statistical test.

Reviews are more frequent during a surgeon's first cases and after the introduction of a new device, technique, or indication. The process is under control when support followed the plan and any substitution or deviation was isolated and had a non-technical cause. Closer review is required for a signal with an unclear cause, an operation outside the expected duration for its complexity, or a gap in the documentation of implant position. Action is required if the same signal occurs twice in one period, if the device or technique is a suspected cause of an early complication, or if operative duration falls outside the expected range at two reviews. The site then assesses the surgeon's case sequence in the learning curve log to decide the response. If authorization may need to change, the matter returns to the credentialing decision.

### 4.5.6 Critical data completeness

Critical data completeness addresses CtQ domain 11 by measuring whether the data needed for the endpoints and safety analysis are available, current, and in agreement with their source. No clause requires the site to measure how far its records have departed from their source, so this indicator provides that assessment.

Three measures are calculated for each review period from existing records: the share of endpoint-critical fields completed within the interval set for each visit type, the number of open queries with their median age, and the share of operative notes finalized on time. A fourth measure is calculated during each cycle of internal verification. Among endpoint-critical fields first recorded outside the CRF, it reports the share of sampled CRF entries that agree with their source.

The process is under control when all three measures remain within their predefined values and every sampled entry agrees with its source. One measure outside its value, such as a late operative note, requires closer review. Action is required for a shortfall beyond a second, lower value, repeated late notes, or an unresolved discrepancy between source and CRF. It is also required if two measures deteriorate from the previous period in each of two consecutive periods.

### 4.5.7 Closing the loop through corrective and preventive action

An indicator leads to corrective and preventive action when the initial response does not resolve the failure. The same process receives findings from internal verification and from the sponsor's monitoring, audits, or inspections, together with signals from complication monitoring and the learning curve. It answers Gap 5 through DR5 by investigating the causes of the failures detected by the controls and indicators and acting to prevent their recurrence. ISO 14155:2026 already requires the PI to determine the cause of significant noncompliance and put corrective and preventive action in place, with the monitor checking implementation and effectiveness.

The framework extends this work to signals detected before a failure becomes significant or produces an external finding. Thresholds are set before the first participant is enrolled, and oversight meetings and internal verification provide a site-led schedule for reviewing the signals, independent of sponsor visits. Where the center already conducts morbidity and mortality reviews, complications are also discussed there so that the clinical assessment of an event informs the review of the underlying process, and vice versa.

We adapted the corrective action steps in ISO 13485:2016 for use at the site. The site reviews the problem and determines its cause before deciding whether action is needed to prevent recurrence. Where action is needed, the site plans and documents it, checks that it does not compromise CIP compliance or participant safety, and reviews its effectiveness. When an indicator prompted the action, effectiveness is assessed by whether that indicator returns under control in subsequent reviews. An action closes only when the review shows that it was effective or when a documented limitation explains why its effect cannot yet be judged. Preventive action follows the same steps for a failure that has not yet occurred, such as a weakness identified in one process that may also exist in a similar one.

If the investigation of a failure reveals a weakness in a process, the site records the resulting risk in the risk register with a control and a review trigger. An indicator or review then follows that trigger, and a further signal can initiate another corrective action. Findings from correction therefore inform the next cycle of risk management. The Corrective and Preventive Action Log (CAPA) records each action and its cause, with the effectiveness review and closure (Appendix W).

## 4.6 The quality lifecycle

Current guidance asks that quality be planned before a study begins and managed throughout its lifecycle<sup>2,3</sup>. We applied this principle to the site's work by assigning each instrument to the phase in which it can have most effect, as DR9 requires. Timing matters most in a surgical investigation, because an error made during surgery cannot be corrected once the implant is fixed, and data generated at the point of care, such as the identifiers of an implanted component, are lost if they are not recorded when they arise (DR6).

The lifecycle sets out when each instrument is prepared and when it is used. It complements the milestone reviews, which identify points for decisions, and the review schedules, which specify how often each control runs. The site's work is organized into seven phases, each covering the CtQ domains relevant to that phase, with a safety stream across the phases of conduct.

### 4.6.1 Phases, gates, and the safety stream

Preparation includes feasibility and start-up, conduct includes enrollment, surgery, and follow-up, and closure includes close-out and continuous improvement (Figure 4.1). During feasibility, the site assesses whether it can conduct the investigation. This assessment informs the sponsor's selection and covers CtQ domain 1. Start-up establishes the four pillars before participant data are collected, including training and credentialing under domain 6 and arrangements for receiving and storing the device. Enrollment covers participant selection and consent, domains 2 and 3, while surgery covers device management and procedural delivery, domains 4 and 5. Follow-up covers domains 7, 8, and 9, from retention to outcome assessment and imaging. Data capture and deviation management, domains 11 and 12, continue through every phase.

Preparation and closure proceed at site level, with the PI confirming each phase's exit condition at a milestone review before the next phase begins. During conduct, participants progress at different times: one may be in screening while another is already in follow-up. Each conduct phase therefore opens for the site with its first case and closes with its last. At the end of start-up, the site confirms its readiness to enroll and records the implementation level it will use.

Safety surveillance covers the period from the first enrolled participant to the last follow-up assessment and protects CtQ domain 10. It operates within the applicable external reporting intervals and connects events across phases, since a complication observed during follow-up may arise from a decision made during surgery. The site reviews safety cases at the frequency it sets at start-up, within the reporting intervals of the CIP, and keeps the record of each implanted unit current throughout the participant's follow-up.

At closure, the site can assess what the investigation has taught it about its own conduct. Corrective and preventive action has already been running since the first case, and preventive action can correct a weakness in other ongoing investigations as soon as it is identified. The closing phase brings these findings and the risk register together to revise the site's standing procedures. Recording the changes allows later teams to use the lessons even when the people involved have left, as DR10 requires. The Post-Investigation Debrief and Lessons Record (PIDLR) documents the debrief and its lessons (Appendix X), and the revised procedures provide the starting point for the next investigation.

### 4.6.2 Where each instrument acts

Fifteen of the twenty-four instruments act during start-up (Figure 4.2). Although this phase produces almost no participant data, it establishes the conditions under which the subsequent data will be collected. Training and credentialing decisions are completed, the risk register is opened, and imaging readiness is assessed. The site also fixes the rules for most other instruments, including criticality grades and indicator thresholds, before enrolling the first participant. Participant records open in the phase where their data arise, while indicators and complication reviews become more informative as cases accumulate. Milestone reviews extend across every phase, and the debrief is used only in the final phase, continuous improvement.

Within start-up, preparation follows the dependencies between instruments. Training requirements and criticality grades must be defined before anyone is released for a task, and release on device training must precede the credentialing decision that uses it. The site also sets the plan for internal verification before the first review.

The framework specifies four timing requirements that ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026 and the MDR do not impose. For Gap 2, the risk register opens before the first participant, while the site can still assess local risks before exposure occurs. For Gap 3, the learning curve log has a defined basis of comparison before the first case and begins with each surgeon's first procedure, including surgeons who join during conduct. Internal verification has its scope and schedule set before conduct, either in its plan or on its checklist, so the site can review its work independently of sponsor visits (Gap 4). Finally, the rule for each indicator in use is fixed before the first participant, allowing a signal to initiate corrective action before an external finding does (Gap 5).

The evidence provides some support for attending to particular phases. Surgical complications may cluster early in a surgeon's learning curve<sup>20</sup>, and loss to follow-up accumulates over later years<sup>22</sup>. These findings do not establish that sites using controls organized by phase perform better than sites responding to sponsor monitoring. The phase structure therefore remains a design hypothesis, consistent with the direction in ICH E8(R1) to focus quality resources on critical activities and risks.

## 4.7 The scalability model

We designed the framework for a nonhospital organization, whose conditions define the minimum implementation level. However, hospitals hosted 239 of the 347 site records in the registry sample (68.9%), so the framework also needs to accommodate the settings in which most of these investigations take place. The scalability model answers DR12 by adapting implementation to the center's resources and the risk of the investigation while maintaining a complete and consistent documentation trail. Proportionality to risk follows ICH E6(R3), while adapting to resources is a choice of this design, acceptable only while the required evidence remains available at every level.

The number of instruments in use varies between levels, while each instrument retains the same form. The three levels follow the organizational environments in Table 3.3 and the support the framework can reasonably assume in each. Where the site does not use a separate instrument, an existing record takes on its control function. Table 4.2 identifies this proxy for each instrument omitted from a minimum set. The recommended minimum is twelve instruments for a nonhospital organization, eighteen for a nonacademic hospital, and all twenty-four for an academic organization. A site with capacity to use more should do so, and investigation-specific risks may require additions to its minimum set.

At the minimum level, the clinical staff also perform the research work, without the support of a hospital system or university. The twelve instruments of this level (Table 4.2) cover the site's existing duties and provide at least one response to each gap.

In these small teams, one person may hold several of the six functions, but each function has a named substitute so that no CtQ function depends on one individual. Where the separation required by DR10 cannot be fully achieved, the operational constraint of DR11 takes precedence. Even so, the person reviewing work must be different from the person who performed it, and reviews must follow the frequency the site fixed at start-up, including the review of safety cases.

A nonacademic hospital provides services such as radiology and controlled storage, with more people available to share review duties. Its recommended minimum rises to eighteen instruments. The six additions support work that requires time for analysis and planning: the learning curve log, the complication log and its separate signal review, the internal monitoring plan, the performance indicator record, and the corrective action log. With these additions, each gap in Table 3.1 has the full response specified by the framework.

In an academic organization, the hospital also has university support and may already have a research unit and an institutional quality system. All twenty-four instruments are recommended at this level. The six additions give larger teams a more formal structure through the graded delegation matrix, milestone review log, training requirements matrix, and records of imaging readiness, screening, and visits. Where the institution already keeps training records or a corrective action system, the instruments refer to it, as DR11 requires. They add the information needed to follow the named surgeon, device, and investigation where the institutional system does not already provide it. The services available from clinical trial units nevertheless vary<sup>41,42</sup>.

Organizational category alone does not establish research capacity. Even hospitals and academic centers report shortages of staff and infrastructure. The levels therefore provide an initial assumption about support that the site must check against its actual conditions. A site lacking the support assumed for its category uses the set of the level below, while a nonhospital organization with its own research capacity may use a higher level. The PI records the choice at the review that closes start-up and revisits it when capacity changes, for example after the departure of the coordinator.

Within each level, the site adds instruments for domains rated as high risk in the risk register. Strong operator dependence, such as that associated with a new implant design or technique, calls for the learning curve log. Limited classification reliability calls for the imaging and training controls of the higher levels, while prolonged follow-up calls for closer review of retention. A nonhospital site investigating a new implant design would therefore add the learning curve log to its twelve instruments. If the required controls exceed its capacity, the site must obtain support from the sponsor, such as proctoring, or decline the investigation at feasibility.

Every implementation level must retain the evidence needed to show that the investigation was conducted as required. Because the MDR requires Member States to inspect investigation sites, records must remain complete, consistent, current, and retrievable even at the minimum level. The records required by ISO 14155:2026 and the MDR remain necessary across implementation levels and degrees of risk, including consent and source documents, delegation and training, device accountability, deviations, and adverse events. The framework specifies where these records are kept, and the instruments in use retain their operating rules: the risk register covers all twelve domains, and the intraoperative record is completed during the procedure.

## 4.8 The operational instrument set

The regulatory texts examined do not require the site to operate a quality system (Gap 1). Each part of the framework therefore has to be justified through its origin, and Table 4.3 does so for the individual instruments. Adoption also requires a reason to use the instruments together. A site already keeps many records, yet a recurring problem can remain unnoticed within them. For example, an unplanned component substitution may appear in both the operative note and the device accountability record and recur in later cases before anyone reviews the records together. The framework must therefore connect the records to decisions, with a control for each domain and a stated reason for including each instrument.

The instruments work at three levels (Figure 4.3). At the event or person level, the ICDL records one procedure and the TRE records one person's training and release. At the next level, the site reviews several records together, using instruments such as the learning curve log for a surgeon or the follow-up review for a period. These reviews inform decisions about site conduct, documented at the third level in instruments such as the delegation matrix and risk register. A resulting decision then sets a condition against which subsequent work is checked.

The component substitution illustrates how these levels connect in a site using the full set. The event is recorded in the ICDL during surgery, then reviewed alongside the surgeon's other cases in the learning curve log and reported in the procedural consistency indicator for that period. If the review confirms a pattern, the PI records a decision in the POT. That decision may change the surgeon's authorization to require proctoring, which is documented in the credentialing record and checked in the ICDL for subsequent procedures. The sequence begins under Operational Controls, is reviewed under Risk Management and Performance Oversight, and leads to a decision under Governance. We designed the instruments after defining the functions of all four pillars so that these exchanges could be built into the set.

# 5. Discussion

## 5.1 Contribution of the thesis

The main contribution of this thesis is a quality framework that the investigational site runs for itself during conduct, derived from ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026, and MDR Annex XV<sup>3,6,8</sup> and fitted to the site with the least support. Conducting an investigation well and showing that the site controlled its conduct are separate achievements. Under the older arrangement the sponsor assured quality through monitoring visits and source data verification, together with audit, and held the evidence that the conduct had been controlled, so the site needed no account of its own processes. ICH E6(R3) now asks the investigator to put processes in place and to make sure that qualified staff and adequate resources support quality at the site<sup>3</sup>, without saying what the site should produce to show it. The same guideline treats the essential records as the means by which the conduct of the investigator is evaluated, by the sponsor and in inspections (ICH E6(R3), Appendix C, C.1.3)<sup>3</sup>, so a site that shares responsibility for quality needs records through which it can show how it carries its share. The framework gives the site the means to do so, and the thesis delivered it as a design, with each component traced to the analysis that produced it.

The framework asks the site to act as a quality agent in its own right. The sponsor still designs the investigation and decides what is measured, and the site still executes the protocol. In a multicenter investigation, however, the sponsor cannot assess the specific risks of each site and build controls for each of them. Some of the most consequential risks are local, such as the way a team captures implant identifiers or positions a shoulder for imaging, together with each surgeon's progress through the first cases, and the site is usually the first to see them as they arise. Every quality activity above it, from the sponsor's planning to central monitoring, rests on the conduct the site performs, and neither planning nor monitoring can recover data that were badly produced at the base. A site without a quality structure depends on what the sponsor asks and corrects its errors after someone else detects them, while a site with one can anticipate part of its failures and prevent them. Responsibility in this sense is a proactive role, in which the site understands the weight of its actions and keeps improving them, while final responsibility for the investigation stays where the regulations place it. We found no published framework that gives the site this role in device investigations, although the successive revisions of ICH guidance point toward sites being asked to show it.

The plan described a comparison of texts and the requirements drawn from it, followed by a framework that answered them, but the design needed more than that. Placing the controls against the course of an investigation showed that the moment of a control matters as much as its content, because an error made while an implant is fixed cannot be corrected afterward, and the lifecycle gave form to this need, already stated in DR9. The registry analysis then showed that nonhospital organizations hosted almost a third of the site records, so the framework had to work first where it could assume the least support. The scalability model came after the instruments, by adjusting the set already designed to meet DR12. The third element was not anticipated, because a set of records seemed enough until it became clear that a problem can sit unnoticed in several records that nobody reads together. The instruments were therefore built to pass information from the record of one event to a decision about how the site works.

Gregor and Hevner classify a design contribution by how mature its problem and its solution are, and they call the transfer of a known solution to a new problem an exaptation<sup>89</sup>. Most of the framework is a contribution of this kind. A documented quality system is an established solution, which the manufacturer and the medical laboratory already run under their own standards<sup>7,56,57</sup>, and the problem is recent, since the revised guidelines give the site a larger share of the responsibility for quality without a structure to carry it. For four of the five gaps, the framework takes a principle that the texts state for the sponsor, such as the risk management cycle or continuous improvement, and builds the site-level instrument that the texts leave out. A regulator could derive each of these four duties from duties the site already holds, so in these areas the framework adds organization to obligations that already exist.

Observation of the surgeon during conduct, which answers the third gap, is also an exaptation, since surgical methodology already follows operator learning, although its derivation is of another kind. ISO 14155:2026 allows the sponsor to justify a roll-in phase in the CIP, and the surgical trials literature controls the operator at entry, but none of the texts we compared gives the site a role once the investigation has started. Since no clause asks for this observation, its justification rests on an argument. When the CtQ logic required by the texts is applied to an intervention delivered by a person whose execution changes over time, operator learning becomes a threat to data validity. A qualification at entry cannot control it, because it records a state at one moment while learning unfolds as a trajectory.

## 5.2 Position in the literature

Risk-based quality management was developed for the party that designs and oversees a trial, in medicines and in devices<sup>1,27,28</sup>, and sites meet it through the sponsor, as a monitoring plan that arrives with the protocol<sup>49</sup>. Few of the sites surveyed in Portugal held a risk tool of their own, and the identification of CtQ factors is among the least used practices even among sponsors<sup>12,48</sup>. The Site Risk Register (SRR) applies the risk cycle of the sponsor to the risks created by the site's own execution, and it starts from the CIP so that it can be built even where the sponsor never formalized its critical factors. In those investigations the SRR may be the first place where anyone applies CtQ reasoning to local processes (Table 5.1).

Surgical methodology controls operator learning mainly before the trial starts, through credentialing and standardization, and the more recent consensus frameworks that add monitoring during the trial specify it for the trial team<sup>29,31,32</sup>. The IDEAL recommendations already proposed statistical process control to follow learning curves<sup>16</sup>, but they addressed the evaluation of an innovation by researchers, so the method did not reach the site as a quality control. The Surgeon Learning Curve Tracking Log (SLCT) keeps each surgeon's case sequence during conduct and compares each case with the same surgeon's earlier cases of the same complexity, so that a pattern leads to the credentialing decision. It uses a structured clinical review in place of formal process control, because a single site rarely has enough cases to set control limits.

Measures of site performance are built mainly to compare sites, and the validated Clinical Trial Site Performance Measure serves benchmarking across networks<sup>36</sup>. It includes consent, which our framework covers only through the eligibility record, so a site whose register rates consent as a high risk should add a measure for it. Our indicators are tied to the CtQ factors of one investigation and tell the site that one of its processes has started to drift, without comparing sites or surgeons. Central monitoring and the ranking of sites by a sponsor improved data quality at many of the sites they flagged<sup>37,38</sup>, but in both cases the signal reached the site from outside. Whether a signal produced inside the site, earlier in the conduct, has a comparable effect is one of the expected effects of the framework (Table 5.3).

The few quality structures that sites hold today depend on resources, from an annual self-assessment of research infrastructure and ISO 9001 certification to the trial units of academic institutions<sup>39–42</sup>. A nonhospital organization has no trial unit or research office behind it. Some independent practices receive standard procedures and staff from site management organizations or site networks, but this support is built for trials in general, and none of these structures carries the CtQ content of a device investigation. The framework therefore sets its minimum level in the nonhospital organization and asks for no software or dedicated staff, so that the instruments carry part of the quality function that an academic hospital receives from its trial unit.

## 5.3 Putting the framework to work

### 5.3.1 A route through the lifecycle

A site starts the instruments in the order set by the lifecycle and by the dependencies between them, which differs from the order by pillar used to describe them. During feasibility the site reads the CIP with whatever the sponsor provides on critical factors and risks and checks each CtQ domain against its own capacity, taking a first estimate of its implementation level from its organizational category. When the investigation requires controls beyond what it can run, the site asks the sponsor for support, such as proctoring, or declines, and its answer informs the judgment of the sponsor on the site under Clause 6.8 of ISO 14155:2026.

Start-up follows a fixed order, because each step needs the one before it. The role structure comes first, since no task can be assigned until each function has a holder and a substitute, and when the PI also operates, the site names at this point the person who will decide on the PI's own authorization. Training and release come next, followed by the credentialing of each surgeon, and only then do delegations become active, so nobody is delegated a task before being released for it. The SRR opens before the first participant and decides which optional instruments the site adds to its minimum set. Every rule is then fixed before any data exist, from the basis of comparison of the SLCT to the thresholds of the indicators and the scope of internal verification, and a later change applies only to future periods.

Once participants are enrolled, only five records have to be completed at the clinical moment, the PSEAR, IQL, ICDL, SART and PFVR, and every other instrument reads them. The first reviews come earlier than their routine schedule, after the first inclusions and after each surgeon's first cases, so that problems of a new workflow are found while few participants have been exposed to them. At close-out the site reconciles device accountability and safety cases and closes each corrective action with an effectiveness review or a documented limitation, and the PIDLR keeps the lessons that pass to the next investigation.

### 5.3.2 The review cycle

The instruments need a review cycle, but a single rhythm for all of them would not match the work they record, and a small site that tried to review twenty-four instruments every month would barely finish one round before the next began. The framework therefore fixes a rhythm only where the risk depends on timing and leaves the rest to the site, which knows its volume of cases and the people available. The SLCT is reviewed at set points in each surgeon's sequence, for example after the fifth and the tenth case, because learning shows in the early cases whatever the calendar says, and safety cases are followed within the intervals the CIP sets for reporting. For the other instruments the site chooses at start-up a monthly, quarterly or yearly review, according to how much each instrument records and how fast its content changes. A serious adverse device effect or a cluster of complications starts a review at once, and so does a protocol amendment or the departure of a key person.

The oversight meeting reads the instruments in an order that lets each finding inform the next. Safety comes first, because the operative times of each surgeon can only be read once the meeting knows which cases had complications, and the indicators and internal verification follow, with the SRR closing the agenda. The PI records each decision in the POT, and a decision that restricts a surgeon returns to the credentialing and delegation records, where the ICDL checks it at the surgeon's next case.

The minimum level omits the SLCT and, with it, the instruments that review complication signals and indicators, so the meeting itself does their work. It reads the substitutions recorded in the ICDL and reviews the SART cases together, and it checks follow-up through the FUCR. The minimum level therefore depends on this recurring meeting more than on any single instrument, and when meetings are missed, the synthesis that the higher levels spread across several instruments disappears without any record showing that it is missing.

### 5.3.4 What the framework is expected to change

Each instrument takes part in a chain that starts with a record made at the clinical moment and read together with other records. The synthesis supports a decision, and the decision becomes the rule that the next record is checked against, so the value of the framework depends on that chain closing in time, which depends on the people who run it as much as on the design. The framework does not fix the conditions under which an effect counts as absent, because a site needs time to learn the instruments before their signals mean much. In the target center the indicators therefore work mainly as a scheduled review of events against rules fixed in advance, and their value comes from that schedule and those rules more than from any statistical property.

### 5.3.5 Documentation and conduct

A site can record its conduct impeccably and still conduct itself badly, and nothing in this thesis settles whether documenting a practice improves it. The claim we can defend is narrower, since controls written for a specific activity leave fewer gaps open, so the framework acts on the probability that a failure goes undetected and says nothing directly about the quality of the conduct itself. This rests on a premise we state as such, namely that many failures in clinical investigations come from lapses and competing demands on the same person, or from the lack of a tool that makes an error visible in time. Deliberate misconduct is outside the reach of the framework, since a person who intends to misrepresent conduct can misrepresent the record as well. For the other failures, diligence cannot act on what nobody sees, and an instrument that brings a failure into view turns it into something a person can respond to.

If the framework changed nothing in conduct and its only effect were a complete and traceable record of what the site did, that outcome would still be better than the same conduct leaving no record. The record allows a case to be reconstructed years later and supports the manufacturer whose post-market obligations depend on it, although it protects a participant only when someone reviews it and acts in time.

Observation may also change behavior, since a surgeon or a nurse who knows that the work is reviewed may attend to it more closely and learn from the review which elements matter. The same pressure can lead people to satisfy the fields while the practice stays as it was. The design limits that risk, because its instruments record decisions and identifiers with their dates, so a device identifier is captured or missing whatever attention is paid to the form, and because most records are read by someone other than their author.

## 5.4 Scaling and transfer

Many of the organizations the framework was designed for have no structure dedicated to research, and a private site is expected to assemble standard operating procedures and trained staff, together with the infrastructure research requires, before it enters the field<sup>43</sup>. We assume, without data from these organizations, that few of them employ people whose only work is clinical trials, so the same people hold several functions. The surgeon is often the PI, and a nurse may also carry out the physical assessments that a physiotherapist would perform in a hospital. Some activities, such as imaging or specialized assessments, may be contracted outside the practice, so the site has to oversee work that it does not perform itself. A framework that assumed a trial unit and dedicated staff would not fit this setting, and ICH E6(R3) itself asks for quality management proportionate to risk, without unnecessary complexity. The scalability model tries to keep the essential controls in place with fewer people and fewer instruments, and to let a site add instruments as its capacity or the risk of its investigation grows.

### 5.4.1 The minimum level and its trade-offs

At the minimum level a site runs twelve instruments, and these cover the core of the framework. Every gap has at least one response and every record that ISO 14155:2026 and the MDR require is kept, while four of the five records made at the clinical moment remain as instruments, with the CRF serving as the visit record. Each function has a named holder and a substitute, and the oversight meeting brings the records together. Inspections assess conduct through the essential records (ICH E6(R3), Appendix C, C.1.3)<sup>3</sup>, and none of the twenty-four instruments is required by ICH E6(R3), ISO 14155:2026 or the MDR, so a site at the minimum level that keeps every required record carries no regulatory exposure beyond that of a site running the full set.

In exchange, the meeting at the minimum level reviews less often than dedicated instruments would, so a signal may be noticed later, and one person may hold several functions, which weakens the separation between execution and oversight required by DR10. The proxies carry a less visible cost, since the CRF and the sponsor's screening log sit in systems the sponsor controls and the site's access to them may end after close-out, leaving part of the site's history in records it will not keep. A site at the minimum level should therefore keep a copy of these records under its own control at close-out, or use the PFVR from the start when follow-up lasts several years.

In practice the minimum also depends on the stage of the investigation. The SRR adds the SLCT for a new implant design or technique, and in a premarket investigation the device is new to every surgeon, so the rule will almost always apply and the effective minimum becomes thirteen instruments. The minimum of twelve applies mainly to investigations of devices already in the surgeon's use, such as post-market clinical follow-up. The PI who records the implementation level also carries part of the work each level adds, so a site has a reason to choose the lowest level its category allows, and the reasoning recorded at start-up is what a sponsor or an inspector would ask to see.

### 5.4.2 Instruments that outlive the investigation

The framework is described for a single investigation, while many sites conduct several, either at once or one after another. Some instruments belong to the site and are kept across investigations, and some keep a site structure that each investigation fills with its own content. The rest are opened again for every investigation (Table 5.4).

The instruments opened for each investigation are mostly records of participants and procedures, or of visits, so their effort grows with the number of cases, which is the work a site budget prices per participant and per procedure. Setting up the structure is work of another kind, concentrated in the instruments the site keeps or reuses. Fifteen instruments act during start-up, when sponsors measure how quickly a site is activated, and the cost of setting them up falls mostly on the first investigation. A site with a steady portfolio spreads that cost and, as it refines the instruments, spends less on running them in each new investigation, whereas a site that takes one investigation every few years carries most of the cost each time.

A site running investigations at the same time needs one role structure and one training history, and also one corrective action log and one oversight meeting for all of them, while the participant records remain separate for each investigation. Safety reporting timeliness and critical data completeness measure site processes shared by all of its investigations, as does the deviation rate by area, so the site can pool their counts to enlarge the small denominators of a single investigation. Surgeon consistency and complication patterns depend on the device and cannot be pooled across different devices, and because each sponsor's data are confidential, pooled counts serve only the site's internal review. Because a surgeon's learning with a device continues across investigations, the SLCT of a later investigation should start from the earlier cases where the site holds them.

### 5.4.3 Transfer to other specialties

Neither the MDR nor ISO 14155:2026 distinguishes a shoulder device from a knee or hip device, so the obligations behind the instruments apply across implant surgery and make transfer plausible, although they do not show it. Within the instruments, anatomical content sits in the complication categories and the imaging requirements, together with the complexity levels of the SLCT, while their structure carries none. Spine surgery shows that specialties also differ in more than content, since one procedure may implant several components, which multiplies the traceability work and the ICDL entries per case. In robot-assisted or navigated arthroplasty part of the execution moves from the surgeon to a system, so the basis of comparison of the SLCT has to separate experience with the procedure from experience with the system. The claim does not reach surgical investigations without an implant, where unit traceability does not arise, or device investigations without surgery, where operator dependence takes another form. Applying the instrument set to the documentation of a completed hip or knee device investigation, and recording which fields had to change, would test the claim at low cost.

### 5.4.4 Transfer to other jurisdictions and editions

The registry places 155 of the 238 organizations in the United States, including 63 of the 68 nonhospital organizations, while the framework was designed under the MDR and ISO 14155:2026, so most of the nonhospital organizations in the sample operate under another regime. The regulatory derivation is unaffected, because it does not depend on the registry, but the question of how far the framework serves these organizations remains open.

Under 21 CFR Part 812, the investigator reports an unanticipated adverse device effect to the sponsor and to the institutional review board within ten working days of first learning of it<sup>11</sup>. A site in the United States therefore has a regulatory interval for the safety reporting indicator, where a European site takes the interval from the CIP. Since the SART already takes its interval from the CIP, it adapts, and what changes are the terms and the regulatory anchors of each instrument. Neither regime prescribes how a site organizes itself, so the structure should transfer once each anchor is mapped to the corresponding provision.

Within the European Union, the version of ISO 14155 with a presumption of conformity under the MDR is still EN ISO 14155:2020+A11:2024<sup>9</sup>, while the 2026 edition represents the current state of the art. Some parts of the framework rest on clauses added in the fourth edition, such as the risk stream for clinical procedures beyond routine practice and the clinical events committee. A site working to the harmonized version can use these parts as state of the art, without the presumption of conformity that the harmonized text carries.

## 5.5 Implications

### 5.5.1 For the investigational site

For the site, the main gain is control of its own conduct while the investigation runs, held in an account that the site can show. That account also has economic and reputational value, and because the organization keeps it, it survives changes of people. When a coordinator leaves or a monitor is replaced, or when a question settled by an earlier monitor comes back, the site still holds the decisions and the reasons behind them.

The work the framework asks of the site can be priced, because research payments from industry are expected to reflect the fair market value of the work performed<sup>92</sup> and site budgets are negotiated item by item with the sponsor. In one review of ten industry-sponsored trials, the final budgets exceeded the sponsors' initial proposals by 59% on average, and the costs of preparing the trial were among the items that changed most<sup>93</sup>. The quality work a site performs for an investigation can therefore enter the budget as an item of its own, within the fair market value of the activity, and a function such as a data quality reviewer can be justified as a budget line in the same way. As the site repeats and refines the instruments, the time they take should fall, while the payment for the activity stays at least the same.

The framework may also make the income of the site more stable. Closer control over assessment and enrollment should lose fewer eligible patients to incomplete records or missed assessments, and each inclusion is paid, while fewer errors mean fewer costs of correction, such as a screen failure caused by an incomplete eligibility record or a missed imaging window. A site that can show how it controls its conduct may also gain standing with the sponsors that select it for later investigations, although we could not test this.

The benefits also reach other parties, since the sponsor gains easier monitoring and more reliable data and the manufacturer relies on the operating room records for traceability. A site deciding where to begin should start with the instruments that protect data that cannot be recovered, meaning the five records made at the clinical moment and the SRR, because the same records protect its revenue. A site that records a weakness also creates a record that a monitor can read, and a finding the site raised against itself is still a finding, although it also shows a site that knows its weaknesses and acts on them. How monitors read such findings is not known, and it will decide whether sites are willing to keep these records.

### 5.5.2 For sponsors and monitors

The framework sits alongside the sponsor's quality system and organizes the processes through which the site conducts each investigation. The sponsor oversees quality across the trial, while the site uses the CIP, with what the sponsor communicates about critical factors and risks, to identify the risks of its own work and set controls in proportion to them (Figure 5.1). The structure of the framework stays with the site across investigations, while its content follows each CIP and the systems the sponsor requires.

Within this interface, the instruments give the evidence for local review. The PI's decision, recorded in the POT, may revise a risk control or open a corrective action, and the records that follow show whether the response worked. A signal that needs the sponsor's assessment goes to the sponsor with its evidence and with any local action already taken, so that the sponsor can read it across sites and return any change to the site's conduct. Required reporting follows its own timelines while the local review continues.

At qualification, Clause 6.8 asks the sponsor to judge the qualifications of the PI and the adequacy of the site, and a site that arrives with its own risk register and oversight records gives documentary evidence on both. At a monitoring visit the same site can hand over its delegation records and corrective action log, with its indicator results, so the monitor reviews a site that has already described its own risks. Risk-based monitoring is adequate only when certain prerequisites at the site are met<sup>46</sup>, and the framework builds some of them. We found no study measuring how much monitoring time this saves.

The two systems can also be aligned more closely than they are today. If the sponsor gave each site, at initiation, a summary of the critical factors and main risks it identified, the SRR and the sponsor's monitoring would address the same targets. Where the CIP allows it, the ICDL can be named as the source of implant identifiers and intraoperative events, so source data verification compares the CRF with a structured record made during the procedure. The site's indicators read at one center the processes that the sponsor's key risk indicators read across the trial, and they could feed central monitoring as long as each datum keeps its source. The clinical events committee that ISO 14155:2026 allows the sponsor to establish addresses the same events as the site's complication review, at a different level, and a local action already taken is information the committee can use.

Only one of 247 randomized surgical trials analyzed learning effects<sup>70</sup>, partly because the case sequence of each surgeon is rarely collected. The SLCT holds that sequence with complexity and duration, so a CIP that planned for it could use the log to examine learning in the main analysis, provided the surgeon's data are protected.

Central monitoring by the sponsor finds problems in the data that reach the CRF, such as an image that does not meet the imaging requirements or a visit outside its window, but it finds them after the data are entered, often when the window to repeat an examination has already closed. The IQL and the ICDL check the same points when the data are produced, so a site can still repeat an image or complete a record while the participant is available. The framework also keeps the site's history when the monitors assigned to a long investigation change.

A sponsor could go further and supply templates and help set up the framework across its sites, as it already supplies the protocol and training. This would move part of the cost of construction to the sponsor, but a site working with three sponsors would then receive three frameworks and run three quality systems at once. The more stable division keeps a structure owned by the site, into which each investigation brings its content and, where the sponsor wishes, its templates, while the operation of the framework and the ownership of its records stay with the site.

### 5.5.3 For standards and regulators

Reading ISO 14155:2026 from the position of the site shows points that a future revision could consider. All three risk streams of Clause 6.2 belong to the sponsor, and no text describes how a site handles the part of those risks that its own execution creates. Clause 9.2.4.5 p) asks monitors to verify that equipment relevant to assessment is maintained and calibrated, yet no clause describes the record through which the site shows this work. These observations come from one position in a standard with a wider purpose, so a revision might reasonably leave them open.

A clause asking a site to keep a risk register would ask for one document and one review cycle, while a requirement for a site-level quality system would ask for much more. Annex XV of the MDR governs the conduct of clinical investigations and says nothing about how a site organizes itself to conduct them, and the harmonization of the fourth edition would be a natural moment to examine that question. Recognition schemes for research sites already exist, since the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency accredits units that run phase I trials against requirements beyond good clinical practice<sup>94</sup>, and the Association for the Accreditation of Human Research Protection Programs accredits the programs through which research organizations protect participants<sup>95</sup>, while ISO 15189:2022 gives the medical laboratory a quality system that can be accredited<sup>57</sup>. None of them addresses the quality of conduct at a site running device investigations, and a framework of the kind proposed here could become the content of a future standard or accreditation for such sites. A standard of this kind could also reduce the fragmentation that frameworks supplied by sponsors create, because a site working for several sponsors would run one structure recognized by all of them. A future revision of ICH E6 or ISO 14155 could, more modestly, give the site an explicit proactive role in quality without moving final responsibility, and the framework shows one form that role could take.

No clause in the present texts requires the records this framework produces, so an inspection under the MDR would not treat their absence as a noncompliance. A site with the framework and a site without it therefore carry the same obligations and differ in what they can show, since only the first can show how it controlled its conduct without reconstructing it from primary documents. If later revisions move to the site part of the quality requirements that now rest on the sponsor, as the direction of ICH E6 suggests, a site with the framework will already hold much of what it is asked for.

### 5.5.4 Ethical implications

The SLCT and the consistency indicator hold performance by surgeon and can lead to a restriction of the cases a surgeon takes in the investigation, so in substance they assess professional performance. What they record, however, is performance with a device or technique that is new to the surgeon, under the conditions of the investigation, and a result below expectation in that setting says nothing about the surgeon's usual practice. Poor practice is a different matter, which belongs to the clinical governance of the organization. A signal recorded in these instruments, or in the complication review, could still follow a surgeon beyond the investigation and harm a career if it were read out of context, so the framework keeps every action that results from these records, from closer observation to a restriction, within the scope of the investigation.

The design codes the surgeon and keeps the key with the PI, which protects the surgeon when other staff, reviewers or patients see the documents by chance, and it fixes before the first case the outcomes that a review can reach, with the reasoning behind any restriction documented. Who may access these records, and for what purpose, also has to be defined before the first case. They are personal data of the professional and have to be handled under the data protection law that applies, which we did not analyze in detail. The indicator does not rank surgeons, since a fair comparison would need an adjustment for case mix and for clustering<sup>65,66</sup> that a single site rarely allows.

In a surgeon-owned practice, the site belongs to the surgeon it observes, and the PI who decides on a restriction may be the surgeon under review or a close colleague who shares the commercial consequence. The same structure affects internal verification, where the reviewer may be the employee of the person reviewed. Every function has a named substitute in the role structure, so when the PI is the surgeon under review, the decision passes to that substitute on a route fixed before the first case.

## 5.6 Evaluation, limitations, and further research

### 5.6.1 The evaluation this thesis performed

The Design Science Research Methodology has six activities, and this thesis reports the first three, which run from problem identification to design and development, together with the last, communication<sup>53</sup>. Demonstration and evaluation are not reported, although the thesis did perform one kind of evaluation. The design-science literature distinguishes evaluation before an artifact is used, which tests whether the design decisions are grounded, from evaluation after its use, which tests whether the artifact works<sup>96</sup>. It also separates formative evaluation, which improves a design while it is still being shaped, from summative evaluation, which judges a finished artifact. A further distinction separates artificial evaluation, carried out away from real use, from naturalistic evaluation in the setting where the artifact will run<sup>97</sup>. Our traceability from each component to a requirement, and from each requirement back to its origin, is an evaluation before use, formative and artificial. It is the expected first step for a design at this stage, because it checks that every part rests on a stated reason before anyone spends resources on implementation. It cannot show that the framework works in a site, because only a naturalistic evaluation can do that, and since we derived the requirements and assigned the components ourselves, the mapping is not independent evidence either. The framework should therefore move next to a naturalistic formative evaluation, in which a site uses the instruments and the design is corrected, and only then to a summative one that judges its effect.

### 5.6.2 Limitations

Some choices of this thesis are delimitations made on purpose, such as the focus on the shoulder and on the European regulatory framework and the choice of a small nonhospital clinic as the design case, and they set the scope of the claims. The limitations that follow each weaken an inference within that scope. The framework has not been implemented, so its expected effects, the economic argument and the estimate of effort remain expectations, and nobody knows yet how much time the instruments take in a small team. The gaps and the design requirements do not depend on any expected effect, so this limitation leaves the derivation intact and bears on the claims about effect, which a feasibility study in one site can begin to test.

The rules and thresholds of the indicators are provisional, because we found no published values for site-level indicators of this kind. The order of the work also shaped the result, since the scalability model was settled after the full set of instruments and the minimum level was obtained by removing instruments and naming proxies for them. A design that started from the minimum level might have produced a smaller set.

Because the literature review was narrative, any statement that a body of work is absent from the field is weak, and the position of this work in the literature rests only on the works we found and read, which a systematic search can test. We reduced the risk by searching the bodies of work most likely to contain a site-level framework and by reporting the closest works we found.

Our evidence on the state of site practice comes from published studies and regulatory guidance, with some expert commentary. We had no access to the internal documents of any site, from its procedures to its audit data, which weakens the claim that the failures the framework addresses are the ones that occur most often. Field research in sites of each organizational category can test that claim and calibrate the framework against observed failures.

We did not consult experts formally during the design. The framework was discussed informally with professionals working in clinical operations, and some of their criticism changed it, but these conversations were neither planned nor recorded, so they cannot count as evidence. The claim that the instruments can be completed as intended therefore rests on the design alone, until a structured content review tests it.

In the registry analysis, one researcher classified each organization from its public self-description, with a second internal pass and no independent assessor. This weakens the assignment of organizations to environments and the reproducibility of the counts in Table 3.3, which a second coder can test. Because the sample is weighted toward the United States, the inference that weakens most is the one from the organizational profile of the sample to European sites, and a registry analysis based on European data, which Eudamed did not provide, would test it.

### 5.6.3 Further research

The framework has completed the development phase of a complex intervention, and the phases that follow, from feasibility to implementation, do not have to come in strict order<sup>98</sup>. The shortest next step is the demonstration activity of the DSRM, which needs no participants. Running the full set through a simulated or retrospective case series, from feasibility to the lessons record, shows whether the instruments connect as the design intends and whether any record lacks a reader.

A content review by sites and monitors, with surgeons included, and a usability test of the templates should come next, because an instrument that nobody can complete correctly cannot be evaluated for anything else. A feasibility study in one nonhospital organization and one hospital can then run the minimum set through a complete investigation. It should measure staff time per instrument, separating the work done once from the work that grows with cases, and compare the completeness of the records with a defined checklist. The share of deviations that the site detected before the monitor did shows most directly whether the framework does what it claims.

Before-and-after comparisons at one site are likely to report an improvement whatever the framework does. In surgical oncology, 86 of 97 non-randomized studies of quality improvement reported a positive effect, against 2 of 12 randomized trials<sup>34</sup>, so the evaluation of effect needs a concurrent comparison. A sponsor could introduce the framework in its sites in a staggered order within one investigation, letting the sites that have not yet started serve as comparators. Where that is not possible, a site could follow its indicators over a long series before and after adoption, as the IDEAL recommendations propose when randomized designs are not feasible. A design led by a sponsor of this kind also tests whether a sponsor can supply templates while the site keeps the operation and ownership of its records.

Indicator validation needs another design, because an investigation spread across several centers leaves each center with few cases over several years. It can instead draw on existing records, since a retrospective study can pair historical values of the indicators with the events that followed them, and pooling the records of several centers of one sponsor provides the volume that one site cannot. Query backlog and reporting timeliness can be recovered from most trial databases, while operative time by complexity level is available only where the site recorded the level.

Mapping each instrument to 21 CFR Part 812 would test the transfer to the regime under which most of the nonhospital organizations in the registry operate, again without a new investigation.

# 6. Conclusion

This thesis advances a site-centered approach to quality management in shoulder medical device clinical investigations. Its central proposition is that the practical application of Quality by Design (QbD) requires an explicit connection between the quality objectives established for an investigation and the processes through which individual sites deliver them. The framework developed here gives that connection an operational structure, enabling the site to identify risks within its own activities, assign responsibility for their control, and use the experience of conduct to inform subsequent decisions.

The analysis identified an asymmetry in the material reviewed: most of the attention has gone to how sponsors design, oversee, and assess investigations, while less operational guidance supports how sites organize and evaluate their own contribution to quality. Sponsor oversight remains essential, but its effectiveness also depends on what happens locally. It cannot continuously observe every clinical decision, anticipate every weakness in a local workflow, or reconstruct information that was never adequately captured. These limits support a more explicit role for the site in applying risk assessment and protecting critical-to-quality (CtQ) factors. The domains proposed in this thesis provide a structure for that assessment, through which each site can consider how its own conduct affects the factors that matter to a particular investigation.

The contribution of the work lies in bringing established quality-management principles into a coherent structure that the site can operate and retain across investigations. The framework connects local observations to review, decisions, and subsequent verification, giving practical form to responsibilities that are otherwise distributed across different requirements and records. In surgical device investigations, this also creates a place for examining operator-dependent risks during conduct, when performance may continue to change despite qualification before the first case. By making these relationships explicit and tracing design choices to their underlying rationale, the thesis provides a concrete proposal that others can examine, adapt, and evaluate.

This approach may anticipate a future in which investigational sites are expected to demonstrate more explicitly how they manage the quality of their own processes. As QbD and risk-proportionate quality management develop, greater attention to the organizational conditions under which sites apply these principles would be a plausible extension. Such a development would require practical support and recognition of the resources involved, especially in centers where clinical care and research depend on the same staff. Whether site-level quality arrangements should eventually become formal requirements requires wider discussion and empirical evidence. The present framework contributes to that discussion by showing how a proactive site role could be structured alongside sponsor oversight.

The contribution remains at the level of a theoretically grounded design. The framework has not been implemented in an investigational site, and its development lacked systematic observation of daily workflows and formal consultation with intended users. These limitations leave important questions about feasibility, workload, and the usefulness of individual controls unresolved. Closer engagement with site teams could reveal demands that were underestimated or simpler ways of performing the proposed functions. Establishing a traceable design nevertheless provides a necessary basis for this work: its assumptions are visible, its intended mechanisms can be examined, and its components can be revised against experience.

Adaptability is therefore central to the proposed instruments. Their open, adaptable format is intended to accommodate different staffing arrangements, existing records, and investigation-specific risks. Practical evaluation should determine which elements deserve to be retained, simplified, combined, or removed, while preserving the quality functions they serve. Additional detail should be introduced where it improves a decision or addresses a demonstrated need. The framework can consequently develop through successive iterations, with its scope and demands adjusted to what sites can sustain.

Further development could also extend its analytical and technological capabilities. Data collected during implementation could support the refinement of indicators, the calibration of review thresholds, and statistical analysis where the number and comparability of observations permit it. Digital implementations could facilitate data capture, link existing records, automate routine calculations, and support the exchange of relevant information with sponsors. These developments should follow an understanding of how the instruments function in practice, so that technology supports workable processes and reduces avoidable effort. The current design offers a basis from which such capabilities can be developed progressively.

Although the framework was developed for shoulder device investigations, its broader ambition is to contribute to clinical research in which sites are supported as active participants in quality management. Its relevance to other specialties and settings remains to be established through adaptation and evaluation. The underlying proposition has wider significance: efforts to improve the quality of clinical investigations should include the capacity of the organizations where participants receive care and evidence is generated. By giving that proposition a theoretical foundation and an explicit operational form, this thesis establishes a starting point for developing site-centered quality systems through practice, with the aim of strengthening participant protection and the reliability of clinical evidence.
